01

Levels of Protein Structure

4 levels exist. Primary → Secondary → Tertiary → Quaternary. Each higher level depends on the one below it.
1°
Primary Structure
Peptide Bond (Covalent)
Linear sequence of amino acids. Determined by the gene. Sanger's method identifies it. Example: Insulin (51 AA)
2°
Secondary Structure
H-bonds (backbone NH···CO)
Regular repeating patterns: α-helix, β-pleated sheet, β-turns. Stabilised by hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms.
3°
Tertiary Structure
Hydrophobic + H-bond + IonicBond + Disulphide
3D folding of a single polypeptide. Hydrophobic core. Disulphide bonds (covalent). Determines biological function.
4°
Quaternary Structure
Non-covalent + some S-S
Assembly of 2+ polypeptide subunits. Example: Haemoglobin (α₂β₂). Not all proteins have this level.
Mnemonic — Bonds by Level
"Pretty Smart Tertiary Queens" → Peptide, Stable H-bonds, Three types (hydrophobic+H+ionic+S-S), Quaternary = all of the above

Or simpler: 1° Peptide → 2° Hydrogen → 3° Multiple (HISD: Hydrophobic, Ionic, S-S, Disulphide) → 4° Same as 3° but between chains

Clinical Correlation — Why Primary Structure Matters

A single amino acid substitution in primary structure → disease. In Sickle Cell Anaemia, Glu→Val at position 6 of β-globin chain creates HbS. This demonstrates that primary structure determines function — a core biochemistry principle.

H-bond Non-covalent 2°, 3° Urea, heat

α-Helix & β-Pleated Sheet

α-Helix ⭐ Examiner Fav
  • Right-handed helix (most proteins)
  • 3.6 amino acids per turn
  • Rise per residue: 1.5 Å
  • Pitch (rise per turn): 5.4 Å
  • H-bonds: between NH of residue n and C=O of residue n-4
  • H-bonds run parallel to helix axis
  • R-groups point outward from helix
  • Disrupted by: Proline (no NH), Glycine (too flexible), charged R-groups
Example Proteins

Keratin (hair, nails): nearly entirely α-helical, coiled-coil structure

Myoglobin: 8 α-helical segments (A–H)

β-Pleated Sheet PYQ 2017, 2018
  • Extended, zigzag conformation
  • Two types: Parallel and Antiparallel
  • H-bonds run perpendicular to chain direction
  • Antiparallel: stronger H-bonds (more stable)
  • R-groups alternate: above and below sheet plane
  • Rise per residue: 3.5 Å

04

O₂ Dissociation Curve

Oxygen Dissociation Curves — Hb vs Mb vs HbF
Right Shift → ↓ O₂ Affinity (O₂ unloading)
  • ↑ pCO₂ (exercise, tissues)
  • ↑ H⁺ (acidosis) — Bohr effect
  • ↑ Temperature
  • ↑ 2,3-BPG
Right shift = MORE O₂ delivered to tissues. Good in exercise, fever.
Left Shift → ↑ O₂ Affinity (O₂ hoarding)
  • ↓ pCO₂ (hyperventilation)
  • ↓ H⁺ (alkalosis)
  • ↓ Temperature
  • ↓ 2,3-BPG (stored blood)
  • HbF (foetal Hb)
  • CO poisoning
Left shift = Hb grabs O₂ but won't release it. Dangerous in CO poisoning.

2,3-BPG & Bohr Effect

2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) ⭐ PYQ Every Year
Rapoport-Luebering Cycle (How 2,3-BPG is Made)
1,3-BPG
(Glycolysis intermediate)
→
Bisphosphoglycerate mutase 2,3-BPG
Only in RBCs (no mitochondria)
→
3-PG
Continues glycolysis

Mechanism of Action:

  • 2,3-BPG binds the central cavity of Hb between β-chains
  • Only binds T-state (deoxyHb)
  • Stabilises T-state → ↓ O₂ affinity → right shift
  • 1 molecule of 2,3-BPG per 1 Hb molecule

Clinical Significance:

  • High altitude: ↑ 2,3-BPG → ↑ O₂ delivery to tissues
  • Stored blood: 2,3-BPG depletes → ↑ O₂ affinity → poor O₂ delivery (transfusion problem)
  • Anaemia: ↑ 2,3-BPG (compensatory)
  • HbF: doesn't bind 2,3-BPG (γ-chains) → ↑ affinity → foetal O₂ extraction from maternal Hb
Bohr Effect PYQ 2013, Multiple ⭐
Definition: The decrease in O₂ affinity of Hb caused by ↑ CO₂ and/or ↑ H⁺. Facilitates O₂ delivery to metabolically active tissues.
Bohr Effect Mechanism
↑ CO₂ in tissues → CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (by carbonic anhydrase)
↓
H⁺ binds to Histidine residues on Hb (β-146 His, α-122 His) → forms salt bridges → stabilises T-state
↓
↓ O₂ affinity → O₂ released to tissues
↓
In lungs: ↓ CO₂ → salt bridges break → R-state → O₂ loads onto Hb again
Key Concept
Bohr = "H goes in → O₂ goes out" (tissues); "O₂ goes in → H goes out" (lungs)

Why HbF is Protective in Sickle Cell

HbF (α₂γ₂) does NOT polymerise with HbS. High HbF levels (as in neonates, or with hydroxyurea treatment) inhibit sickling by diluting HbS polymer formation. This is why symptoms appear only after HbF switches to HbA (~6 months of age).

EQ alert: "Patient with HbS often suffers from anaemia" — answer: haemolytic due to sickling + membrane damage. Sickled cells are rigid and get destroyed in spleen.
EQ Template — HbF has more O₂ affinity than HbA
"HbF has more affinity towards oxygen than HbA." [2019-S]
  1. Fact: HbF has P₅₀ ~20 mmHg vs HbA P₅₀ ~26 mmHg → lower P₅₀ = higher O₂ affinity
  2. Structural Basis: HbF has γ-chains instead of β-chains. γ-chains have Serine at position 143 instead of Histidine (β-143 His)
  3. 2,3-BPG Interaction: 2,3-BPG binds poorly to γ-chains (Ser 143 creates steric hindrance). Less 2,3-BPG effect → HbF stays in R-state more → ↑ O₂ affinity
  4. Physiological Significance: Foetus extracts O₂ from maternal HbA across the placenta. HbF's higher affinity ensures O₂ transfer from maternal to foetal blood
  5. Clinical: After birth, γ→β switch occurs; HbF → HbA by 6 months. Persistence of HbF (HPFH) is actually protective in sickle cell and β-thalassaemia

Neonatal/Physiological Jaundice PYQ 2011, 2017, 2021

Why it Occurs:

  • HbF → HbA switch: massive RBC turnover
  • Immature UGT1A1 in neonatal liver → poor conjugation
  • Increased enterohepatic circulation (intestinal bacteria absent)

Phototherapy Mechanism:

  • Blue light (460 nm) → converts bilirubin (4Z,15Z) → photoisomers (4Z,15E lumirubin)
  • Photoisomers are water-soluble → can be excreted in bile/urine WITHOUT conjugation
  • Works without needing UGT1A1 → ideal for neonates
  • Preferred over phenobarbitone in Crigler-Najjar Type I (no UGT at all)

EQ Template — Hypoalbuminaemia leads to peripheral oedema [2025]
"Hypoalbuminaemia leads to peripheral oedema."
  1. Normal role of albumin: Albumin (3.5–5 g/dL) is the principal contributor to plasma oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure (~25 mmHg)
  2. Oncotic pressure function: Draws water BACK into capillaries from interstitial space (opposing hydrostatic pressure that pushes fluid out)
  3. Hypoalbuminaemia: ↓ plasma oncotic pressure → hydrostatic pressure > oncotic pressure → net fluid filtration into interstitium
  4. Result: Peripheral oedema (pitting), ascites, pulmonary oedema, periorbital oedema
  5. Causes: Liver disease (↓ synthesis), Nephrotic syndrome (↑ loss in urine), Malnutrition (kwashiorkor), Malabsorption
  6. Example: Nephrotic syndrome — serum albumin <2.5 g/dL → massive generalised oedema (anasarca)

Viva & Exam Q&A

Protein Structure Vivas
What bonds stabilise quaternary structure of proteins?GROUP A

Quaternary structure is stabilised by the same non-covalent forces that stabilise tertiary structure between adjacent polypeptide chains:

  • Hydrophobic interactions (predominant at subunit interfaces)
  • Hydrogen bonds between chains
  • Ionic/electrostatic interactions (salt bridges)
  • Disulphide bonds (covalent, e.g., between chains of IgG)

Key example: Haemoglobin (α₂β₂) — subunits held mainly by hydrophobic contacts and 8 ion pairs (salt bridges) in T-state.

Why can proline not be part of an α-helix?2014

Proline has a cyclic pyrrolidine ring that:

  • Has no NH group in its peptide bond → cannot donate H-bond → breaks helix H-bonding pattern
  • Introduces a rigid kink/bend (restricted rotation around N-Cα bond)
  • Creates steric clash with adjacent residues

Proline is called a "helix breaker." It frequently occurs at bends and turns. Collagen is unusual — it forms a triple helix ONLY because of its Gly-Pro-X repeat.

Define denaturation. Which bonds are broken?Core Concept

Denaturation = Disruption of the native 3D structure of a protein without breaking peptide bonds (1° structure intact).

Bonds broken: H-bonds, hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds, sometimes disulphide bonds.

Result: Loss of biological activity, altered solubility, viscosity changes.

Agents: Heat, urea/guanidinium (H-bonds), detergents (hydrophobic), acid/alkali (ionic), reducing agents (S-S bonds, e.g., β-mercaptoethanol).

Renaturation is possible if denaturing agent is removed and 1° structure is intact (e.g., ribonuclease A experiment by Anfinsen).

Haemoglobin Vivas
Why is the O₂ dissociation curve of Hb sigmoidal while Mb is hyperbolic?PYQ 2014, 2016, 2023

Myoglobin: monomer with 1 haem. No cooperativity. Each molecule binds O₂ independently → simple hyperbolic curve. Very high affinity at all pO₂.

Haemoglobin: tetramer (α₂β₂). Exhibits positive cooperativity:

  • First O₂ binds with LOW affinity (T-state)
  • Triggers conformational change → R-state → second O₂ binds more easily
  • Each successive binding is easier → sigmoidal (S-shaped) curve

Physiological significance: Sigmoid shape means Hb is very efficient — loads O₂ fully in lungs (high pO₂), unloads a large fraction in tissues (low pO₂). Mb's hyperbolic curve means it stays saturated throughout → ideal for O₂ storage, not transport.

What is Haemoglobin A1c? Why is it important in diabetes?PYQ 2011, 2018, 2016

HbA1c (Glycated Haemoglobin): Hb with glucose non-enzymatically (spontaneously) attached to the N-terminal valine of both β-chains via Schiff base → rearranges to stable ketoamine (Amadori product).

Why useful in diabetes:

  • RBCs live ~120 days → HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over 8–12 weeks
  • Unlike fasting glucose (snapshot), HbA1c = cumulative glycaemic exposure
  • Normal: <5.7%; Pre-diabetes: 5.7–6.4%; DM: ≥6.5%
  • Target in treated DM: <7% (to prevent microvascular complications)

Not affected by short-term glucose fluctuations or time of day. Limitations: haemolytic anaemia (falsely low), sickle cell trait may affect accuracy.

What is methaemoglobinaemia? How does it occur?Core

Methaemoglobin (MetHb): Hb in which iron is oxidised from Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺. Fe³⁺ cannot reversibly bind O₂.

Causes: Oxidising drugs/toxins (dapsone, nitrites, sulphonamides, prilocaine), Congenital HbM (proximal/distal His mutations), NADH-metHb reductase deficiency.

Effect: ↓ functional Hb + left shift of remaining Hb (cannot release O₂) → tissue hypoxia.

Clinical: Cyanosis with chocolate brown blood. SpO₂ falsely reads ~85% by pulse oximetry.

Treatment: Methylene blue (activates NADPH-metHb reductase via G6PD) OR Vitamin C (non-enzymatic reduction).

Bilirubin & Plasma Protein Vivas
Why does conjugated bilirubin appear in urine but unconjugated does not?PYQ 2019

Unconjugated bilirubin:

  • Lipid-soluble, tightly bound to albumin in blood
  • Too large as albumin complex → NOT filtered by glomerulus
  • Even if some is free, it binds to tubular cells (not excreted)
  • Therefore: absent in urine even when serum levels high (haemolytic jaundice — "acholouric jaundice")

Conjugated (direct) bilirubin:

  • Water-soluble (2 glucuronic acids attached)
  • NOT bound to albumin → freely filterable at glomerulus
  • When serum levels rise → appears in urine (bilirubinuria)
  • Seen in: hepatocellular jaundice, obstructive jaundice
What are chaperones? What is their role in protein folding?PYQ 2016, 2018

Chaperones are proteins that assist in the correct folding of newly synthesised polypeptides. They do NOT contribute to the final structure (not part of the final protein).

Mechanism:

  • Bind to exposed hydrophobic patches on unfolded/partially folded proteins
  • Prevent premature or incorrect interactions (misfolding, aggregation)
  • Provide a protected environment for folding (e.g., GroEL/GroES barrel in bacteria, HSP60 in mitochondria)
  • ATP-dependent (most chaperones use ATP hydrolysis)

Types: Hsp70 (cytosol, early folding), Hsp90 (steroid receptor stabilisation), Hsp60 (GroEL — barrel type), Calnexin/Calreticulin (ER, glycoprotein folding).

Misfolding despite chaperones → ubiquitin-proteasome degradation of aberrant proteins. Accumulation of misfolded proteins → Prion diseases, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's.

What are Bence Jones proteins? Significance?Myeloma

Bence Jones proteins: Free immunoglobulin light chains (κ or λ) produced in excess by myeloma plasma cells and excreted in urine.

Detection: Heat precipitation at 40–60°C, redissolve at 100°C (classic test). Modern: urine protein electrophoresis or immunofixation.

Significance: Diagnostic of multiple myeloma, Waldenström macroglobulinaemia, or light chain disease. Cause renal tubular damage (cast nephropathy = "myeloma kidney").

Essay Question Templates

EQ — 2,3-BPG helps in delivery of oxygen to tissues [PYQ 2011]
"2,3-BPG helps in delivery of oxygen to the tissues."
  1. Source: Made from 1,3-BPG in RBCs via Rapoport-Luebering cycle (bisphosphoglycerate mutase). Concentration in RBCs ~4–5 mmol/L (equimolar with Hb)
  2. Binding Site: Binds the central cavity of deoxyHb between the β-chains (involves Lys82, His143, His2, Val1 of each β-chain). One molecule per Hb tetramer
  3. Mechanism: 2,3-BPG stabilises the T-state (deoxyHb) by cross-linking the β-chains with multiple ionic bonds → ↓ O₂ affinity → right shift of ODC
  4. Net Effect: At tissue level (low pO₂), Hb releases MORE O₂ than it would without 2,3-BPG → better O₂ unloading
  5. Regulatory contexts: ↑ at high altitude (compensatory), in anaemia, in hypoxia. ↓ in stored blood (2,3-DPG depletes → less O₂ delivery → risk in massive transfusion)
  6. HbF comparison: HbF (γ-chains) binds 2,3-BPG poorly → higher O₂ affinity → ideal for transplacental O₂ transfer
EQ — Patient with HbS often suffers from anaemia [PYQ 2013, 2017]
"Patient with Hb-S often suffers from anaemia."
  1. Molecular defect: GAG→GTG mutation in β-globin gene, codon 6. Glu (polar) → Val (non-polar). Creates "sticky patch" on deoxyHbS surface
  2. Polymerisation: In low-O₂ conditions, deoxyHbS fibres polymerise → RBCs become sickle-shaped, rigid, non-deformable
  3. Haemolysis: Sickled RBCs are rigid → cannot pass through spleen → destroyed by splenic macrophages (extravascular haemolysis). Also intravascular haemolysis due to membrane damage. RBC survival ↓ from 120 days → 20–25 days
  4. Vaso-occlusion: Sickled cells block microvasculature → ischaemic pain crises, organ infarction (spleen, kidney, brain, bones)
  5. Type of anaemia: Normocytic normochromic haemolytic anaemia with compensatory reticulocytosis
  6. Complications: Splenomegaly (early), autosplenectomy (late), aplastic crisis (parvovirus B19), susceptibility to infections (encapsulated organisms)
EQ — Myoglobin does not exhibit Bohr effect [PYQ 2015]
"Myoglobin does not exhibit Bohr effect."
  1. Bohr effect definition: The decrease in O₂ affinity of Hb when pH drops (↑ H⁺) or pCO₂ rises, mediated via salt-bridge formation in T-state Hb
  2. Structural basis of Bohr in Hb: H⁺ protonates histidine residues (β-146 His, α-122 His) → salt bridges form → stabilises T-state → ↓ O₂ affinity
  3. Why Mb shows no Bohr effect: Myoglobin is a monomer — has no T-state/R-state equilibrium, no cooperative binding, no allosteric subunit communication. The key histidine residues involved in Bohr salt-bridges in Hb are not involved in the same way in monomeric Mb
  4. Physiological implication: Mb's function is O₂ STORAGE in muscle. It should NOT release O₂ until pO₂ is very low. Hence, being unresponsive to pH/CO₂ changes is appropriate
  5. Conclusion: Bohr effect is exclusively an allosteric property of oligomeric haemoglobin, made possible by subunit cooperativity — absent in the monomer myoglobin
