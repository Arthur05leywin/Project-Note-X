Acid-Base Balance

Normal blood pH = 7.35–7.45. Maintained by three systems: (1) Buffer systems (immediate, seconds), (2) Respiratory compensation (minutes–hours), (3) Renal compensation (hours–days, most powerful).
Normal Arterial Blood Gas (ABG) Values PYQ 2014-S, 2018-S, 2023
pH7.35–7.45
pCO₂35–45 mmHg
pO₂80–100 mmHg
HCO₃⁻22–26 mEq/L
Base Excess±2 mEq/L
O₂ Saturation95–100 %
Acid-Base Regulatory Systems — Hierarchy
IMMEDIATE (seconds) — Chemical Buffers HCO₃⁻/H₂CO₃ · Phosphate (HPO₄²⁻/H₂PO₄⁻) · Protein/Haemoglobin · Ammonia buffer in kidney First line of defence — do NOT eliminate acid, just neutralise it temporarily
↓ If insufficient → Respiratory system kicks in
FAST (minutes to hours) — Respiratory Compensation CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (catalysed by carbonic anhydrase)
Acidosis → ↑RR → blow off CO₂ → pH rises
Alkalosis → ↓RR → retain CO₂ → pH falls
Lungs eliminate CO₂ (volatile acid). Fast but not complete correction.
↓ If persistent → Renal system — most powerful but slowest
SLOW but COMPLETE (hours to days) — Renal Compensation Acidosis: Kidneys ↑ H⁺ excretion (as NH₄⁺ and titratable acid) + ↑ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption
Alkalosis: Kidneys ↓ H⁺ excretion + ↓ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption + ↑ HCO₃⁻ excretion
Only system that can completely restore normal pH. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide) block renal H⁺ secretion.
Renal Mechanisms for H⁺ Regulation PYQ 2019-S, 2025

H⁺ Secretion (Acidosis — increase all three):

  • Titratable acidity: H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ → H₂PO₄⁻ (phosphate buffer) — excreted in urine
  • Ammonium excretion: Glutamine → Glutamate + NH₃ (in tubular cells) → NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ (excreted in urine). Most important during chronic acidosis — can increase 10-fold
  • HCO₃⁻ reabsorption: All filtered HCO₃⁻ reabsorbed (mainly proximal tubule) — mediated by carbonic anhydrase

Key Renal Facts for Exam:

  • Proximal tubule: reabsorbs 80–85% of filtered HCO₃⁻
  • Distal tubule + collecting duct: fine-tune H⁺ secretion, NH₄⁺ production
  • Urine pH can range from 4.5 to 8.5 (wide range = renal power)
  • Aldosterone → ↑ H⁺ secretion in collecting duct (explains metabolic alkalosis in hyperaldosteronism)
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide) → ↓ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption → metabolic acidosis

Blood Buffers & Henderson-Hasselbalch

Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation ⭐ PYQ 2013-S, 2019-S, 2021

pH = pKa + log [A⁻] / [HA]

For bicarbonate buffer:

pH = 6.1 + log [HCO₃⁻] / [H₂CO₃]

Normal ratio = 20:1 → pH = 6.1 + log 20 = 6.1 + 1.3 = 7.4

The ratio HCO₃⁻ : H₂CO₃ = 20:1 must be maintained for pH 7.4. Lungs control H₂CO₃ (via CO₂); kidneys control HCO₃⁻. Change either → pH shifts.

Mnemonic — Blood Buffers in Order
"Big Healthy People Play" = Bicarbonate, Haemoglobin, Protein, Phosphate
Histidine's Role in Buffering PYQ 2024

Histidine residues in haemoglobin have imidazole side chains with pKa ~6.0 — ideal for physiological buffering. The β-93 histidine is key. In deoxyHb, the pKa of histidine shifts to ~8.0 → stronger buffer → Bohr effect (H⁺ promotes O₂ release). Histidine is the only amino acid that buffers at physiological pH.

⚕️ Clinical Case — Persistent Vomiting (ABG Interpretation) ⭐ PYQ 2023
Case: 50-year-old male, persistent vomiting, dehydrated, shallow breathing

Lab Results: pH 7.72 · HCO₃⁻ 45 mmol/L · pCO₂ 60 mmHg · Na⁺ 140 mEq/L · K⁺ 2.5 mEq/L · Urine: Acidic

ABG Interpretation Approach
Step 1: pH? pH 7.72 → Alkalosis (normal 7.35–7.45)
↓ Identify primary disturbance
Step 2: Primary cause? HCO₃⁻ 45 mEq/L (high) → Primary Metabolic Alkalosis
Cause: Vomiting → loss of HCl from stomach → HCO₃⁻ accumulates
↓ Check compensation
Step 3: Compensation? pCO₂ 60 mmHg (expected ↑ to retain CO₂) — respiratory compensation: ↓RR → shallow breathing → CO₂ retention → helps bring pH back. Appropriate respiratory compensation.
↓ Explain secondary findings
Step 4: Hypokalemia + Acidic urine — Paradoxical? Why hypokalemia? Vomiting → volume depletion → Aldosterone ↑ → Na⁺ reabsorption + K⁺ and H⁺ excretion → K⁺ loss
Why acidic urine in alkalosis? K⁺ depleted cells exchange K⁺ for H⁺ → H⁺ moves intracellularly + K⁺ exits → remaining H⁺ in distal tubule excreted in urine → paradoxically acidic urine despite systemic alkalosis (called "paradoxical aciduria")
ABG Quick Rule
"ROME" = Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal

Respiratory acidosis → pH↓ and pCO₂↑ (opposite directions). Metabolic acidosis → pH↓ and HCO₃⁻↓ (same/equal direction). Applies for alkalosis too.

Anion Gap

Anion Gap — Definition & Significance ⭐ PYQ 2014-S, 2018-S

Anion Gap = [Na⁺] − ([Cl⁻] + [HCO₃⁻])

Normal = 8–16 mEq/L (average 12 mEq/L)

Represents unmeasured anions: albumin (main), phosphate, sulphate, organic acids

TypeAnion GapMechanismCauses
High Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis (HAGMA)↑ (>16 mEq/L)Accumulation of unmeasured anions (organic acids, etc.) while HCO₃⁻ fallsMUDPILES: Methanol, Uraemia, DKA, Paraldehyde, Isoniazid/Iron, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates
Normal Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis (NAGMA / Hyperchloraemic acidosis)Normal (8–16)HCO₃⁻ lost (replaced by Cl⁻ to maintain electroneutrality) → Cl⁻ risesDiarrhoea (GI HCO₃⁻ loss), RTA (Renal Tubular Acidosis), acetazolamide, ureterosigmoidostomy, saline infusion
HAGMA Mnemonic
"MUDPILES" = Methanol · Uraemia · DKA · Paraldehyde · Isoniazid/Iron · Lactic acidosis · Ethylene glycol · Salicylates
Hypoalbuminaemia reduces the anion gap by ~2.5 mEq/L for every 1 g/dL fall in albumin below 4 g/dL. Correct for albumin before interpreting anion gap!

Jaundice & Bilirubin

Jaundice = yellowish discoloration of skin, sclerae, and mucous membranes when serum bilirubin >2 mg/dL. Normal serum bilirubin = 0.2–1.2 mg/dL. Clinical jaundice visible at >2 mg/dL.
Bilirubin Metabolism — Complete Pathway
Step 1 — Haem Catabolism (Spleen/RES) Senescent RBCs → Haemoglobin → Haem + Globin
Haem → Haem oxygenase → Biliverdin (green) + CO + Fe²⁺
Biliverdin → Biliverdin reductase → Bilirubin (yellow, water-insoluble = indirect/unconjugated)
↓ Bound to albumin — transported in blood as indirect bilirubin
Step 2 — Hepatic Uptake Indirect bilirubin (albumin-bound) → hepatocyte
Dissociates from albumin → binds ligandin (Y protein) + Z protein inside hepatocyte
↓ Conjugation in ER
Step 3 — Conjugation Bilirubin + 2× UDP-glucuronic acid → Bilirubin diglucuronide (direct/conjugated bilirubin)
Enzyme: UDP-glucuronyl transferase (UGT1A1) — absent in Crigler-Najjar; reduced in Gilbert's
Conjugated bilirubin = water-soluble → can be excreted in bile
↓ Active transport into bile canaliculi (MRP2 transporter) — rate-limiting step
Step 4 — Intestinal Fate Bilirubin diglucuronide → intestinal bacteria → deconjugation → Urobilinogen
Urobilinogen: (a) 10–20% absorbed → portal blood → liver (enterohepatic circulation) or kidney → urine (gives urine yellow colour)
(b) Remainder → further reduced → Stercobilin → faeces (brown colour)

Gilbert's Syndrome

Mildly reduced UGT1A1 activity (~30% of normal) → mild unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, especially after fasting or stress. Benign, no liver disease. Serum bilirubin 1.2–3 mg/dL. Fasting test: ↑ bilirubin after 48-hour fast.

Neonatal Physiological Jaundice + Phototherapy PYQ 2011, 2017-S

UGT1A1 immature at birth → unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia → indirect bili (lipid-soluble) crosses BBB → kernicterus. Phototherapy: blue light (420–480 nm) converts insoluble bilirubin → lumirubin/photobilirubin (water-soluble, polar isomers) → excreted without conjugation in bile and urine.

Why Creatinine is More Specific than Urea ⭐ PYQ 2022

Creatinine:

  • Produced at constant rate from creatine phosphate in muscle
  • Freely filtered at glomerulus
  • Minimal tubular secretion (slightly overestimates GFR)
  • NOT affected by protein diet, hydration, or GI bleeding
  • More specific for renal function

Urea (BUN) is affected by:

  • High-protein diet → ↑ urea (pre-renal false positive)
  • GI bleeding → protein absorbed → ↑ urea
  • Dehydration → ↑ urea
  • Liver failure → ↓ urea (decreased synthesis)
  • Tubular reabsorption of urea increases with dehydration
Uremia & Ammonia Toxicity — PYQ 2024

Chronic renal failure → urea accumulates → gut bacteria convert urea → NH₃ → absorbed → exceeds liver detoxification → hyperammonaemia → inhibits α-KG, glutamate, ATP in brain → encephalopathy (asterixis, confusion). Also: metabolic acidosis (cannot excrete H⁺ + loss of HCO₃⁻ reabsorption), anaemia (↓ erythropoietin), hyperphosphataemia + hypocalcaemia (↓ calcitriol), uraemic frost.

HbA1c — Glycated Haemoglobin ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2016, 2018

Mechanism:

  • Glucose attaches non-enzymatically to N-terminal valine of β-globin chains (Hb) → glycation (Amadori product)
  • Rate of glycation is proportional to ambient blood glucose over the lifespan of the RBC
  • RBC lifespan = 120 days → HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over 2–3 months
  • HbA1c rises 0.3% for every 30 mg/dL rise in mean plasma glucose

Clinical significance:

  • HbA1c <7% = well-controlled diabetes (target)
  • NOT affected by short-term glucose fluctuations
  • Falsely LOW in haemolytic anaemia (short RBC lifespan), haemorrhage, HbS trait
  • Falsely HIGH in iron deficiency anaemia (long RBC lifespan), splenectomy
  • Not useful in haemoglobinopathies (HbS, HbC) — use fructosamine instead
Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) PYQ 2011-S
OGTT Protocol
Preparation 3 days of high-carbohydrate diet (≥150 g/day) + overnight fast (8–14 hours) + no exercise, smoking, drugs
↓ 0 min: Fasting sample taken
Load 75 g anhydrous glucose in 250 mL water (adult) · 1.75 g/kg in children (max 75 g) · Drink within 5 minutes
↓ Blood samples at 30, 60, 90, 120 minutes
Interpretation Normal: 2-hr value <140 mg/dL · IGT (pre-DM): 140–199 · DM: ≥200 mg/dL

Free Radicals & ROS

A free radical = any species with one or more unpaired electrons in its outermost orbital → highly reactive, short-lived, chain-reaction propagating. Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) = oxygen-centred free radicals + reactive non-radical oxygen derivatives.

Free Radical Chain Reaction — Lipid Peroxidation
Initiation OH• + Polyunsaturated Fatty Acid (LH) → L• (lipid radical) + H₂O
↓ Propagation — chain reaction
Propagation L• + O₂ → LOO• (lipid peroxy radical)
LOO• + LH (new fatty acid) → LOOH (lipid hydroperoxide) + L•
Chain reaction continues — ONE radical can destroy many fatty acids
↓ Termination by antioxidants (Vit E)
Termination LOO• + Vitamin E → LOOH + Vit E• (tocopheroxyl radical, less reactive)
Vit E• + Vit C → Vit E regenerated + ascorbate radical
Chain reaction stops. TBARS (malondialdehyde) formed — used as marker of lipid peroxidation
ROS — Beneficial Effects PYQ 2015-S, 2021
  • Respiratory burst (oxidative burst): NADPH oxidase in neutrophils/macrophages → O₂•⁻ → HOCl (myeloperoxidase) → kills bacteria. CGD (Chronic Granulomatous Disease) = NADPH oxidase defect → recurrent infections
  • Cell signalling: H₂O₂ acts as second messenger in insulin signalling, NF-κB activation, growth factor receptor signalling
  • Thyroid hormone synthesis: H₂O₂ generated by DUOX (thyroid oxidase) is required for iodination of thyroglobulin
  • Nitric oxide: O₂•⁻-independent — vasodilation, neurotransmission, immune function
  • Sperm capacitation: ROS generated by sperm NADPH oxidase required for hyperactivation

Paracetamol (Acetaminophen) Overdose — GSH Connection PYQ Classic

Therapeutic dose: paracetamol → glucuronide/sulphate conjugates (Phase II). Overdose → CYP2E1 (Phase I) → NAPQI (toxic reactive metabolite). Normal: NAPQI + GSH → non-toxic mercapturic acid. Overdose: GSH depleted → NAPQI binds hepatocyte proteins → hepatocellular necrosis → fulminant hepatic failure. Treatment: N-Acetylcysteine (NAC) — replenishes GSH by providing cysteine → cures paracetamol hepatotoxicity if given early.

Xenobiotics & Cytochrome P450

Xenobiotic = any chemical foreign to the body (drugs, pollutants, food additives, carcinogens). Biotransformation = enzymatic conversion of xenobiotics to more polar, excretable metabolites. Occurs mainly in liver (SER). Also in intestine, lung, kidney, skin.
Phases of Xenobiotic Metabolism ⭐ PYQ 2016-S, 2023, 2025
Biotransformation — 2 Phases (Phase III sometimes listed)
PHASE I — Functionalisation (reveal or add reactive group) Reactions: Oxidation (most common), Reduction, Hydrolysis
Enzymes: Cytochrome P450 (CYP) monooxygenases (main), monoamine oxidase, alcohol dehydrogenase, esterases
Products: More polar, often still toxic. NAPQI from paracetamol = classic toxic Phase I product
Location: Smooth ER (microsomes) of hepatocytes
Makes drug more reactive — NOT always detoxification!
↓ Phase I product → Phase II conjugation
PHASE II — Conjugation (attach a polar group) Reactions: Glucuronidation (most common, uses UDPGA) · Sulphation · Acetylation · Methylation · Glutathione conjugation · Glycine conjugation
Products: Highly polar, non-toxic, excretable in urine or bile
Location: Cytosol + ER (glucuronidation in ER)
Usually detoxification. Some conjugates still active (morphine-6-glucuronide is more potent than morphine)
↓ (Optional Phase III — transport)
PHASE III — Elimination/Transport MDR transporters (P-glycoprotein), MRP2, OATP → excrete conjugates into bile or urine
Important for multi-drug resistance in cancer
Cytochrome P450 System ⭐ PYQ 2013, 2013-S, 2014-S, 2019, 2025

Structure:

  • Haemoprotein (iron-containing haem) — absorbs light at 450 nm when CO-bound (hence P450)
  • Located in smooth ER (microsomes)
  • Requires NADPH + O₂ (monooxygenase reaction)
  • Reaction: RH + O₂ + NADPH + H⁺ → ROH + H₂O + NADP⁺
  • Electron donors: NADPH-CYP reductase (FAD, FMN) + cytochrome b5

Important CYP isoforms:

  • CYP1A2: Caffeine, theophylline, warfarin
  • CYP2D6: Codeine, β-blockers, antidepressants (polymorphic — poor/extensive metabolisers)
  • CYP2E1: Ethanol, paracetamol (generates NAPQI), isoniazid
  • CYP3A4: Most abundant CYP (~50% of drug metabolism) — statins, cyclosporin, midazolam

CYP Inducers (↑ CYP activity → ↓ drug effect):

  • Rifampicin, Phenobarbitone, Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, Alcohol (chronic), St John's Wort
  • ↑ CYP → ↑ drug metabolism → ↓ therapeutic effect (enzyme induction)
  • Also → ↑ sex steroid metabolism (OCP failure with rifampicin)

CYP Inhibitors (↓ CYP activity → ↑ drug effect/toxicity):

  • Ketoconazole, Erythromycin, Cimetidine, Grapefruit juice (CYP3A4), Isoniazid, Fluconazole, Acute alcohol
  • ↓ CYP → ↓ drug metabolism → ↑ plasma levels → toxicity

Viva Q&A

Acid-Base Balance
Write the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. How does the kidney maintain acid-base balance?PYQ 2019-S

pH = pKa + log [A⁻]/[HA]

For bicarbonate buffer: pH = 6.1 + log [HCO₃⁻] / [H₂CO₃]

Normal ratio = 20:1 → pH 7.4. Kidneys maintain this by:

  • HCO₃⁻ reabsorption: Tubular cells secrete H⁺ into lumen → H⁺ + filtered HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂ → enters cell → recombines → HCO₃⁻ exits basolateral side into blood. Catalysed by carbonic anhydrase at both steps
  • Titratable acid excretion: H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ → H₂PO₄⁻ in tubular lumen → excreted. Contributes ~20–30 mEq H⁺/day
  • NH₄⁺ excretion: Glutamine → glutaminase → NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ → excreted. Increases dramatically (10×) in chronic acidosis — most adaptable system

Exam tip: Always state that only the kidney can COMPLETELY restore pH (not just buffer). Respiratory compensation is faster but incomplete.

What is anion gap? What is its biomedical importance?PYQ 2014-S, 2018-S

Anion gap = [Na⁺] − ([Cl⁻] + [HCO₃⁻]) = 8–16 mEq/L

Represents unmeasured anions (albumin, phosphate, sulphate, organic acids) that maintain electroneutrality.

Importance:

  • Differentiates causes of metabolic acidosis: High AG = accumulation of unmeasured anions (MUDPILES); Normal AG = HCO₃⁻ loss with Cl⁻ replacement (diarrhoea, RTA)
  • Guides treatment: High AG (DKA) → insulin + fluids; High AG (aspirin) → alkalinisation of urine
  • Prognosis: Very high AG (>30) suggests severe lactic acidosis → poor prognosis

Correct for albumin: every 1 g/dL drop in albumin below 4 → reduce normal AG by 2.5 mEq/L

Liver & Renal Function
Why does ALP increase in obstructive jaundice but not haemolytic jaundice?PYQ Classic Viva

ALP in obstructive jaundice:

  • ALP is normally membrane-bound in bile canaliculi of hepatocytes
  • Obstruction → bile salts accumulate in canaliculi → bile salts (detergents) solubilise membrane lipids → release membrane-bound ALP into blood
  • Also: bile salts induce increased ALP synthesis (transcription) in bile duct epithelium
  • Result: ALP rises markedly (3–10× normal)

Why NOT in haemolytic jaundice: No bile duct involvement, no bile salt accumulation → ALP not released. Liver's synthetic and excretory function intact.

Key: 5'-nucleotidase distinguishes liver ALP from bone ALP — rises only with liver origin

Why is serum creatinine more specific than plasma urea for renal function?PYQ 2022

Creatinine superiority:

  • Produced at constant rate from creatine phosphate in muscle (not affected by diet)
  • Freely filtered at glomerulus, minimal tubular secretion → accurate GFR estimate
  • Not reabsorbed by tubules

Urea limitations:

  • ↑ by: high protein diet, GI bleeding (protein absorbed), dehydration (↑ tubular reabsorption), catabolic states
  • ↓ by: liver failure (↓ synthesis), malnutrition, pregnancy
  • 20–40% reabsorbed in proximal tubule (passively) → influenced by flow rate

However: serum Cr rises LATE — 50% of nephron mass lost before Cr rises above normal. Creatinine clearance more sensitive early marker.

Free Radicals, Antioxidants & Xenobiotics
What is the role of Superoxide Dismutase? How does it protect cells?PYQ 2013

SOD reaction: 2O₂•⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂ (dismutation)

Protection mechanism:

  • O₂•⁻ itself moderately reactive but becomes dangerous by generating OH• via Haber-Weiss/Fenton reactions (Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + OH•)
  • SOD converts O₂•⁻ to H₂O₂ (less reactive, cannot generate OH• without Fe²⁺)
  • H₂O₂ then detoxified by catalase (peroxisomes) or GPx (cytoplasm)
  • Cu/Zn-SOD = cytoplasm; Mn-SOD = mitochondria (where most O₂•⁻ is generated)

Clinical link:

  • SOD1 (Cu/Zn-SOD) mutations → Familial ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis) — motor neuron disease
  • SOD3 deficiency → ↑ cardiovascular oxidative damage

SOD is the first line of enzymatic antioxidant defence — without it, O₂•⁻ accumulates → reacts with NO• → ONOO⁻ (peroxynitrite) → cell death

Explain Phase I and Phase II reactions of xenobiotic metabolism with examples.PYQ 2016-S, 2023

Phase I (Functionalisation):

  • Goal: Introduce or unmask a reactive functional group (-OH, -NH₂, -SH) → makes molecule more polar and reactive for Phase II
  • Main reaction: Oxidation by CYP450 monooxygenases (SER). Also: reduction, hydrolysis
  • NOT always detoxification — can activate procarcinogens (benzo[a]pyrene → epoxide → DNA damage)
  • Example: Paracetamol → CYP2E1 → NAPQI (toxic); Codeine → CYP2D6 → Morphine (active)

Phase II (Conjugation):

  • Goal: Conjugate with large polar molecule → high MW, very water-soluble → excrete in bile or urine
  • Reactions: Glucuronidation (most common), sulphation, acetylation, glutathione conjugation, glycine conjugation, methylation
  • Generally detoxifying — produces non-toxic, excretable products
  • Example: Morphine + UDPGA → morphine-6-glucuronide (paradoxically MORE potent); Bilirubin → bilirubin diglucuronide (water-soluble → bile)

Exam trap: Phase I is NOT always detoxification. Phase II is generally (but not always) detoxification.

What is glutathione? Describe its role in detoxification.PYQ 2014-S, 2016-S, 2022

Glutathione (GSH): Tripeptide — γ-Glutamyl-Cysteinyl-Glycine. The γ-bond (unusual) protects from non-specific proteolysis.

Roles in detoxification:

  • Direct radical scavenging: GSH + OH• → GS• + H₂O → GS• + GS• → GSSG (oxidised glutathione)
  • GPx substrate: H₂O₂ + 2GSH → 2H₂O + GSSG; ROOH + 2GSH → ROH + GSSG + H₂O
  • Phase II conjugation: GSH + electrophilic toxin (NAPQI, epoxides) → glutathione conjugate → cysteinyl conjugate → mercapturic acid → excreted. Protects DNA and proteins from reactive metabolites
  • RBC protection: ↓ GSH (G6PD deficiency → ↓ NADPH → ↓ GSH regeneration) → Heinz body formation → haemolytic anaemia
  • Leukotriene synthesis: GSH + arachidonic acid pathway → leukotrienes (LTC₄, LTD₄, LTE₄) — inflammatory mediators

N-Acetylcysteine (NAC) is the antidote for paracetamol overdose because it donates cysteine → replenishes GSH → binds NAPQI before it destroys hepatocytes

Diabetes & Jaundice
Why is phototherapy used to cure neonatal/physiological jaundice?PYQ 2011, 2017-S

Background: Neonates have immature UGT1A1 → cannot conjugate bilirubin adequately → unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin accumulates. At high levels → crosses BBB (lipid-soluble) → deposits in basal ganglia → kernicterus (irreversible brain damage).

Why phototherapy works:

  • Blue-green light (420–490 nm; peak efficacy 460 nm) photoexcites unconjugated bilirubin in skin capillaries
  • Two photochemical reactions: (a) Photoisomerisation → 4Z,15E-bilirubin (configurational isomer) → more polar, excreted in bile WITHOUT conjugation; (b) Photooxidation → lumirubin (structural isomer) → water-soluble, excreted in urine and bile
  • Both products water-soluble → can be excreted without conjugation → safe (cannot cross BBB)

Key point: No liver function required for phototherapy efficacy — therefore works even in UGT-deficient neonates (Crigler-Najjar Type II). Blue light specifically (not white) — hence blue lights in neonatal wards.

How does HbA1c provide information for management of diabetes mellitus?PYQ 2016

Mechanism: Glucose non-enzymatically glycates N-terminal valine of β-globin → HbA1c (Amadori rearrangement). Rate ∝ ambient blood glucose × time.

Why it's useful:

  • RBC lifespan = 120 days → HbA1c reflects mean blood glucose over 2–3 months → not affected by today's meal, stress, or glucose fluctuation
  • Fasting blood glucose reflects only the past 8 hours; HbA1c gives the long-term picture
  • Target HbA1c <7% → associated with ↓ microvascular complications (DCCT, UKPDS trials)
  • Every 1% rise in HbA1c → ↑ risk of MI by 14%, retinopathy by 37%

Limitations: Falsely low in haemolytic anaemia, sickle cell disease (short RBC lifespan). Falsely high in iron deficiency anaemia. Not reliable in haemoglobinopathies → use fructosamine instead.

Essay Question Templates

EQ — Acid-Base Balance & Anion Gap [PYQ 2014-S, 2018-S]
"Describe how pH of blood is regulated by lung and kidney. Give an account of metabolic acidosis and anion gap."
  1. Normal blood pH: 7.35–7.45. Maintained by three systems acting in sequence — buffers (seconds), respiratory (minutes), renal (hours–days).
  2. Respiratory regulation: Carotid/medullary chemoreceptors sense ↑ H⁺ (or ↑ CO₂) → stimulate respiratory centre → ↑ RR → more CO₂ expelled → [H₂CO₃] falls → pH rises. The converse in alkalosis. Lungs maintain the CO₂ side of the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation.
  3. Renal regulation: Kidneys maintain HCO₃⁻ concentration. Mechanisms: (i) HCO₃⁻ reabsorption (proximal tubule, carbonic anhydrase-dependent); (ii) Titratable acid excretion (H⁺ + phosphate → H₂PO₄⁻); (iii) Ammonium excretion (glutamine → NH₃ → NH₄⁺) — increases 10-fold in chronic acidosis. Only mechanism for COMPLETE pH correction.
  4. Metabolic Acidosis: Primary ↓ HCO₃⁻ → pH falls → respiratory compensation (Kussmaul breathing, ↓ pCO₂). Causes: DKA, lactic acidosis, renal failure, diarrhoea, salicylate poisoning. HbA1c provides long-term glucose monitoring.
  5. Anion Gap: AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻) = 8–16 mEq/L. Represents unmeasured anions. High AG metabolic acidosis (MUDPILES) = accumulation of organic acids displacing HCO₃⁻. Normal AG acidosis = HCO₃⁻ loss with compensatory Cl⁻ retention (hyperchloraemic).
  6. Clinical significance of AG: Distinguishes cause of metabolic acidosis without additional tests. Guides treatment — DKA (insulin); lactic acidosis (treat sepsis/hypoxia); methanol (fomepizole, dialysis). Very high AG (>30) = severe → poor prognosis.
EQ — Free Radicals Have Beneficial Role [PYQ 2015-S, 2021]
"Free radicals may have some beneficial role also."
  1. Definition: Free radicals = species with ≥1 unpaired electron. Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) include O₂•⁻, H₂O₂, OH•. Harmful in excess but essential in controlled amounts.
  2. Beneficial role — Phagocytosis: NADPH oxidase in neutrophils/macrophages generates O₂•⁻ → dismuted to H₂O₂ → myeloperoxidase + Cl⁻ → HOCl (hypochlorous acid) → potent bactericidal. Essential for innate immunity. Deficiency (CGD) → recurrent life-threatening infections.
  3. Cell signalling: H₂O₂ acts as second messenger — activates NF-κB, MAP kinases, VEGF signalling. Low concentrations regulate insulin receptor signalling (inhibit PTP1B phosphatase → prolong insulin signal). Superoxide regulates vascular tone.
  4. Thyroid hormone synthesis: DUOX (thyroid oxidase) generates H₂O₂ which is essential for thyroid peroxidase-catalysed iodination of thyroglobulin → T3 and T4 synthesis. No H₂O₂ = no thyroid hormone.
  5. Nitric oxide: NO• (a radical) = powerful physiological vasodilator (activates guanylyl cyclase → cGMP → smooth muscle relaxation). Also neurotransmitter and immune effector. Essential for penile erection (PDE5 inhibitors work on this pathway).
  6. Reproductive function: ROS from sperm NADPH oxidase required for sperm capacitation and acrosome reaction → fertilisation. Excessive ROS (from leukocytes in semen) = male infertility.
  7. Examiner tip: Balance is key — physiological ROS at low levels = beneficial; excess ROS (oxidative stress) = damage. Antioxidant defence systems (SOD, catalase, GPx, Vit E, C) maintain this balance.
EQ — Renal Clearance as Predictor of Renal Failure [PYQ 2014, 2018]
"Renal clearance study is a nearly predictor of impending renal failure."
  1. Definition of clearance: Volume of plasma cleared of a substance per unit time (mL/min). CrCl = (Urine Cr × Volume/time) / Plasma Cr. Represents GFR in clinical practice.
  2. Ideal clearance marker: Inulin clearance = true GFR (freely filtered, not secreted, not reabsorbed). For clinical use, creatinine clearance is the standard approximation.
  3. Why clearance detects failure early: Serum creatinine remains in normal range until ~50% of nephron mass is lost (due to compensatory hyperfiltration in remaining nephrons). Creatinine clearance falls progressively as nephrons are lost — detects early renal impairment before serum Cr rises.
  4. CKD staging by GFR: Stage 1 (≥90) → Stage 2 (60–89) → Stage 3a (45–59) → Stage 3b (30–44) → Stage 4 (15–29) → Stage 5/ESRD (<15 mL/min). Clearance study allows staging and guides timing of renal replacement therapy.
  5. Modern formulae: MDRD (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease) and CKD-EPI equations estimate GFR from serum Cr, age, sex, race without 24-hour urine collection. Useful for population screening.
  6. Clinical implications: Drug dosing adjustments required when GFR <60 (metformin, NSAIDs, antibiotics). Early nephrology referral when GFR <30. Dialysis planning when GFR approaching 15.
EQ — Glutathione is Important Mediator of Detoxification [PYQ 2016-S, 2022]
"Glutathione is an important mediator for detoxification of toxic materials in humans."
  1. Structure: Tripeptide — γ-Glu-Cys-Gly. The -SH (thiol) of cysteine is the active group. The unusual γ-glutamyl bond protects from non-specific peptidase digestion. Synthesised in virtually all cells; liver is major site.
  2. Antioxidant role: GPx uses GSH to reduce H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + GSSG. ROOH → ROH + GSSG + H₂O. Regenerated by glutathione reductase (uses NADPH from HMP shunt). Maintains reducing environment in cells.
  3. Phase II detoxification (GST): Glutathione S-transferase conjugates GSH with electrophilic toxins → glutathione conjugate → cysteinyl conjugate → N-acetylcysteine conjugate (mercapturic acid) → excreted in urine. Detoxifies NAPQI (paracetamol), epoxides (carcinogens), disulfiram metabolites.
  4. RBC protection: RBCs produce NADPH only via HMP shunt (G6PD). G6PD deficiency → ↓ NADPH → ↓ GSH regeneration → oxidised Hb → Heinz bodies → haemolytic anaemia.
  5. Leukotriene synthesis: LTC₄ = leukotriene A₄ + GSH (LTC₄ synthase). LTC₄, LTD₄, LTE₄ (cysteinyl leukotrienes) = potent bronchoconstrictors in asthma. GSH is therefore also a precursor for inflammatory mediators.
  6. Clinical application: N-Acetylcysteine (NAC) — antidote for paracetamol overdose (replenishes GSH). Also used in contrast nephropathy prevention. Oral NAC (Mucomyst) for CF (mucolytic — reduces disulphide bonds in mucus).
