Glycolysis ★ EXAMINER FAV 🔴 TIER 1

📌 One-liner definition

Glycolysis is the cytoplasmic, oxygen-independent breakdown of one molecule of glucose (6C) into two molecules of pyruvate (3C), yielding a net gain of 2 ATP + 2 NADH.

⭐
Rate-Limiting Step: Rate Limiting Step Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) — catalyses fructose-6-P → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. Called the "pacemaker of glycolysis." Activated by AMP, ADP, fructose-2,6-bisphosphate. Inhibited by ATP, citrate, low pH.
🔁
Anaerobic Glycolysis (in RBCs, muscle during sprint): Pyruvate → Lactate (by LDH). This regenerates NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue without oxygen. RBCs have no mitochondria — 100% dependent on anaerobic glycolysis for ATP.
🩸
Rapoport-Luebering Shunt (RBC specific): 1,3-BPG → 2,3-BPG (bypasses substrate-level phosphorylation → no ATP made here). 2,3-BPG stabilises T-form of Hb → ↓ O₂ affinity → promotes O₂ delivery to tissues.
🩺 Clinical Lab Correlation: Fluoride Vials
  • Blood for glucose estimation is collected in fluoride vials because Fluoride irreversibly inhibits the enzyme Enolase, stopping RBC glycolysis during transport.

Irreversible Steps of Glycolysis (Bypass points for Gluconeogenesis)

  • Step 1 Glucose → G6P — by Hexokinase/Glucokinase (bypassed in GNG by Glucose-6-phosphatase)
  • Step 3 F6P → F-1,6-BP — by PFK-1 (bypassed in GNG by Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase)
  • Step 10 PEP → Pyruvate — by Pyruvate Kinase (bypassed in GNG by Pyruvate carboxylase + PEPCK)
⚠️
⚠️ EXAM TRAP — Glycolysis: Students confuse "rate-limiting" with "first step." PFK-1 (Step 3) is the RLS — NOT Hexokinase (Step 1). Also: the net ATP yield is 2 ATP (not 4) because 2 are invested first. Anaerobic glycolysis ends in Lactate, NOT Ethanol (that is yeast fermentation).
📝 MBBS Essay Blueprint: Glycolysis 10-Mark Answering Protocol

If the examiner asks: "Define glycolysis. Describe its pathway, energetics, and regulation. Add a note on its clinical importance/fluoride vial," use this bulletproof structure to secure a perfect 10/10:

  • 1. Definition & Location: Define as the cytoplasmic, oxygen-independent breakdown of glucose to pyruvate (aerobic) or lactate (anaerobic). State it occurs in all cells, localized in the cytosol.
  • 2. The Pathway Flowchart: Draw the 10-step flowchart. Bold the 3 irreversible steps (Step 1: Hexokinase, Step 3: PFK-1, Step 10: Pyruvate Kinase) using double-line arrows or red asterisks.
  • 3. Energetics Accounting Table: Detail the ATP yield:
    Pathway Stage / Step Aerobic Glycolysis Anaerobic Glycolysis
    ATP Invested -2 ATP (HK & PFK-1 steps) -2 ATP (HK & PFK-1 steps)
    Substrate-Level Phosphorylation +4 ATP (PGK & PK steps ×2) +4 ATP (PGK & PK steps ×2)

  • 4. Key Regulatory Steps: Focus on PFK-1 (Pacemaker). Mention its allosteric activators (AMP, F-2,6-BP) and inhibitors (ATP, Citrate), and touch upon Hexokinase vs. Glucokinase.
  • 5. Clinical Correlations: Briefly explain the mechanism of Fluoride vials (Enolase inhibition) and Rapoport-Luebering Shunt (RBC specific, 2,3-BPG oxygen delivery).

⭐
PDH regulation: Activated by: ADP, CoA, NAD⁺, Ca²⁺, pyruvate. Inhibited by: ATP, Acetyl-CoA, NADH (products inhibit). PDH kinase phosphorylates (inactivates) PDH; PDH phosphatase dephosphorylates (activates) it.
🚨
Clinical: Thiamine (B1) deficiency → PDH complex failure → pyruvate accumulates → converted to lactate → Lactic acidosis. Presents as Wernicke's encephalopathy in alcoholics. Transketolase (HMP shunt) also needs TPP — used as diagnostic marker.
⚠️
⚠️ EXAM TRAP — PDH Complex: PDH needs 5 coenzymes — TPP (B1), Lipoic acid, CoA (B5), FAD (B2), NAD⁺ (B3). Students often miss Lipoic acid (not a named B-vitamin). Remember: Arsenite poisons PDH by inactivating lipoic acid. PDH deficiency causes Lactic acidosis + neurological symptoms, NOT ketoacidosis.

TCA / Krebs Cycle ★ EXAMINER FAV 🔴 TIER 1

📌 One-liner definition

The TCA cycle is an amphibolic (both anabolic + catabolic), mitochondrial cyclic pathway where 1 Acetyl-CoA (2C) condenses with Oxaloacetate (4C) to generate 3 NADH + 1 FADH₂ + 1 GTP + 2 CO₂ per turn.

Irreversible (Regulated) Steps of TCA

  • 1 Citrate Synthase — Oxaloacetate + Acetyl-CoA → Citrate RLS — Inhibited by: ATP, NADH, Succinyl-CoA, Citrate
  • 3 Isocitrate Dehydrogenase — Activated by: ADP, Ca²⁺. Inhibited by: ATP, NADH
  • 4 α-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase — Similar regulation to PDH complex. Needs TPP, Lipoic acid, CoA, FAD, NAD⁺
🔗
TCA is AMPHIBOLIC — know why this is asked: Catabolic (breaks down Acetyl-CoA → CO₂ + energy). Anabolic (intermediates drawn off for biosynthesis): α-KG → glutamate → amino acids; Succinyl-CoA → porphyrins/haem; OAA → gluconeogenesis.
☠️
TCA Cycle Inhibitors (Frequent PYQ): Fluoroacetate (rat poison) inhibits Aconitase. Malonate is a classic competitive inhibitor of Succinate Dehydrogenase. Arsenite inhibits α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase.
⚠️
⚠️ EXAM TRAP — TCA Cycle: "Amphibolic" does NOT mean "ambiguous" — it means the cycle serves BOTH catabolic AND anabolic functions. Also: the yield per turn is 10 ATP (P/O ratio 2.5 for NADH, 1.5 for FADH₂) NOT the old 12 ATP figure. Succinate Dehydrogenase is the ONLY TCA enzyme embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane (all others are in the matrix).
📝 MBBS Essay Blueprint: Amphibolic TCA Cycle 10-Mark Answering Protocol

If the examiner asks: "TCA cycle is amphibolic in nature. Justify with suitable flowcharts/examples," use this high-scoring blueprint to secure 10/10 marks:

  • 1. Definition: Define amphibolic as having dual functions—both catabolic (breakdown of Acetyl-CoA to release ATP energy) and anabolic (intermediates drawn off for biosynthetic pathways).
  • 2. Pathway Flowchart: Draw the TCA cycle circle. Draw outward arrows from intermediates to their anabolic fates and inward arrows showing replenishing reactions.
  • 3. The 4 Key Anabolic Draw-off Vectors: Provide this exact table to show your mastery:
    Intermediate Anabolic Pathway / Fates
    Succinyl-CoA Condenses with glycine to form δ-ALA → Heme synthesis.
    Citrate Exported to cytosol to supply Acetyl-CoA for Fatty Acid & Cholesterol synthesis.
    α-Ketoglutarate Transaminated to Glutamate → yields amino acids & GABA neurotransmitter.
    Oxaloacetate Transaminated to Aspartate → Purine / Pyrimidine synthesis, or enters Gluconeogenesis.
  • 4. Justify Anaplerosis (Crucial to secure full marks): Explain that drawing off intermediates would drain the cycle and stop energy production. Explain how Pyruvate Carboxylase (biotin-dependent, activated by Acetyl-CoA) replenishes OAA to keep the cycle running.

Rate-Limiting Step: Rate Limiting Step G6PD (Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase) — generates NADPH. Activated by NADP⁺. Inhibited by NADPH.
🧬
Functions of NADPH from HMP shunt: (1) Reduces glutathione (GSH) — protects RBCs from oxidative damage. (2) Required for fatty acid synthesis. (3) Required for cholesterol synthesis. (4) Cytochrome P450 hydroxylation. (5) Phagocyte respiratory burst.
🩺 Clinical Vignette Case Study: G6PD Deficiency Hemolytic Crisis
Case Scenario: A 19-year-old male MBBS student presents to the medicine OPD with complains of dark, tea-colored urine, generalized weakness, and mild jaundice. He was recently diagnosed with Plasmodium vivax malaria and started on a course of Primaquine 48 hours ago.
Lab Findings: Hb: 8.2 g/dL (reduced), Reticulocyte count: 6% (elevated), Peripheral smear shows Heinz bodies (aggregated methemoglobin) and "bite cells". Indirect bilirubin is elevated.
How to Answer in the Exam:
  • Diagnosis: G6PD deficiency presenting with acute hemolytic anemia triggered by oxidant drug (Primaquine). (Inheritance: X-linked recessive).
  • Why mature RBCs are vulnerable: RBCs have no mitochondria or ribosomes. They cannot synthesize new proteins. Once G6PD degrades over their lifespan, it cannot be replaced. G6PD is their only source of NADPH.
  • Pathophysiology cascade: Primaquine exposure → generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) → reduced glutathione (GSH) cannot be regenerated due to lack of NADPH → Methemoglobin precipitates as Heinz bodies → splenic macrophages bite out these precipitates, creating bite cells and hemolyzing the RBCs.
  • Lab diagnostic caution: Do not measure G6PD activity during the acute hemolytic phase. Active hemolysis clears the most enzyme-deficient (older) RBCs first, leading to a false-normal result. Test G6PD levels 6-8 weeks after the crisis resolves.

Gluconeogenesis 🔴 TIER 1

📌 Definition

Synthesis of new glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors. Occurs mainly in liver (90%) and kidney cortex (10%) during fasting/starvation. Not simply the reverse of glycolysis — three irreversible steps of glycolysis are bypassed by unique enzymes.

🧱
Precursors (GALA-P mnemonic): Glycerol (from TAG breakdown) · Alanine + other amino acids (glucogenic AAs) · Lactate (Cori cycle) · Acetyl-CoA (indirectly, via OAA) · Propionate (odd-chain FA → Succinyl-CoA)

🔄
Cori Cycle (Lactic acid cycle): Muscle lactate → blood → liver → gluconeogenesis → glucose → blood → back to muscle. Net: transfers metabolic burden from muscle to liver. Energy-expensive: It costs exactly 6 ATP equivalents (4 ATP + 2 GTP) in the liver to synthesize 1 molecule of glucose from 2 pyruvates, compared to the 2 ATP gained in muscle glycolysis.
⚡
Regulation by Fructose-2,6-Bisphosphate (F-2,6-BP): Classic PYQ 2017 F-2,6-BP activates PFK-1 (glycolysis) and inhibits F-1,6-BPase (GNG) — a single molecule that switches between the two pathways. Insulin ↑ F-2,6-BP → glycolysis. Glucagon ↓ F-2,6-BP → GNG.
⚠️
⚠️ EXAM TRAP — Gluconeogenesis: Students often write that Gluconeogenesis occurs in the muscle. This is a fatal mistake! Muscle cannot perform gluconeogenesis because it lacks the key enzyme Glucose-6-Phosphatase. Also, fatty acids (even-chain) cannot serve as a precursor for glucose in humans because the Pyruvate Dehydrogenase reaction is irreversible (Acetyl-CoA cannot be converted back to pyruvate).
📝 MBBS Essay Blueprint: Gluconeogenesis & F-2,6-BP Switch Answering Protocol

If the examiner asks: "Define Gluconeogenesis. State its substrates, describe the bypass reactions, energy costs, and the reciprocal regulation by F-2,6-BP switch," organize your answer using this textbook structure to score maximum marks:

  • 1. Definition & Tissue Location: Synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors. Location: primarily Liver (90%) and Kidney cortex (10%) (NOT muscle!). Subcellular site: compartmentalized between Mitochondria, Cytosol, and Endoplasmic Reticulum.
  • 2. Precursors List (GALA-P): Glycerol (from lipid lipolysis), Amino acids (primarily Alanine/Glucogenic AAs), Lactate (from Cori cycle), Propionate (from odd-chain fatty acids).
  • 3. The 3 Compartmentalized Bypass Reactions:
    Bypass Step Glycolytic Enzyme (Irreversible) Gluconeogenic Bypass Enzymes & Compartment
    Bypass 1 Pyruvate Kinase 1. Pyruvate Carboxylase (Mitochondria; requires Biotin, ATP, CO₂; activated by Acetyl-CoA)
    2. PEPCK (Cytosol; converts OAA to PEP; requires GTP)
    Bypass 2 Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) Fructose-1,6-Bisphosphatase (Cytosol; allosterically inhibited by F-2,6-BP & AMP)
    Bypass 3 Hexokinase / Glucokinase Glucose-6-Phosphatase (Endoplasmic Reticulum membrane; absent in skeletal muscle)
  • 4. Energetic Cost Accounting: To synthesize 1 mole of glucose from 2 moles of Pyruvate, it costs exactly 6 high-energy phosphate bonds:
    • 2 ATP invested at the Pyruvate Carboxylase reaction (1 per Pyruvate)
    • 2 GTP invested at the PEPCK reaction (1 per OAA)
    • 2 ATP invested at the 1,3-BPG kinase step (reversed)
    • Total cost = 4 ATP + 2 GTP = 6 High-Energy Bonds (compared to only 2 ATP produced in glycolysis).
  • 5. The Reciprocal F-2,6-BP Master Switch: Describe how the bifunctional enzyme PFK-2 / FBPase-2 governs the switch:
    • Fed State / High Insulin: Dephosphorylation activates the PFK-2 domain → ↑ F-2,6-BP levels → powerfully activates PFK-1 (stimulates Glycolysis) & inhibits F-1,6-BPase (shuts down GNG).
    • Fasting State / High Glucagon: GPCR-cAMP-PKA pathway phosphorylates the bifunctional enzyme, activating the FBPase-2 domain → ↓ F-2,6-BP levels → relieves inhibition on F-1,6-BPase (stimulates GNG) & shuts off Glycolysis.

Fructose & Galactose Metabolism 🟡 TIER 2

⚖️ Fructose Disorders: Essential Fructosuria vs. Hereditary Fructose Intolerance (HFI)
Feature Essential Fructosuria Hereditary Fructose Intolerance (HFI) CRITICAL
Deficient Enzyme Fructokinase (first step of fructose metabolism) Aldolase B (cleaves Fructose-1-Phosphate)
Accumulation Fructose (excreted in urine; not toxic inside cells) Fructose-1-Phosphate (F-1-P) (trapped inside hepatocytes)
Pathophysiology Fructose is not phosphorylated; benignly filtered by kidneys or bypassed via Hexokinase. No ATP depletion. Phosphate Trapping: Buildup of F-1-P sequester-depletes intracellular inorganic phosphate (Pi) and ATP. Lack of ATP/Pi halts Glycogenolysis & Gluconeogenesis!
Clinical Signs Asymptomatic (incidentally detected as a reducing sugar in urine; Clinitest positive, glucose stick negative). Severe hypoglycemia, vomiting, jaundice, hepatomegaly, lactic acidosis, proximal renal tubule dysfunction (Fanconi syndrome) upon fructose/sucrose ingestion.
Dietary Therapy No restriction required. Benign condition. Strict elimination of Fructose, Sucrose, and Sorbitol from diet.
Examiner Trap Fructose gives a positive reducing sugar test (Clinitest). Do not confuse with Diabetes Mellitus (glucose dipstick is negative!). Symptoms appear only after weaning (introduction of fruit juices, honey, or formula milk containing sucrose).
⚖️ Galactose Disorders: GALK Deficiency vs. Classical Galactosemia (GALT Deficiency)
Feature Galactokinase (GALK) Deficiency Classical Galactosemia (GALT Deficiency) CRITICAL
Deficient Enzyme Galactokinase (GALK) (phosphorylates galactose) Galactose-1-Phosphate Uridyltransferase (GALT)
Accumulation Galactose (excreted in urine) & Galactitol (reduced by Aldose Reductase) Galactose-1-Phosphate & Galactitol (highly toxic)
Pathophysiology Galactose accumulates and is shunted to Galactitol. Galactitol accumulates in the lens, causing osmotic damage. Galactose-1-P accumulation traps inorganic phosphate and acts as a direct tissue toxin to liver, brain, kidneys, and lens.
Clinical Signs Mild presentation: Infantile cataracts, galactosemia, galactosuria. No renal/liver failure or developmental delay. Severe presentation: Failure to thrive, jaundice, hepatomegaly, infantile cataracts, intellectual disability, and life-threatening E. coli neonatal sepsis.
Dietary Therapy Eliminate galactose and lactose (milk/dairy products) from diet. Strict elimination of Galactose and Lactose (human and bovine milk) immediately from birth.
Examiner Trap Only cataracts are present. No liver damage or developmental delay. Contrast this with classical galactosemia. Symptoms start within the first few days of life, as soon as breastfeeding/milk feeding begins (unlike HFI, which starts later with weaning!).

🔥 Viva Rapid Fire — Carbohydrate Metabolism
Q1 Why can't RBCs use the TCA cycle?
RBCs have no mitochondria — TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation are mitochondria-dependent. RBCs rely entirely on anaerobic glycolysis for ATP. They also use HMP shunt to generate NADPH for maintaining reduced glutathione (protection against oxidative damage).
Q2 Why does Von Gierke's disease cause hyperuricaemia?
G6Pase deficiency → G6P accumulates → excess G6P shunted into HMP pathway → excess Ribose-5-phosphate → increased de novo purine synthesis → excess purines → increased uric acid production → hyperuricaemia and gout.
Q3 Why is gluconeogenesis NOT the simple reverse of glycolysis?
Three irreversible steps of glycolysis cannot be reversed. These are catalysed by Hexokinase, PFK-1, and Pyruvate Kinase. GNG uses unique bypass enzymes: Glucose-6-phosphatase, Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, and the Pyruvate Carboxylase + PEPCK system respectively.
Q4 What is the role of 2,3-BPG? Why is it important in stored blood?
2,3-BPG binds to the β-chains of deoxy-Hb, stabilising the T (tense/deoxy) form, reducing O₂ affinity → promotes O₂ delivery to tissues. In stored blood (bank blood), 2,3-BPG levels fall → Hb has increased O₂ affinity → poor O₂ delivery to recipient's tissues (stored blood paradox).
Q5 Why is TCA called amphibolic?
Amphibolic = both anabolic and catabolic. Catabolic: oxidises Acetyl-CoA → CO₂ + energy. Anabolic: intermediates are precursors for biosynthesis — α-KG → glutamate → amino acids; Succinyl-CoA → haem synthesis; OAA → gluconeogenesis; Citrate → fatty acid synthesis.
Q6 Why does muscle lack glucose-6-phosphatase but liver has it?
Liver's role is to maintain blood glucose homeostasis — it must release free glucose into circulation during fasting. G6Pase allows dephosphorylation of G6P → free glucose → export. Muscle is a glucose consumer — it needs G6P to stay within the cell for glycolysis. If muscle had G6Pase, glucose would leak out rather than be used for energy.
Q7 Fat can be synthesised from glucose but glucose cannot be synthesised from fat — justify.
Glucose → Acetyl-CoA (irreversible via PDH). Fatty acids → β-oxidation → Acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA cannot be converted back to pyruvate/OAA in net amounts in mammals — the PDH reaction is irreversible. Acetyl-CoA can only enter TCA (catabolic) or be used for lipid synthesis, not for GNG. Exception: glycerol backbone of TAG can yield glucose (via DHAP).
Q8 What is the significance of oral glucose in ORS (Oral Rehydration Solution)?
Glucose in ORS co-transports sodium via SGLT1 (Na⁺-glucose co-transporter) in the intestinal mucosa — a secondary active transport system. This Na⁺ uptake drags water with it by osmosis, rehydrating the patient. Glucose doesn't just provide energy — its primary role in ORS is to drive Na⁺ absorption even in secretory diarrhoea where other Na⁺ transporters are inhibited.
