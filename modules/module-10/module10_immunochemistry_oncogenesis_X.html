🔥 Last Day Revision Mode

ULTRA-CONCISE EXAM FACTS
  • Immunoglobulins: IgG (most abundant, crosses placenta), IgA (secretory, dimers in tears/milk), IgM (pentamer, primary response), IgE (allergic reactions, Type I hypersensitivity), IgD (B-cell surface marker).
  • Hypersensitivity: Type I (Anaphylactic, IgE mediated), Type II (Cytotoxic, IgG/IgM + complement, e.g., Rh incompatibility), Type III (Immune-complex, e.g., SLE, glomerulonephritis), Type IV (Delayed-type, T-cell mediated, e.g., Mantoux test, contact dermatitis).
  • Cell Cycle Checkpoints: G1/S (regulated by Cyclin D-CDK4/6 and Rb), G2/M (regulated by Cyclin B-CDK1). p53 acts at G1/S to trigger repair or apoptosis via Bax/Bcl2.
  • Oncogenes vs TSGs: Proto-oncogenes need one mutation (gain of function, e.g., Ras, Myc, ErbB2) to promote cancer. Tumour Suppressors need two mutations (loss of function, 'Two-hit hypothesis', e.g., Rb, p53, BRCA1).
  • Tumour Markers: AFP (Hepatocellular carcinoma, Yolk sac tumor), CEA (Colorectal cancer), PSA (Prostate cancer), CA-125 (Ovarian cancer), CA-19-9 (Pancreatic cancer).
  • Signal Transduction: cAMP pathway (GPCR → Adenylyl Cyclase → cAMP → PKA), IP3/DAG pathway (GPCR → Phospholipase C → IP3/DAG → Ca²⁺ release & PKC), RTK pathway (GF binding → dimerisation → autophosphorylation → Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK).

Immunoglobulins (Ig) = glycoproteins secreted by plasma cells (differentiated B lymphocytes). They are the humoral arm of adaptive immunity — specifically recognise and bind antigens.

Multiple Myeloma — IgG/IgA Related Disorder PYQ 2012-S

Malignant proliferation of a single plasma cell clone → secretes one type of Ig (M-protein/paraprotein) → monoclonal band on serum electrophoresis (M-spike in γ region). IgG most common (~55%), IgA next. Features: bone pain (osteolysis), anaemia, hypercalcaemia, renal failure (Bence-Jones protein = free light chains in urine), susceptibility to infection. Bence-Jones protein = free κ or λ light chains that pass through glomerular filter (small size).

MHCNot MHC-restrictedMHC-restricted — TCR recognises antigen only when presented by MHC
B Cell vs T Cell Mediated Immunity ⭐ PYQ 2021

B Cell (Humoral) Immunity:

  • B cells mature in Bone marrow
  • Activated by antigen (T cell help usually needed)
  • Differentiate into Plasma cells (antibody-secreting) + Memory B cells
  • Antibodies mediate: Neutralisation, Opsonisation, ADCC, Complement activation, Agglutination
  • Surface marker: CD19, CD20, CD21, BCR (surface Ig)
  • Best against: Extracellular pathogens, bacteria, toxins, viruses (before cell entry)

T Cell (Cellular) Immunity:

  • T cells mature in Thymus
  • Subtypes: CD4⁺ Helper T (Th1, Th2, Th17, Treg), CD8⁺ Cytotoxic T
  • CD4 recognises antigen on MHC II (APCs); CD8 on MHC I (any nucleated cell)
  • Th1: Activates macrophages, cytotoxic response, IFN-γ
  • Th2: B cell help, IgE, eosinophils, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 (allergy/parasites)
  • CTL (CD8⁺): Kills virus-infected cells via perforin/granzyme + Fas-FasL
  • Best against: Intracellular pathogens, viruses, fungi, tumours

Type I Hypersensitivity — Mechanism in Detail PYQ 2022
Sensitisation Phase (first exposure) Allergen → APCs → Th2 cells → IL-4, IL-13 → B cells class-switch to IgE production → IgE binds FcεRI on mast cells/basophils (sensitisation, no symptoms)
↓ Second (or subsequent) allergen exposure
Elicitation Phase (re-exposure) Allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells → FcεRI clustering → Degranulation (within seconds-minutes):
Preformed mediators: Histamine (vasodilation, pruritus, bronchoconstriction), Tryptase (mast cell marker)
Newly synthesised: Leukotrienes C4, D4, E4 (potent bronchoconstrictors — "slow-reacting substances"), PGD₂, PAF
Cytokines (late phase): IL-4, IL-5, TNF-α → eosinophil recruitment (late-phase reaction, 6–12 hours)
↓ Clinical manifestations
Systemic anaphylaxis: ↓ BP (vasodilation + ↑ permeability → hypovolaemia) + Bronchospasm + Urticaria. Treatment: Epinephrine (adrenaline) 1:1000 IM — reverses all components

Oncogenes & Proto-oncogenes

Proto-oncogene = normal gene that regulates cell growth, proliferation, differentiation. Oncogene = mutated/overexpressed proto-oncogene that drives uncontrolled proliferation. Proto-oncogenes are "accelerators" — in cancer, the accelerator is stuck down.
Mechanisms of Proto-oncogene → Oncogene Conversion ⭐ PYQ 2014-S, 2018, 2021, 2024
MechanismHow it worksExample
Point mutationSingle base change in coding sequence → constitutively active proteinRAS gene point mutation (G12V, G12D) → Ras GTPase stuck in GTP-bound active state → continuous proliferation signals. Found in 30% of all human cancers
Gene amplificationMultiple copies of proto-oncogene → overexpression of proteinN-MYC amplification → neuroblastoma. HER2/neu amplification → breast cancer. DHFR amplification → methotrexate resistance
Chromosomal translocationGene moved next to strong promoter OR fusion gene createdt(9;22) = Philadelphia chromosome → BCR-ABL fusion → constitutive tyrosine kinase → CML [PYQ 2023]. t(8;14) = c-MYC next to IgH promoter → Burkitt's lymphoma. t(11;22) = EWS-FLI1 fusion → Ewing's sarcoma [PYQ 2024]
Insertional mutagenesisRetroviral LTR inserts near proto-oncogene → ↑ transcriptionMouse mammary tumour virus (MMTV) activates Wnt1
Promoter rearrangementProto-oncogene brought under control of a strong promoterIg gene promoter fused to MYC (alternative to translocation)

Tumour Suppressor Genes

TSGs = "brakes" of cell cycle. Both copies must be lost for tumour development (Knudson's Two-Hit Hypothesis). First hit = mutation; Second hit = LOH (loss of heterozygosity), deletion, methylation.
Major Tumour Suppressor Genes ⭐ PYQ 2019-S, 2023, 2024
GeneChromosomeNormal FunctionCancer when lost
p53 (TP53) ⭐ MOST ASKED17p13.1Transcription factor activated by DNA damage → p21 (arrest) → BAX/PUMA (apoptosis) → GADD45 (DNA repair). Keeps normal p53 at low levels via MDM2-mediated ubiquitinationMutated in ~50% ALL cancers. Li-Fraumeni syndrome (germline) → diverse early-onset cancers
Rb (Retinoblastoma)13q14Sequesters E2F when hypophosphorylated → G1 arrest. "Master brake of cell cycle"Both alleles lost → Retinoblastoma (hereditary bilateral vs sporadic unilateral). Also osteosarcoma, small cell lung cancer
BRCA1 / BRCA217q21 / 13q12DNA repair (homologous recombination of DSBs). Genome integrityHereditary breast and ovarian cancer. BRCA1 → breast + ovarian + cervical. BRCA2 → breast (M+F) + pancreatic + prostate
APC5q21Regulates β-catenin (Wnt pathway). Promotes β-catenin degradation → prevents pro-proliferative gene expressionBoth alleles lost → Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP) → colorectal cancer. First hit in sporadic colorectal cancer too
PTEN10q23Phosphatase — dephosphorylates PIP₃ → opposes PI3K → ↓ AKT → ↑ apoptosisCowden syndrome. Prostate, breast, endometrial cancers
VHL3p25Targets HIF-1α for degradation. Controls angiogenesis via VEGF regulationVon Hippel-Lindau syndrome → Renal cell carcinoma (clear cell), haemangioblastoma. Loss → HIF-1α accumulates → VEGF → tumour vasculature
p53 as Guardian of the Genome ⭐ PYQ 2019-S, 2023, 2025
Normal state p53 kept low by MDM2 (E3 ubiquitin ligase) → ubiquitinates p53 → proteasomal degradation. MDM2 is itself induced by p53 (negative feedback)
↓ DNA damage, oncogenic stress, hypoxia, ribosomal stress
Activation ATM/ATR kinases phosphorylate p53 at Ser15, Ser20 → MDM2 cannot bind → p53 stabilised + acetylated + tetramerises → TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR active
↓ p53 transcribes target genes — decision based on damage severity
Reversible damage → ARREST p21 (CDKN1A) → inhibits CDK2 → Rb stays unphosphorylated → E2F sequestered → no S phase → G1/S arrest → time for DNA repair
OR
Irreparable damage → APOPTOSIS BAX, PUMA, NOXA → mitochondrial pathway → cytochrome c release → caspase 9 → effector caspases → cell death
OR
Chronic/replicative stress → SENESCENCE Permanent cell cycle arrest. Prevents tumourigenesis in pre-malignant lesions

Apoptosis

Apoptosis = programmed cell death. Characterised by: cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing, DNA laddering (180 bp fragments), apoptotic bodies (phagocytosed — NO inflammation). Distinct from necrosis (swelling, lysis, inflammation).
Two Pathways of Apoptosis ⭐ PYQ 2023, 2024

Intrinsic (Mitochondrial) Pathway:

DNA damage / Oxidative stress / p53 activation
↓ BAX/BAK (pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family) ↑; BCL2/BCL-XL ↓
Outer mitochondrial membrane permeabilisation → Cytochrome c release
↓
Cytochrome c + Apaf-1 + dATP → Apoptosome → activates Procaspase-9
↓ Caspase-9 (initiator)
Activates Caspase-3/6/7 (effectors) → cell demolition

Extrinsic (Death Receptor) Pathway:

FasL binds Fas (CD95) OR TNF binds TNFR1
↓ Receptor trimerisation → DISC formation
DISC recruits FADD + Procaspase-8 → Caspase-8 (initiator)
↓ Directly OR via BID → mitochondria (cross-talk)
Activates Caspase-3/6/7 (effectors) → cell death
Apoptosis vs Cancer PYQ 2023

BCL2 overexpression (t(14;18) in follicular lymphoma) → cells cannot undergo apoptosis → immortalised. Survivin (IAP family) overexpressed in many cancers → inhibits caspases. Loss of p53 → cannot trigger apoptosis. Cytochrome c also participates in ETC — dual role (electron carrier + apoptosis trigger).

Hallmarks of Cancer (Hanahan & Weinberg)

1. Self-sufficiency in growth signals (oncogenes) · 2. Insensitivity to anti-growth signals (TSG loss) · 3. Evasion of apoptosis (BCL2↑, p53 lost) · 4. Unlimited replicative potential (telomerase↑) · 5. Sustained angiogenesis (VEGF↑, VHL loss) · 6. Tissue invasion and metastasis (MMP↑, E-cadherin↓) · 7. Reprogramming of energy metabolism (Warburg effect) · 8. Evading immune destruction (PD-L1↑)

Warburg effect: Cancer cells preferentially use aerobic glycolysis (glucose → lactate even in O₂) rather than OXPHOS → produces lactate + provides biosynthetic intermediates for rapid growth. Basis for FDG-PET scan (↑ glucose uptake in tumours).

Signal Transduction

G-Protein Coupled Receptor (GPCR) Pathway ⭐ PYQ 2010, 2013, 2022, 2024
cAMP Second Messenger System — Glucagon/Adrenaline
Step 1 — Ligand binding Hormone (glucagon/adrenaline) binds 7-TM GPCR → conformational change → GDP on Gα replaced by GTP → Gα-GTP dissociates from Gβγ
↓ Gα-GTP activates effector
Step 2 — Second messenger production Gαs + Adenylyl Cyclase → ATP → cAMP (second messenger) + PPi
Gαi inhibits adenylyl cyclase → ↓ cAMP (e.g., somatostatin, muscarinic M2)
↓ cAMP activates PKA
Step 3 — Protein Kinase A (PKA) cAMP binds regulatory subunits of PKA → releases catalytic subunits → active PKA phosphorylates target proteins (Ser/Thr):
Glycogen phosphorylase kinase (↑) → glycogenolysis ↑
Glycogen synthase (↓) → glycogenesis ↓

Immunoglobulins & Immunity
Draw the structure of IgG and label all key regions. What is the difference between Fab and Fc?PYQ 2017-S, 2019, 2023

IgG structure: Y-shaped glycoprotein. MW ~150 kDa. 4 chains: 2 identical heavy (γ, ~50 kDa each) + 2 identical light (κ or λ, ~25 kDa each). Held together by disulphide bonds (inter-heavy in hinge region + heavy-light).

Fab (Fragment Antigen Binding):

  • Contains: VH + VL + CH1 + CL domains
  • Function: Antigen binding (2 Fab arms per IgG = bivalent)
  • Paratope = 3 CDRs (hypervariable loops) on each V domain
  • Monovalent after papain cleavage → cannot agglutinate antigen

Fc (Fragment Crystallisable):

  • Contains: CH2 + CH3 domains of both heavy chains
  • Function: Effector functions — classical complement activation (C1q binds CH2), FcγR binding (NK cells, macrophages for ADCC), FcRn binding (neonatal Fc receptor → transcytosis across placenta + prolonged half-life)
  • Glycosylated at Asn-297

Papain: cuts above hinge → 2 Fab + 1 Fc. Pepsin: cuts below hinge → 1 F(ab')₂ (still bivalent) + degraded pFc'

Only IgG crosses the placenta — explain the mechanism.PYQ implied in classification Qs

The neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) on placental syncytiotrophoblasts mediates transcytosis of IgG across the placental barrier.

  • FcRn binds IgG Fc region at acidic pH (in endosomes) after receptor-mediated endocytosis
  • Transported across cell → released at neutral pH on fetal side → IgG enters fetal circulation
  • This is the ONLY Ig small enough and with the right Fc for FcRn binding
  • IgM, IgA, IgE, IgD cannot cross (IgM too large = pentamer; others lack affinity for FcRn)
  • Clinical significance: Maternal IgG protects neonate for first 3–6 months (maternal IgG half-life ~23 days)
  • Danger: Maternal anti-Rh IgG crosses → haemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN)
  • Also: maternal thyroid-stimulating Ig (IgG) crosses → neonatal hyperthyroidism

FcRn also responsible for the long half-life of IgG (~23 days) — rescues IgG from lysosomal degradation in endothelial cells (same mechanism)

Oncogenes & Cancer
What is p53? Why is it called the "Guardian of the Genome"?PYQ 2019-S, 2023, 2025

p53: Tumour suppressor protein (53 kDa) encoded by TP53 gene on chromosome 17p13. Normally kept at low levels by MDM2-mediated ubiquitination → proteasomal degradation.

Why "Guardian of the Genome":

  • Sensor: Activated by DNA damage (ATM/ATR), oncogenic stress (ARF pathway), hypoxia, ribonucleotide depletion → phosphorylated → resistant to MDM2
  • G1/S checkpoint: p53 → p21 (CIP1) → inhibits CDK2-Cyclin E → Rb stays unphosphorylated → E2F sequestered → S-phase genes not expressed → arrest
  • Apoptosis: Irreparable damage → p53 → BAX, PUMA, NOXA (pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family) → mitochondrial pathway → caspase activation → cell death
  • DNA repair: p53 → GADD45, p48 (XPC partner) → nucleotide excision repair
  • Senescence: Chronic activation → p21, p16 → permanent growth arrest

Cancer relevance: Mutated in ~50% all human cancers (most commonly mutated TSG). Germline p53 mutation → Li-Fraumeni syndrome (sarcomas, breast cancer, brain tumours at young age). Loss of p53 → cells cannot arrest or die → genomic instability → cancer progression.

"Without p53, damaged cells survive and accumulate further mutations → cancer. p53 is the last line of defence — hence Guardian of the Genome."

Explain Knudson's Two-Hit Hypothesis with retinoblastoma as an example.PYQ implied in TSG questions

Two-Hit Hypothesis (Alfred Knudson, 1971):

  • Tumour suppressor genes require BOTH alleles to be inactivated for tumour development (recessive at cellular level)
  • Hereditary retinoblastoma: First hit = germline mutation in one RB1 allele (inherited). Second hit = somatic mutation/deletion of remaining normal allele in retinal cell. Because first hit is in every cell → only needs one more somatic hit → bilateral, multiple tumours, early age
  • Sporadic retinoblastoma: Both hits must occur somatically in the SAME retinal cell → much less likely → unilateral, single tumour, later age of onset
  • Why "Two-Hit": Rb protein (pRb) is the cell cycle brake. One functional Rb is sufficient (haploinsufficiency not seen in most cases). Both copies must be lost to release the brake

This model applies to: BRCA1/2 (one germline + one somatic → cancer); APC (FAP); VHL (renal cell carcinoma); p53 in Li-Fraumeni

Cancer may be caused by excessive activity of protein tyrosine kinase — explain.PYQ 2022

Normal RTK signalling: Growth factor binds RTK → transient Tyr kinase activation → proliferation signal → Tyr phosphatase terminates signal.

Excessive Tyr kinase in cancer:

  • Mutation → constitutive activation: RET point mutation (MEN2) → RET RTK always "on" → medullary thyroid carcinoma. KIT mutation → GIST (gastrointestinal stromal tumour) → imatinib target
  • Gene amplification: HER2/neu amplification → excessive ErbB2 RTK → sustained RAS-ERK + PI3K-AKT → breast cancer cell proliferation, survival, metastasis
  • Fusion gene: BCR-ABL (t(9;22)) → constitutively active non-receptor Tyr kinase → RAS-ERK + PI3K-AKT → CML. Imatinib (Gleevec) specifically inhibits BCR-ABL kinase → targeted therapy
  • Autocrine loop: Tumour makes its own growth factor (PDGF) → activates its own RTK → self-sustaining proliferation

Key concept: Normal RTK = light switch (on/off). Cancer RTK = stuck "on" switch. Targeted therapies (imatinib, trastuzumab, gefitinib) are designed to flip it back off.

Signal Transduction
Methotrexate is used in anti-cancer therapy — explain its mechanism.PYQ 2011, 2012, 2016-S, 2019-S

Target: Dihydrofolate Reductase (DHFR)

Mechanism:

  • Folic acid → DHF → THF (by DHFR, requires NADPH)
  • THF → one-carbon donors → thymidylate synthesis (dTMP), purine ring synthesis
  • MTX = structural analogue of folic acid → binds DHFR with ~1000× higher affinity than natural substrate → competitive inhibition (but effectively irreversible at therapeutic concentrations)
  • DHF cannot be reduced → THF pool depleted → no thymidylate (TMP) → no DNA synthesis → S-phase arrest → apoptosis

Selectivity: Rapidly dividing cells (cancer, immune cells) rely more on new nucleotide synthesis → more sensitive.

Rescue: Leucovorin (folinic acid = 5-formyl-THF) given after high-dose MTX → bypasses DHFR → rescues normal cells (cancer cells cannot take up leucovorin as well)

Uses: Leukaemia (ALL, CML), rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, Crohn's disease, ectopic pregnancy

Resistance mechanisms: ↑ DHFR expression (gene amplification), mutant DHFR with ↓ affinity, ↓ folate carrier, ↓ polyglutamation

Telomerase is involved in ageing and cancer — explain.PYQ 2016-S, 2025

Telomeres: TTAGGG repeats (2–15 kb) at chromosome ends. Protect chromosomes from end-degradation, fusion, and genomic instability.

End-replication problem: Normal DNA polymerase cannot replicate the very 3' end of lagging strand → telomeres shorten with each cell division (~50–200 bp/division).

Telomerase: Ribonucleoprotein enzyme (TERT = catalytic subunit, TERC = RNA template with AAUCCC sequence). Extends telomeres using its own RNA as template (reverse transcriptase activity).

Ageing:

  • Somatic cells (no telomerase) → telomeres shorten → reach critical length → Hayflick limit → replicative senescence → contributes to ageing
  • Stem cells + germ cells express low telomerase → partial protection
  • Dyskeratosis congenita = telomerase mutations → premature ageing syndrome, aplastic anaemia

Cancer:

  • Cancer cells reactivate TERT → telomeres maintained → unlimited replication potential → immortalisation
  • ~85–90% of human cancers have telomerase activity
  • Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres (ALT) = recombination-based → 10–15% of cancers
  • TERT = potential anticancer drug target

Summary: Telomere shortening → ageing/senescence. Telomerase reactivation → cancer immortality. Two sides of the same coin.

EQ — Cancer is a Gene-Related Disease [PYQ 2011-S, 2024]
"Cancer is a gene-related disease."
  1. Definition: Cancer = clonal expansion of a cell that has acquired multiple somatic mutations in genes controlling proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. It is fundamentally a disease of the genome.
  2. Two classes of cancer genes: (a) Proto-oncogenes → Oncogenes: gain-of-function mutations → dominant, need only ONE allele mutated (accelerators stuck on). (b) Tumour Suppressor Genes: loss-of-function → recessive, need BOTH alleles lost (brakes cut). Both classes must be compromised for full malignancy.
  3. Genetic alterations: Point mutations (RAS in pancreatic cancer), gene amplification (HER2 in breast cancer), chromosomal translocations (BCR-ABL → CML, MYC → Burkitt's lymphoma), deletions (RB1 → retinoblastoma), methylation silencing (APC in colorectal cancer), microsatellite instability (Lynch syndrome).
  4. Multi-step carcinogenesis: Vogelstein's model (colorectal cancer): APC mutation → RAS activation → SMAD4 loss → p53 loss → invasive carcinoma. Each step = additional mutation. Typically requires 5–7 driver mutations accumulated over years.
  5. Inherited cancers: Germline mutations (BRCA1/2, APC, RB1, TP53, MLH1/MSH2) → provide "first hit" in every cell → cancer at earlier age with defined tissue specificity.
  6. Epigenetics: DNA methylation (silences TSGs), histone modification → heritable gene expression changes without DNA sequence change → also contribute to cancer development.
  7. Examiner tip: Cancer requires MULTIPLE genetic changes (multi-step). No single gene mutation is sufficient. Hereditary cancers have germline first hit (fewer steps needed → earlier onset).
EQ — Proto-oncogenes are Regulatory Genes [PYQ 2021]
"Proto-oncogenes are regulatory genes."
  1. Definition: Proto-oncogenes = normal cellular genes (c-oncogenes) that encode proteins regulating growth, differentiation, and survival. They are NOT cancer genes — they are NORMAL genes performing essential physiological functions.
  2. Categories of products: Growth factors (c-SIS → PDGF); Growth factor receptors with kinase activity (ERBB1/EGFR, HER2, RET, KIT); Intracellular signal transducers (RAS, RAF); Nuclear transcription factors (MYC, FOS, JUN); Cell cycle regulators (Cyclin D1, CDK4); Apoptosis regulators (BCL2).
  3. Regulatory roles in normal physiology: Tightly controlled — expressed only when cell needs to proliferate (wound healing, development). Expression is transient, turned off by negative feedback, and regulated by growth factor signals.
  4. How they become oncogenes: Any gain-of-function change — point mutation, amplification, translocation, insertional mutagenesis — converts the proto-oncogene (normal) to an oncogene (constitutively active). Only ONE allele needs to be altered (dominant).
  5. Key examples: RAS proto-oncogene = GTPase switch. Oncogenic RAS = GTP-bound permanently (cannot hydrolyse GTP due to Gly-12 mutation). MYC = transcription factor for cell growth genes; translocated MYC in Burkitt's lymphoma = constitutively expressed under IgH promoter.
  6. Clinical relevance: Targeting oncogenes has led to targeted cancer therapies — imatinib (BCR-ABL), trastuzumab (HER2), erlotinib (EGFR). Understanding proto-oncogene function is essential for drug design.
EQ — Apoptosis is Important for Preventing Cancer [PYQ 2023]
"Apoptosis is very important for preventing cancer."
  1. Definition of apoptosis: Programmed cell death — genetically encoded, energy-dependent process. Characterised by cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, DNA laddering (nucleosomal fragments of 180 bp), membrane blebbing, apoptotic bodies (phagocytosed — NO inflammation). Mediated by caspases.
  2. Normal anticancer role of apoptosis: (a) p53 activation after DNA damage → if irreparable → BAX, PUMA, NOXA → mitochondrial pathway → cell dies before it can become cancerous. (b) Activated T cells kill virally infected or pre-malignant cells via Fas-FasL and perforin-granzyme. (c) c-MYC activation (e.g., from growth factor stimulation) normally triggers ARF pathway → p53-dependent apoptosis → prevents MYC-driven proliferation from going uncontrolled.
  3. How cancer cells evade apoptosis: BCL2 overexpression (t(14;18) follicular lymphoma) → sequesters BAX, BIM → cannot trigger MOMP. p53 mutation (50% of cancers) → cannot activate apoptotic programme. Survivin/IAP family overexpression → inhibit caspases. Loss of Fas expression → evade CTL killing.
  4. Therapies targeting apoptosis: Venetoclax (BCL2 inhibitor) → restores apoptosis in CLL/AML. BH3 mimetics → release BAX/BAK. Traditional chemotherapy agents work partly by triggering apoptosis in cancer cells. Immune checkpoint inhibitors → reactivate CTLs → Fas-FasL killing
  5. Cytochrome c — dual role: In healthy cells = electron carrier in ETC (Complex III → Complex IV). In apoptosis = released from mitochondria → apoptosome → caspase-9 activation. This dual role connects metabolism and cell death.
  6. Examiner tip: Always mention both intrinsic (mitochondrial, caspase-9) and extrinsic (death receptor, caspase-8) pathways. In cancer prevention, intrinsic pathway (p53-BAX axis) is most important.
EQ — Insulin Receptor Has Tyrosine Kinase Activity [PYQ 2010-S]
"Insulin receptor has tyrosine kinase activity."
  1. Structure of insulin receptor: Heterotetrameric glycoprotein — 2α + 2β subunits linked by S-S bonds (αα and αβ). α subunits: extracellular, insulin binding site. β subunits: transmembrane + cytoplasmic, contain Tyr kinase domain. MW ~350 kDa (mature receptor).
  2. Evidence of Tyr kinase: β-subunit cytoplasmic domain contains intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity (belongs to RTK superfamily). Kinase domain: DFG motif, activation loop Tyr (Y1158, Y1162, Y1163) — autophosphorylation of these Tyr residues activates kinase ~10-fold.
  3. Mechanism of activation: Insulin binds α subunit → conformational change → transautophosphorylation of β subunit Tyr residues → kinase activated → phosphorylates IRS-1 (insulin receptor substrate-1) and Shc at Tyr residues → PI3K-AKT pathway (glucose uptake, glycogen synthesis, anti-apoptosis) + RAS-ERK pathway (gene expression, growth).
  4. GLUT4 translocation: PI3K-AKT → AS160 (TBC1D4) phosphorylation → Rab-GTPase activation → GLUT4 vesicle fusion with plasma membrane → ↑ glucose uptake (muscle, adipose).
  5. Insulin resistance: In Type 2 DM — receptor Tyr kinase activity impaired (serine phosphorylation of IRS-1 by inflammatory kinases like JNK, IKK → blocks Tyr signalling) → downstream pathway fails → hyperglycaemia despite insulin.
  6. Cancer connection: IGF-1R (structurally similar to insulin receptor) → activates same PI3K-AKT pathway → promotes cancer cell survival and proliferation. Targeting IGF-1R = cancer therapy strategy.
