ULTRA-CONCISE EXAM FACTS
  • Vitamin A: Retinol (transport as RBP), Retinal (visual cycle, 11-cis Retinal + Opsin = Rhodopsin), Retinoic Acid (gene regulation). Deficiency causes Xerophthalmia and Bitot's spots.
  • Vitamin D: Active form is 1,25-DHCC (Calcitriol). Stimulates intestinal Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻ absorption. Deficiency causes Rickets in children, Osteomalacia in adults.
  • Vitamin K: Coenzyme for gamma-glutamyl carboxylase. Adds carboxyl group to factors II, VII, IX, X, protein C and S. Inhibited by Warfarin.
  • Thiamine (B1): TPP is coenzyme for Pyruvate Dehydrogenase, alpha-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase, and Transketolase. Deficiency causes Beriberi (Wet/Dry) and Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.
  • Folate Trap: B12 deficiency leads to trapping of folate as N5-methyl-THF, which cannot be converted back → secondary functional folate deficiency → Megaloblastic Anaemia.
  • Iron Absorption: Absorbed in duodenum as ferrous (Fe²⁺) iron. Stored as ferritin, transported in blood as transferrin (Fe³⁺). Hepcidin regulates iron export via ferroportin.
01

Fat-Soluble Vitamins

Fat-soluble = A, D, E, K. Stored in liver and adipose tissue. Absorbed with dietary fat (require bile salts). Toxicity possible with excess (especially A & D). NOT excreted in urine.
Mnemonic
"Fat Dads Eat Kale" = Fat-soluble: A (retinol), D (calciferol), E (tocopherol), K (koagulation)
Vitamin A (Retinol) ⭐ PYQ 2025 — Wald's Visual Cycle
  • Forms: Retinol (alcohol), Retinal (aldehyde), Retinoic acid (acid), β-Carotene (provitamin — plant source; 6 µg = 1 µg retinol)
  • Storage: Liver as retinyl esters (stellate cells)
  • Transport: Retinol-Binding Protein (RBP) + Transthyretin complex in blood
  • Biochemical roles: Vision (retinal), Gene expression (retinoic acid → nuclear receptor RAR/RXR), Glycoprotein synthesis, Antioxidant (β-carotene)

Deficiency:

  • Night blindness (first sign) — rod cell failure
  • Xerophthalmia — dry eyes, Bitot's spots
  • Keratomalacia — corneal ulceration → blindness
  • Follicular hyperkeratosis (toad skin)
  • Impaired immunity (↓ IgA secretion)

Toxicity (Hypervitaminosis A):

  • Acute: ↑ ICP, vomiting, headache
  • Chronic: alopecia, hepatotoxicity, teratogenicity
Wald's Visual Cycle — Rhodopsin Cycle (PYQ 2014-S, 2025)
Dark-adapted Rod Cell 11-cis Retinal + Opsin → Rhodopsin (purple-red pigment, Scotopsin + 11-cis retinal)
↓ Light hits rhodopsin → photon absorbed
Bleaching reaction — isomerisation 11-cis Retinal → all-trans Retinal → Rhodopsin dissociates → Opsin released → conformational change → nerve impulse generated
↓ all-trans Retinal → all-trans Retinol (by retinol dehydrogenase, NADH)
Recovery in darkness (RPE cells) all-trans Retinol → all-trans Retinal → 11-cis Retinal (by retinal isomerase) → combines with opsin → Rhodopsin regenerated Vitamin A is NOT consumed — it is recycled. But losses occur → dietary replenishment needed
Night Blindness — Molecular Basis

Vit A deficiency → insufficient 11-cis retinal → rhodopsin cannot be regenerated adequately after bleaching → rod cells fail to respond in dim light → nyctalopia (night blindness). The cone cells (colour vision, bright light) use Iodopsin and are less affected initially. Bitot's spots = keratinised conjunctival cells, pathognomonic of Vit A deficiency.

Vitamin D (Calciferol) — The Hormone Vitamin ⭐ PYQ 2019-S, 2023 — Calcium homeostasis
Vitamin D Activation — Step by Step
Step 1 — Skin (UV-B, 290-315 nm) 7-dehydrocholesterol → UV-B → Cholecalciferol (Vitamin D3) Diet: D2 (ergocalciferol) from plants; D3 (cholecalciferol) from animals/fish liver oil
↓ Transported by Vitamin D-Binding Protein (DBP) to liver
Step 2 — Liver (25-hydroxylase) Cholecalciferol → 25-hydroxycholecalciferol (Calcidiol / 25-OH-D3) Storage form — measured in blood to assess Vit D status
↓ Transported to kidney (PTH stimulates this step)
Step 3 — Kidney (1α-hydroxylase) KEY STEP 25-OH-D3 → 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol (Calcitriol / 1,25-(OH)₂-D3) ACTIVE form. Stimulated by: PTH, low Ca²⁺, low Pi. Inhibited by: high Ca²⁺, FGF-23
↓ Acts via nuclear receptor (VDR) — steroid hormone mechanism
Actions of Calcitriol Intestine: ↑ Ca²⁺ + Pi absorption (induces Calbindin D9k, TRPV6) Bone: stimulates osteoblasts + mobilises Ca²⁺ (with PTH) Kidney: ↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption → Net: ↑ serum Ca²⁺ and Pi
Kidney failure → cannot perform 1α-hydroxylation → active calcitriol ↓ → Ca²⁺ absorption ↓ → secondary hyperparathyroidism → renal osteodystrophy. Treatment: give active calcitriol (1,25-OH₂-D3) directly — NOT plain Vitamin D.

Vitamin E (Tocopherol) PYQ 2012-S, 2022
  • Most potent lipid-soluble antioxidant
  • Protects polyunsaturated FA (PUFA) in membranes from lipid peroxidation
  • Protects RBC membrane — prevents haemolysis
  • Regenerated by Vitamin C (ascorbate)
  • Deficiency: Haemolytic anaemia (especially in premature neonates), peripheral neuropathy, spinocerebellar ataxia, retinopathy
  • Works synergistically with selenium (glutathione peroxidase)
Vitamin K (Phylloquinone) ⭐ PYQ 2014-S, 2019, 2023
  • Essential cofactor for γ-carboxylation of glutamate residues in clotting factors
  • Factors dependent: II (Prothrombin), VII, IX, X, Protein C, Protein S, Protein Z
  • Mechanism: Vit K (hydroquinone form) donates electrons → γ-carboxylase adds CO₂ to Glu → γ-carboxyglutamate (Gla) → binds Ca²⁺ + phospholipid (essential for clot formation)
  • Vit K epoxide formed → Vit K epoxide reductase regenerates Vit K (blocked by Warfarin)
  • K1 (Phylloquinone) = plants; K2 (Menaquinone) = gut bacteria; K3 (Menadione) = synthetic
  • Deficiency: Bleeding tendency, prolonged PT/INR, Haemorrhagic disease of newborn (HDN) — neonates have sterile gut, no K2 synthesis

Diarrhoea: malabsorption, mucosal atrophy

Dementia: depression, confusion, eventually dementia

Death (if untreated)

Classic association: maize-dependent diet (low Trp + niacin bound as niacytin — not bioavailable)

Vitamin C & Collagen

Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid) ⭐ PYQ 2018-S, 2021, 2017-S

Biochemical Functions:

  • Collagen synthesis: Cofactor for Prolyl hydroxylase AND Lysyl hydroxylase → hydroxyproline + hydroxylysine formation → essential for triple helix stability
  • Antioxidant: Scavenges ROS (O₂•⁻, OH•, H₂O₂). Regenerates Vitamin E from tocopheryl radical
  • Iron absorption: Reduces Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺ (absorbed form) in intestinal lumen → ↑ non-haem iron absorption (~3-fold)
  • Norepinephrine synthesis: Cofactor for Dopamine β-hydroxylase
  • Hydroxylation of drugs/steroids (cytochrome P450)
  • Carnitine synthesis (two hydroxylation steps)
  • Tyrosine metabolism

Scurvy — Deficiency:

  • Perifollicular haemorrhage (corkscrew hairs) — pathognomonic
  • Bleeding gums, gingivitis, tooth loosening
  • Poor wound healing
  • Subperiosteal haemorrhage (children — painful)
  • Anaemia (haemolytic + microcytic)
  • Delayed bone healing — Trümmerfeld zone (Barlow's disease in infants)
  • Mechanism: defective collagen → weakened vessel walls + connective tissue
Scurvy Symptoms
"Bleeding Gums, Wound Healing, Perifollicular = BaWP" — Barlow's, Weakness, Perifollicular haemorrhage

Folate & B12 — Folate Trap

This is the highest-yield biochemistry topic in this module. The folate trap explains why B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia AND neurological damage while folate deficiency causes ONLY megaloblastic anaemia (no neuro).
Folate Metabolism & The Methyl Folate Trap
Dietary Folate (Folic Acid) Dietary polyglutamate → Deconjugated → Folic acid (PteGlu) → Reduced to Dihydrofolate (DHF) by DHFR → Further reduced to Tetrahydrofolate (THF) — active form
↓ THF accepts one-carbon units → methyleneTHF → methylTHF
Key Reaction — Methionine Synthase (requires B12) Methyl-THF + Homocysteine → THF + Methionine This reaction REQUIRES Vitamin B12 (as methylcobalamin) to transfer the methyl group
↓ If B12 deficient → methyl-THF CANNOT donate its methyl group → accumulates
THE FOLATE TRAP (B12 deficiency) All cellular folate becomes "trapped" as methyl-THF — the monoglutamate form cannot be re-polyglutamated and retained inside cells → No free THF available → cannot synthesise thymidylate (dTMP) → DNA synthesis impaired → megaloblastic anaemia (same as folate deficiency) But: homocysteine accumulates → demyelination → NEUROLOGICAL DAMAGE (B12-specific, not seen in folate deficiency alone)
↓ Free THF used in thymidylate synthesis
Thymidylate Synthase Reaction (DNA synthesis) dUMP + Methylene-THF → dTMP + DHF DHF → THF (by DHFR). Blocked by: Methotrexate (DHFR inhibitor), 5-FU (thymidylate synthase inhibitor)

Never give folate alone to a B12-deficient patient without giving B12. It corrects the anaemia (mask) but the neurological degeneration (subacute combined degeneration) continues and worsens undetected.
Neural Tube Defects & Folate in Pregnancy

Folate required for de novo purine and thymidylate synthesis → DNA synthesis for neural tube closure (days 21–28 of gestation — before most women know they're pregnant). Periconceptional folic acid supplementation (400 µg/day before conception and in first trimester) reduces NTD risk by ~70%. NTDs: anencephaly, spina bifida, meningomyelocele.

One-Carbon Metabolism & SAM

One-carbon metabolism = transfer of single carbon units between molecules. Folate and B12 are the key carriers. SAM is the universal methyl donor for most biological methylation reactions.
SAM Cycle — One-Carbon Metabolism Hub
Methionine Activation (ATP-dependent) Methionine + ATP → S-Adenosylmethionine (SAM) + PPi + Pi Catalysed by Methionine adenosyltransferase. SAM = universal methyl donor
↓ SAM donates CH₃ to any acceptor (DNA, RNA, proteins, phospholipids, neurotransmitters)
Methylation Reactions by SAM SAM → S-Adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) after donating methyl group Examples: Norepinephrine → Epinephrine (COMT); Guanidinoacetate → Creatine; DNA CpG methylation (epigenetics); Phosphatidylethanolamine → Phosphatidylcholine; Histamine → N-methylhistamine
↓ SAH hydrolysed → Homocysteine + Adenosine
Homocysteine Fate Route 1 (Remethylation): Homocysteine + Methyl-THF → Methionine (needs B12 + Methionine synthase) — salvage pathway Route 2 (Transsulphuration): Homocysteine + Serine → Cystathionine → Cysteine (needs PLP/B6) Cysteine → Glutathione (GSH). Taurine. Sulphate.
Homocysteinaemia PYQ 2024 — B12 deficiency case

Causes of elevated homocysteine:

  • B12 deficiency (↓ remethylation)
  • Folate deficiency (↓ methyl-THF supply)
  • B6 deficiency (↓ transsulphuration)
  • Homocystinuria — CBS deficiency (autosomal recessive)
  • MTHFR mutation (C677T polymorphism — most common)

Clinical consequences:

  • Accelerated atherosclerosis (endothelial damage)
  • Arterial and venous thrombosis
  • Homocystinuria: lens dislocation (ectopia lentis), Marfanoid habitus, intellectual disability, osteoporosis, thromboembolism
  • Independent CVD risk factor

Iron Metabolism

Iron is the most commonly deficient micronutrient worldwide. The body has no regulated excretory mechanism for iron — all regulation happens at the level of ABSORPTION. Total body iron ~4g in males, ~2.5g in females.
Iron Absorption, Transport & Storage
Dietary Sources & Forms Haem iron (myoglobin, Hb from meat): absorbed directly as intact haem via HCP1 receptor → ~25% absorption, not affected by dietary factors Non-haem iron (ferric Fe³⁺ from plants, cereals): must be reduced to Fe²⁺ first → absorbed by DMT-1 (divalent metal transporter) → lower bioavailability (~5%) Vitamin C (ascorbate) reduces Fe³⁺→Fe²⁺ → ↑ non-haem absorption 3-fold
↓ Inside enterocyte — iron can be stored as Ferritin or exported
Export from Enterocyte — Basolateral Side Fe²⁺ exported by Ferroportin (only iron exporter in body) → Oxidised to Fe³⁺ by Hephaestin (ferroxidase, copper-dependent) → Binds Apotransferrin → Transferrin (carries 2 Fe³⁺ atoms, β₁-globulin)
↓ Transferrin delivers iron to cells via Transferrin Receptor (TfR)
Cellular Uptake — Receptor-mediated Endocytosis Transferrin-TfR complex → endosome (acidified) → Fe³⁺ released → reduced to Fe²⁺ by Steap3 → exits endosome via DMT-1 → used for haem/Fe-S cluster synthesis or stored as Ferritin
↓ Excess stored; regulation by Hepcidin
Iron Storage — Ferritin & Haemosiderin Ferritin: 24 subunits, stores up to 4500 Fe atoms as Fe(OH)₃ core. Serum ferritin = best indicator of iron stores (1 ng/mL ≈ 8 mg stored iron) Haemosiderin: Degraded ferritin, insoluble, stains with Perl's Prussian blue. Seen in iron overload.
Hepcidin — Master Regulator of Iron ⭐ High Yield
Hepcidin = 25-amino acid peptide hormone made by LIVER. It is the key iron hormone that controls the amount of iron entering the circulation.

When iron/inflammation HIGH → Hepcidin ↑:

  • Hepcidin binds Ferroportin → internalisation and degradation of Ferroportin
  • No Ferroportin → iron TRAPPED in enterocytes + macrophages
  • Iron absorption ↓, iron recycling ↓
  • Serum iron ↓

When iron LOW / hypoxia / anaemia → Hepcidin ↓:

  • Ferroportin expressed on enterocyte surface
  • Iron freely exported into plasma
  • Iron absorption ↑
  • Serum iron ↑
Anaemia of Chronic Disease — Hepcidin Mechanism

Chronic infection/inflammation → IL-6 → liver produces ↑ Hepcidin → Ferroportin degraded → iron trapped in macrophages → iron not available for erythropoiesis → normocytic/microcytic anaemia with normal-to-high iron stores (serum ferritin ↑, serum iron ↓, TIBC ↓ — distinguishes from iron deficiency anaemia).

Calcium Homeostasis & Vitamin D

Normal serum calcium: 8.5–10.5 mg/dL. 50% ionised (active), 40% albumin-bound, 10% complexed. Regulated by: PTH, Calcitriol (1,25-OH₂-D3), Calcitonin.
Calcium Homeostasis — 3-Hormone System
Trigger: Serum Ca²⁺ falls below normal Parathyroid glands sense ↓ Ca²⁺ via CaSR (calcium-sensing receptor)
↓ PTH secreted within minutes
PTH Actions (rapid, within minutes to hours) Bone: Osteoclast stimulation (via osteoblast → RANKL) → bone resorption → Ca²⁺ + Pi released Kidney: ↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption (distal tubule), ↑ Pi excretion (phosphaturia), ↑ 1α-hydroxylase → calcitriol production Net: ↑ serum Ca²⁺, ↓ serum Pi
↓ PTH also stimulates calcitriol production (slower, hours-days)
Calcitriol Actions (slower, genomic) Intestine: ↑ Ca²⁺ absorption (Calbindin D9k), ↑ Pi absorption (NaPi-IIb) Bone: facilitates mineralisation (with adequate Ca/Pi) Kidney: ↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption Net: ↑↑ serum Ca²⁺ and Pi
↓ When Ca²⁺ returns to normal → thyroid C-cells respond
Calcitonin (from thyroid C-cells) — when Ca²⁺ HIGH Inhibits osteoclasts → ↓ bone resorption ↑ Renal Ca²⁺ excretion Net: ↓ serum Ca²⁺ Less important in adults; marker for medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC)
Hypocalcaemia

Causes: Hypoparathyroidism, Vit D deficiency, CKD, hypomagnesaemia (↓ PTH secretion + resistance).

Clinical: Tetany (Chvostek's sign — facial twitch; Trousseau's sign — carpopedal spasm with BP cuff), convulsions, QT prolongation, cataracts (chronic).

Biochemistry: ↓ Ca, ↑ PTH (secondary), ↑ ALP (if Vit D deficiency)

Hypercalcaemia

Causes: Primary hyperparathyroidism, malignancy (PTHrP, bone mets), Vit D toxicity, sarcoidosis, milk-alkali syndrome.

Clinical: "Bones, Stones, Groans, Psychic Moans" — osteitis fibrosa cystica, nephrolithiasis/nephrocalcinosis, constipation/abdominal pain, depression/psychosis.

Severe: Cardiac arrhythmias (short QT)

Kwashiorkor — Biochemical Basis of Oedema ⭐ PYQ 2021, 2023
The oedema in Kwashiorkor is NOT due to excess sodium or heart failure — it is purely from hypoalbuminaemia reducing plasma oncotic pressure.

Collagen Post-Translational Modifications

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the body (~30% of total protein). Its synthesis involves an extraordinary number of co- and post-translational modifications — each is a potential exam question.
Collagen Synthesis — Intracellular to Extracellular
Step 1 — Gene → Translation mRNA → Pre-pro-α chains (contains signal peptide + N/C propeptides) Gly-X-Y repeat throughout: X = often Pro, Y = often Hydroxyproline
↓ Enter ER lumen — Signal peptide cleaved → Pro-α chains
Step 2 — Hydroxylation (ER) — Requires Vitamin C + O₂ + Fe²⁺ Prolyl hydroxylase: Proline → Hydroxyproline (4-Hyp stabilises triple helix via H-bonds) Lysyl hydroxylase: Lysine → Hydroxylysine (attachment point for O-linked glycosylation + cross-link formation) Both use α-ketoglutarate + O₂; require Fe²⁺ and Ascorbate (Vit C) to re-reduce Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺
↓ Some hydroxylysines glycosylated with galactose ± glucose
Step 3 — Triple Helix Formation (ER) Three pro-α chains → C-propeptide nucleates → zipper-like folding → Procollagen (triple helix with N/C propeptides)
↓ Secreted via Golgi into extracellular space
Step 4 — N/C Propeptide Cleavage (extracellular) Procollagen N-protease + C-protease cleave propeptides → Tropocollagen (native collagen monomer) Cleavage defect → Dermatosparaxis type Ehlers-Danlos (EDS)
↓ Self-assembly into collagen fibrils
Step 5 — Cross-linking by Lysyl Oxidase (extracellular) — requires Copper Lysyl oxidase oxidises Lys/HydroxyLys → Allysine → forms covalent cross-links between adjacent tropocollagen molecules → mature collagen fibril (very high tensile strength) Copper deficiency → defective cross-linking → weak connective tissue (seen in Menkes disease)

Viva & Exam Q&A

Vitamins — Fat-Soluble
Explain the molecular basis of night blindness in Vitamin A deficiency.PYQ 2014-S, 2025

Visual cycle (Wald cycle): 11-cis Retinal (Vit A aldehyde) combines with Scotopsin in rod cells → Rhodopsin. Light → 11-cis Retinal → all-trans Retinal → conformational change → nerve impulse. In dark, all-trans Retinal recycled back to 11-cis Retinal via RPE cells.

In Vit A deficiency: Insufficient 11-cis Retinal for rhodopsin regeneration after bleaching → rod cells fail to respond in dim/dark conditions → nyctalopia (night blindness). Cone cells (colour vision) need Iodopsin and are less sensitive, hence colour vision preserved initially.

Examiner tip: Mention that Vit A is NOT consumed in the visual cycle — it is recycled. Night blindness = defect in RECOVERY phase of the cycle.
Why does Vitamin K deficiency cause bleeding? Name the factors affected.PYQ 2014-S, 2019, 2023

Vit K acts as cofactor for γ-carboxylase enzyme. This enzyme adds a carboxyl group (CO₂) to specific glutamate residues in clotting factor precursors → forming γ-carboxyglutamate (Gla) residues → these chelate Ca²⁺ → Ca²⁺ binding anchors the factors to phospholipid surface of platelets → essential for clot formation.

Vit K-dependent factors: 2, 7, 9, 10, Protein C, Protein S, Protein Z

Without γ-carboxylation, these factors are produced (PIVKA — Proteins Induced by Vit K Absence) but are non-functional → bleeding tendency → ↑ PT, ↑ PTT.

Warfarin inhibits Vit K epoxide reductase → blocks Vit K recycling → same anticoagulant effect.

HDN (Haemorrhagic disease of newborn): sterile gut → no K2 from bacteria + minimal transfer across placenta + low in breast milk → neonatal bleeding. Prophylactic Vit K given at birth.
Folate Trap & B12
Explain the folate trap. Why does B12 deficiency cause neurological damage but folate deficiency does not?PYQ 2018, 2022, 2024

Folate trap: In B12 deficiency, the reaction Methyl-THF + Homocysteine → THF + Methionine (by Methionine synthase, needs B12) cannot proceed. All cellular folate becomes trapped as methyl-THF. Cannot be polyglutamated and retained intracellularly. Free THF depleted → DNA synthesis impaired → megaloblastic anaemia.

Why neuro damage only in B12 deficiency: B12 has a separate second reaction — conversion of Methylmalonyl-CoA → Succinyl-CoA (needs Adenosylcobalamin). In B12 deficiency, Methylmalonyl-CoA accumulates → incorporated into myelin → abnormal branched fatty acids → demyelination of dorsal + lateral spinal cord columns → Subacute Combined Degeneration (SACD) — posterior column (fine touch, vibration) + lateral corticospinal tract (motor) affected.

Folate deficiency causes anaemia (via same dTMP synthesis block) but has NO second B12 reaction to mimic → no SACD.

Danger: Giving folate to B12-deficient patient corrects the anaemia (untraps folate partially?) but doesn't restore B12 → SACD continues undetected → permanent neurological damage.
What is the significance of methylmalonyl acid in urine for diagnosis of B12 deficiency?PYQ 2024

B12 (as adenosylcobalamin) is required for Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase: Methylmalonyl-CoA → Succinyl-CoA. In B12 deficiency, this step fails → Methylmalonyl-CoA accumulates → excreted as Methylmalonic acid (MMA) in urine.

MMA elevation is specific for B12 deficiency — not elevated in folate deficiency. This is the key lab test to differentiate the two causes of megaloblastic anaemia. Also elevated in Propionic acidaemia and Methylmalonic acidaemia (enzyme defects).

Practical: Measure both serum MMA and homocysteine — MMA specific for B12; homocysteine elevated in both B12 and folate deficiency.

Iron & Minerals
Describe iron absorption, transport and storage. Name the iron-containing proteins.PYQ 2012-S, 2014-S, 2022, 2025

Iron-containing proteins:

  • Haem proteins: Haemoglobin (O₂ transport), Myoglobin (O₂ storage in muscle), Cytochromes (ETC — I, II, III, IV), Catalase (H₂O₂ decomposition), Peroxidases
  • Non-haem proteins: Ferritin + Haemosiderin (storage), Transferrin (transport), Ferroportin (export), DMT-1 (intestinal import), Ferredoxin, Iron-Sulphur clusters (Complex I, II)

Absorption: Haem iron → HCP1 receptor (25% absorption). Non-haem Fe³⁺ → Dcytb (duodenal cytochrome b, needs Vit C to reduce Fe³⁺→Fe²⁺) → DMT-1 into enterocyte → stored as Ferritin OR exported via Ferroportin + Hephaestin (Cu-dependent ferroxidase oxidises Fe²⁺→Fe³⁺) → Transferrin in plasma.

Regulation: Hepcidin (liver) degrades Ferroportin when iron stores high or inflammation → blocks export → reduces serum iron.

Storage: Ferritin in liver, spleen, bone marrow. Serum ferritin = most sensitive indicator of iron stores. Haemosiderin = insoluble storage, formed from partial lysosomal degradation of ferritin aggregates.

Wilson's disease is a disorder of copper metabolism — justify.PYQ 2022

Wilson's disease is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutation in the ATP7B gene (chromosome 13q14), encoding a copper-transporting P-type ATPase located in hepatocytes.

Molecular mechanism: ATP7B normally exports copper from hepatocytes into bile for excretion AND incorporates copper into ceruloplasmin. In Wilson's, ATP7B absent/dysfunctional → copper not exported into bile → not incorporated into ceruloplasmin → copper accumulates in liver (toxic, free radical formation) → cirrhosis → leaks into blood → deposits in brain (basal ganglia → neuropsychiatric symptoms), cornea (Kayser-Fleischer rings), kidney, joints.

Lab: ↓ Serum ceruloplasmin (<20 mg/dL), ↑ urinary copper (>100 µg/24h), ↑ liver copper (>250 µg/g dry weight), ↓ serum copper (paradoxically — most copper is ceruloplasmin-bound; free/non-ceruloplasmin copper ↑).

Treatment: D-penicillamine or Trientine (copper chelation); Zinc (competes with copper absorption); Liver transplant in fulminant cases.
Collagen & PEM
Why does Vitamin C deficiency cause scurvy? What is the molecular link to collagen?PYQ 2018-S, 2021

Molecular link: Collagen synthesis requires hydroxylation of Proline (→ Hydroxyproline) and Lysine (→ Hydroxylysine) by Prolyl hydroxylase and Lysyl hydroxylase respectively. Both enzymes are mixed-function oxidases that require Fe²⁺ in their active site. During the reaction, Fe²⁺ is oxidised to Fe³⁺. Vitamin C (ascorbate) regenerates Fe²⁺ from Fe³⁺ — without it, enzyme becomes inactive after one catalytic cycle.

Without Hydroxyproline, the collagen triple helix cannot form properly → unstable procollagen → secreted defective collagen → weak connective tissue → vessel walls fragile → bleeding.

Clinical features explained: Perifollicular haemorrhage (corkscrew hairs) = defective perifolliicular capillaries. Bleeding gums = weak gingival connective tissue. Poor wound healing = defective collagen deposition. Subperiosteal bleeding in children = weak periosteum.

Differentiate Marasmus from Kwashiorkor.PYQ 2021, 2023

Marasmus: Total calorie + protein deficiency. Infant < 1 year. Severely emaciated ("skin and bones"), muscle wasting, no subcutaneous fat. No oedema. Serum albumin near normal. Liver normal (body uses fat stores, muscle for energy). No fatty liver. "Marasmus = Metabolic Adaptation" — body adapts to total starvation.

Kwashiorkor: Protein deficiency with adequate calories (carbs). Child 1–5 years. Appearance: oedema (pitting), pot belly, moon face, skin changes (flaky paint dermatosis), hair hypopigmentation + flag sign. Serum albumin ↓↓ (< 3.5 g/dL). Fatty liver (↓ apolipoprotein synthesis → VLDL cannot form → TG accumulates). Oedema = ↓ oncotic pressure from ↓ albumin.

Key biochemical difference: In Kwashiorkor, insulin (from carbs) promotes amino acid uptake into muscle → liver deprived of AAs → ↓ albumin + apolipoprotein synthesis. In Marasmus, amino acids mobilised from muscle for energy + gluconeogenesis → albumin maintained better.

EQ — Iron Absorption, Transport, Storage [PYQ 2012-S, 2014-S, 2022, 2025]
"Diagrammatically discuss the absorption, transport and storage of iron. Enlist the iron-containing proteins. Justify the role of cytochrome in electron transport chain."
  1. Dietary Iron: Two forms — Haem iron (Fe²⁺, from meat, 25% absorbed, HCP1 receptor) and Non-haem iron (Fe³⁺, from plants/cereals, lower bioavailability, DMT-1 after reduction to Fe²⁺ by Dcytb/Vit C).
  2. Absorption (duodenum + upper jejunum): Fe²⁺ enters enterocyte via DMT-1. Stored as Ferritin (mucosal block) OR exported via Ferroportin. Hephaestin (Cu-containing ferroxidase) oxidises Fe²⁺→Fe³⁺ for loading onto Transferrin. Vit C ↑ absorption by keeping iron in Fe²⁺ form and by ↑ mucosal cell ferritin synthesis (actually, Vit C mainly reduces Fe³⁺ in lumen).
  3. Transport: Transferrin (β₁-globulin, binds 2 Fe³⁺) transports iron in plasma. TIBC = Total Iron Binding Capacity. Normal saturation ~33%. TfR1 mediates cellular uptake via clathrin-coated endocytosis.
  4. Storage: Ferritin (24-subunit protein, stores up to 4500 Fe atoms as ferric oxyhydroxide polymer — soluble, non-toxic). Serum ferritin reflects stores. Haemosiderin (degraded ferritin aggregates, insoluble, Prussian blue stain +) in iron overload.
  5. Regulation by Hepcidin: Liver-derived peptide. High iron/inflammation → ↑ Hepcidin → degrades Ferroportin → ↓ iron export from enterocytes + macrophages → anaemia of chronic disease mechanism.
  6. Iron-containing proteins: Haem: Hb, Myoglobin, Cytochromes (b, c, c1, a, a3), Catalase, Peroxidase. Non-haem: Ferritin, Haemosiderin, Transferrin, Fe-S clusters (Complexes I, II, III).
EQ — Folate Trap [PYQ 2018, 2022, 2024]
"Vitamin B12 should be given along with folic acid to treat folic acid deficient anaemia."
  1. The Problem: B12 deficiency and folate deficiency both cause megaloblastic anaemia — because both impair dTMP synthesis → defective DNA synthesis → large immature RBCs. Without distinguishing the cause, giving only folate to a B12-deficient patient may seem logical.
  2. The Folate Trap mechanism: B12 (methylcobalamin) is essential for Methionine Synthase: Methyl-THF + Homocysteine → THF + Methionine. In B12 deficiency, methyl-THF cannot donate its methyl group → all folate trapped as methyl-THF → no free THF → no dTMP synthesis → megaloblastic changes persist.
  3. Why giving folic acid alone to B12-deficient patient is dangerous: Large doses of folic acid can partially overcome the trap (increase methyl-THF beyond the trapping capacity) → temporarily corrects the blood picture → anaemia improves → patient and doctor think they are treating correctly. BUT the underlying B12 deficiency continues → Methylmalonyl-CoA accumulates → demyelination → SACD progresses silently → irreversible neurological damage.
  4. The correct approach: ALWAYS diagnose the exact deficiency. Measure serum B12, serum folate, serum MMA (↑ in B12 only), serum homocysteine (↑ in both). Give B12 to all megaloblastic anaemia patients unless folate deficiency is definitively proven.
  5. Why B12 must accompany folate: In any mixed deficiency (common in malnourished patients), B12 ensures the folate trap is fully released, methionine synthesis is restored (needed for SAM → methylation reactions → myelin maintenance), and the SACD risk is eliminated.
EQ — Calcium Homeostasis [PYQ 2023, 2016-S]
"How is the homeostasis of plasma calcium level achieved? Indicate factors modifying calcium absorption from gut."
  1. Normal serum calcium: 8.5–10.5 mg/dL. Three fractions: ionised (50%, biologically active), albumin-bound (40%), complexed with citrate/phosphate (10%). Corrected calcium = measured Ca + 0.8 × (4 − albumin g/dL).
  2. PTH — acute regulator: ↓ Ca²⁺ → parathyroid glands (CaSR) → PTH secreted → Bone: osteoclast activation → Ca²⁺ + Pi released; Kidney: ↑ tubular Ca²⁺ reabsorption, ↑ Pi excretion, ↑ 1α-hydroxylase → Calcitriol. Net: ↑ serum Ca²⁺.
  3. Calcitriol (1,25-OH₂-D3) — chronic regulator: Intestine: induces Calbindin D → ↑ Ca²⁺ absorption (main route for long-term homeostasis). Bone + kidney: as above. Genomic action via VDR nuclear receptor.
  4. Calcitonin — when Ca²⁺ high: C-cells of thyroid → inhibits osteoclasts → ↓ bone resorption → ↓ serum Ca²⁺. Less important in adults; marker for medullary thyroid carcinoma.
  5. Factors increasing Ca²⁺ absorption: Vit D (calcitriol — main driver), Acidic pH (duodenum), Lactose (forms soluble complex), Lysine/Arginine (amino acids), Low dietary calcium (↑ receptor expression), Growth hormone, PTH (indirectly via calcitriol).
  6. Factors decreasing Ca²⁺ absorption: Phytates and Oxalates (form insoluble complexes — phytic acid in cereals, oxalic acid in spinach/tea), Fatty acids (form calcium soaps — steatorrhoea → ↓ Ca²⁺), Alkaline pH, High dietary phosphate, Glucocorticoids (↓ calcitriol synthesis), Excess Mg²⁺ (competes for absorption).
