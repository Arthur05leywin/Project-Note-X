The Big Picture

🩺 Why This Matters Clinically

Enzyme inhibition is not just theory — it is the basis of almost every drug in medicine. Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase. Methotrexate inhibits DHFR. Aspirin irreversibly inhibits COX. Allopurinol is a suicide inhibitor of xanthine oxidase. Understanding inhibition kinetics tells you how drugs work, why they fail, and how resistance develops.

02

Enzyme Catalysis & Kinetics Derivation

🔑 Thermodynamic Principles

Enzymes are biological catalysts that accelerate chemical reactions by stabilizing the transition state and lowering the activation energy (ΔG‡). They do not alter the overall free energy change (ΔG) or shift the equilibrium position of the reaction; they simply allow the system to reach equilibrium much faster.

The catalytic event occurs within the Active Site, a highly specialized three-dimensional cleft or pocket lined with catalytic and binding amino acid residues. Active site binding is explained by Emil Fischer's classic Lock-and-Key Model (rigid complementarity) and Daniel Koshland's modern Induced Fit Model (dynamic conformational change upon ligand binding).

The Briggs-Haldane Steady-State Derivation

To understand how enzyme velocity behaves mathematically, we derive the fundamental Michaelis-Menten Equation using the Briggs-Haldane steady-state approximation.

// The Reaction Scheme
Enzyme + Substrate
(E + S)
⇌ (k₁ / k₋₁)
Enzyme-Substrate Complex
(ES)
→ (k₂)
Enzyme + Product
(E + P)
📐
The Steady-State Assumption: The concentration of the transition state [ES] remains constant over time. Therefore, the rate of ES formation equals the rate of ES breakdown:
k₁[E][S] = (k₋₁ + k₂)[ES]
🧠
Defining the Michaelis Constant (Km): By grouping the rate constants:
Km = (k₋₁ + k₂) / k₁
Substitute Km back: [E][S] = Km[ES].
💾
Conservation of Enzyme: Since free enzyme [E] cannot be measured directly, we express it in terms of total enzyme [E]t:
[E] = [E]t - [ES]
Substituting this into the Km equation yields: ([E]t - [ES])[S] = Km [ES].
🚀
Solving for [ES] & Velocity: Solving for [ES] gives: [ES] = ([E]t[S]) / (Km + [S]). Since velocity v = k₂[ES] and maximum velocity Vmax = k₂[E]t, we multiply both sides by k₂ to arrive at the Michaelis-Menten Equation:
v = (Vmax · [S]) / (Km + [S])
This hyperbolic equation explains that at low [S] ([S] ≪ Km), velocity is first-order, while at high [S] ([S] ≫ Km), the enzyme is saturated and velocity is zero-order (v ≈ Vmax).

Lineweaver-Burk Linearization

Taking the double reciprocal of the Michaelis-Menten equation yields the linear Lineweaver-Burk Equation:

1/v = (Km/Vmax) · (1/[S]) + 1/Vmax

This allows researchers to plot experimental data as a straight line (y = mx + c), where the Y-intercept represents 1/Vmax and the X-intercept represents -1/Km.

Allosteric Regulation & Cooperativity

🌀 Mechanics of Allostery

Not all enzymes obey classic Michaelis-Menten kinetics. Allosteric enzymes are oligomeric proteins (multiple subunits) that exhibit cooperativity — where the binding of a ligand to one active site influences the binding properties of other active sites.

Allosteric enzymes display a characteristic sigmoidal (S-shaped) saturation curve rather than a hyperbola. They bind effectors at a distinct regulatory pocket called the allosteric site, causing conformational shifts that either activate (positive cooperative) or inhibit (negative cooperative) the enzyme.

The Two Models of Cooperativity

Medical biochemistry defines two prominent models explaining allosteric shifts:

Feature Concerted Model (MWC Model) Sequential Model (KNF Model)
Proponents Monod, Wyman, and Changeux Koshland, Némethy, and Filmer
Subunit States All subunits must exist in the same state (either all Tense [T] or all Relaxed [R]). No hybrid states allowed. Subunits can change states individually. Hybrid states (some T, some R) are common.
Symmetry Rule Symmetry is preserved. Binding of a substrate shifts the equilibrium of the entire oligomer between the T-state (low affinity) and R-state (high affinity). Symmetry is not preserved. Substrate binding to one subunit induces a conformational shift only in that subunit, which then sequentially alters neighbors.
Effector Action Activators stabilize the R-state; inhibitors stabilize the T-state, shifting the S-curve left or right. Binding increases or decreases affinity of neighboring active sites sequentially via induced fit.

Covalent Modification & Zymogen Activation

⚡ Post-Translational Regulation

In addition to allosteric effectors, the body uses two highly efficient molecular mechanisms to regulate enzyme activity on demand:

  • Reversible Covalent Modification: The addition or removal of a chemical group (most commonly phosphate, via kinases and phosphatases) to specific serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues of the enzyme. For example, glycogen phosphorylase is active when phosphorylated, while glycogen synthase is inactive when phosphorylated.
  • Zymogen Activation (Proteolytic Cleavage): The irreversible activation of an inactive enzyme precursor (zymogen/proenzyme) by the selective cleavage of one or more peptide bonds. This is crucial for digestive enzymes and blood coagulation factors, preventing them from destroying the cells that synthesize them.

Zymogen Cascades in Digestion

The classic medical example of zymogen activation is the activation cascade in the duodenum:

Viva Pearl: If trypsin is prematurely activated inside the pancreas, it causes a catastrophic self-digestion of the organ, clinically presenting as acute hemorrhagic pancreatitis. The pancreas protects itself by synthesizing Pancreatic Trypsin Inhibitor (PSTI) to block any trace trypsin.

Km & Vmax — The Core Concept ★ EXAMINER FAV

📐 Michaelis-Menten Equation

v = (Vmax [S]) / (Km + [S])

Where: v = reaction velocity, Vmax = maximum velocity, [S] = substrate concentration, Km = Michaelis constant

🎯
Km Definition: Substrate concentration at which reaction velocity = ½ Vmax. It reflects the affinity of enzyme for substrate. Low Km = High affinity (less substrate needed). High Km = Low affinity.
⚡
Hexokinase vs Glucokinase (Classic PYQ!): Hexokinase has low Km for glucose — works even at low blood glucose (brain, RBC). Glucokinase has high Km — only active after a meal when glucose is high (liver, pancreatic β cells). This is why glucokinase acts as a "glucose sensor."

"Competitive Kills Km, Non-competitive Nails Vmax"
Competitive → Km changes (↑), Vmax stays.
Non-competitive → Vmax changes (↓), Km stays.

"Competitive meets at Y, Non-competitive meets at X"

🔥 Viva Rapid Fire — Enzyme Inhibition
Q1 What happens to Km and Vmax in competitive inhibition?
Km increases (apparent), Vmax remains unchanged. The inhibitor competes with substrate for the active site. High substrate concentration can displace the inhibitor.
Q2 Why is allopurinol called a suicide inhibitor?
Allopurinol is first oxidized by xanthine oxidase to alloxanthine (oxypurinol), which then permanently and covalently binds to the active site of xanthine oxidase, inactivating it irreversibly. The enzyme participates in its own inhibition — hence "suicide."
Q3 Why is Km of glucokinase higher than hexokinase?
Glucokinase (hepatic) is a low-affinity, high-Km enzyme that functions as a glucose sensor — it activates only when blood glucose is high (postprandial). Hexokinase has high affinity (low Km) and works even at basal glucose levels, ensuring glucose supply to brain and RBCs at all times.
Q4 What does the Lineweaver-Burk plot show?
It's a double reciprocal plot of 1/v vs 1/[S]. Y-intercept = 1/Vmax. X-intercept = -1/Km. Slope = Km/Vmax. Used to distinguish types of inhibition: competitive lines cross at Y-axis; non-competitive lines cross at X-axis.
Q5 Name four non-functional plasma enzymes and their significance.
1) ALT/SGPT — liver damage (hepatocellular). 2) AST/SGOT — liver, cardiac damage. 3) Alkaline Phosphatase — obstructive jaundice, bone disease. 4) Amylase — pancreatitis. 5) CK-MB — Myocardial infarction. These enzymes normally function intracellularly; their appearance in plasma indicates cell damage.
Q6 Differentiate inhibitors from uncouplers (ETC context — frequent combo Q)
Inhibitors block electron transport at specific complexes (e.g., CN⁻ at Complex IV, Rotenone at Complex I) — stop both electron flow AND ATP synthesis. Uncouplers (e.g., 2,4-DNP, thermogenin) allow electron transport to continue but dissipate the proton gradient — electrons flow but NO ATP is made. Heat is generated instead.
