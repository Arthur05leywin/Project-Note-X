Pterion ⭐ PYQ 2013, 2017, 2020, 2024
  • H-shaped junction of 4 bones: Frontal, Parietal, Temporal (squamous), Greater wing of Sphenoid
  • Located 4 cm above midpoint of zygomatic arch, 3.5 cm behind frontozygomatic suture
  • Thinnest and weakest part of the skull vault
  • Deep to temporalis muscle — overlaid by temporal fascia
  • Internally corresponds to anterior branch of middle meningeal artery
Pterion Fracture → Extradural Hematoma

Blunt trauma to temple → pterion fractures → lacerates anterior division of middle meningeal artery → arterial bleeding accumulates in extradural space → lenticular (biconvex) hematoma on CT.

Classic story: "Lucid interval" — patient briefly conscious, then rapid deterioration as hematoma expands. Needs urgent surgical evacuation.

Scalp & Meninges

Layers of the Scalp ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2016, 2021
SCALP Mnemonic
S · C · A · L · P

Skin (thick, hair-bearing, sebaceous cysts here) → Connective tissue dense (arteries, veins, nerves run here — doesn't retract when cut → bleed profusely) → Aponeurosis epicranialis (epicranial aponeurosis — connects frontalis to occipitalis) → Loose areolar tissue (clinically dangerous layer) → Pericranium (periosteum of skull — stops at sutures)

Blood Supply (from periphery):

  • Anteriorly: Supratrochlear + Supraorbital (from ophthalmic — ICA territory)
  • Laterally: Superficial temporal artery (from ECA)
  • Posterolaterally: Posterior auricular artery (ECA)
  • Posteriorly: Occipital artery (ECA)
  • All anastomose freely → scalp tolerates large flaps

Nerve Supply:

  • Anterior: V1 (supratrochlear, supraorbital)
  • Lateral (temple): V3 (auriculotemporal), zygomaticotemporal
  • Posterior: C2 (greater occipital nerve — dorsal ramus) — main nerve of scalp
  • Posterolateral: C2/C3 (lesser occipital, great auricular from cervical plexus)
The loose areolar (L) layer is the "dangerous layer" — infection/blood/pus spreads freely here in all directions, limited only by skull sutures (pericranium fuses to suture edges → containment walls). Scalp infections can spread to emissary veins → intracranial sinuses → cavernous sinus thrombosis.

Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis

Infection from "danger triangle of face" (area from corners of mouth to bridge of nose) → facial vein → ophthalmic vein → cavernous sinus thrombosis. Presents: proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia (III, IV, VI palsy), pain (V1, V2), fever.

Posterior Triangle PYQ 2015, 2019, 2023

Boundaries:

  • Anterior: Posterior border of SCM
  • Posterior: Anterior border of Trapezius
  • Inferior: Middle 1/3 of clavicle
  • Roof: Investing layer of deep cervical fascia
  • Floor: Prevertebral muscles (splenius capitis, levator scapulae, scaleni)
  • Divided by inferior belly of omohyoid into: occipital triangle (sup) + subclavian (omoclavicular) triangle (inf)

Contents:

  • CN XI (accessory nerve) — crosses obliquely, supplies SCM + trapezius. Most vulnerable nerve in posterior triangle surgery.
  • Cervical plexus branches (C2–C4): lesser occipital, great auricular, transverse cervical, supraclavicular
  • 3rd part subclavian artery (in subclavian triangle)
  • Trunks of brachial plexus (in subclavian triangle)
  • External jugular vein (crosses SCM to pierce roof)
  • Occipital, transverse cervical, suprascapular arteries
CN XI Injury in Posterior Triangle

Neck dissection, lymph node biopsy, or penetrating trauma in posterior triangle → CN XI injury → trapezius paralysis → drooping shoulder, winging of scapula (lateral), difficulty raising arm above horizontal, "shoulder droop" deformity. SCM paralysis if nerve injured proximally.

Muscles of Head & Neck

Sternocleidomastoid (SCM) ⭐ PYQ 2014, 2019, 2021
  • Origin: Two heads — sternal head (manubrium), clavicular head (medial 1/3 clavicle)
  • Insertion: Mastoid process + lateral 1/2 superior nuchal line
  • Nerve: CN XI (motor) + C2, C3 (proprioception/pain)
  • Actions:
    • Unilateral: tilts head to same side, rotates face to opposite side
    • Bilateral: flexes head on neck; assists in forced inspiration (raises sternum)
Torticollis (Wry Neck)

Fibrosis of SCM → shortening → head tilted to affected side, face rotated away. Congenital torticollis: SCM haematoma at birth → fibrosis. Spasmodic torticollis: intermittent spasm — treat with Botox to SCM. Surgery = SCM release.

Temporomandibular Joint & Jaw Dislocation ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2013, 2023
The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a bilateral synovial joint of the condylar variety. Articular surfaces are covered by fibrocartilage (not hyaline) and divided completely by an articular disc.

Joint Anatomy & Ligaments:

  • Articular Surfaces: Condyle of mandible and the mandibular fossa + articular tubercle of temporal bone.
  • Articular Disc: Fibrocartilaginous plate. Upper compartment allows gliding/translation (protrusion/retraction). Lower compartment allows hinge/rotation (elevation/depression).
  • Stabilizing Ligaments:
    1. Lateral (Temporomandibular): Main ligament. Restricts posterior condyle movement, protects external auditory meatus.
    2. Sphenomandibular: Accessory. Remnant of Meckel's cartilage. Runs from sphenoid spine to lingula of mandible.
    3. Stylomandibular: Accessory. Runs from styloid process to angle of mandible. Separates parotid and submandibular glands.

Movements & Muscles:

  • Elevation (Close jaw): Masseter, Temporalis, Medial Pterygoid.
  • Retraction: Posterior fibers of Temporalis.
  • Protrusion: Lateral and Medial Pterygoids acting together.
  • Depression (Open jaw): Initiated by gravity + lateral pterygoid (pulls condyle and articular disc forward onto the articular tubercle — translation in upper cavity). Further opening rotates the condyle (assisted by digastric, mylohyoid, geniohyoid).

Anatomical Basis of Jaw Dislocation

Mechanism: Wide opening of the mouth (e.g., yawning, laughing, or dental extraction) causes the lateral pterygoid to pull the mandibular condyle too far forward, causing it to slip anterior to the summit of the articular tubercle into the infratemporal fossa.

Clinical Signs: Locked open jaw (inability to close mouth), pain, spasm of masseter/temporalis, and drooling of saliva.

Reduction: Wrap thumbs in gauze, place on lower molar teeth, press downward and backward to overcome muscle spasm and push the condyle back into the mandibular fossa.

Cranial Nerves in Head & Neck

CN V — Trigeminal Nerve ⭐ PYQ 2015, 2018, 2022
Distribution of the Trigeminal Nerve (Gray's Anatomy)
Largest cranial nerve. Arises from pons. Sensory to face, scalp, dura. Motor to muscles of mastication (V3 only). Three divisions exit skull through distinct foramina.
Trigeminal Nerve — Three Divisions
V1 — OPHTHALMIC (sensory only) Exits: Superior Orbital Fissure
Branches: Lacrimal, Frontal (→ supraorbital + supratrochlear), Nasociliary
Sensory: Forehead, scalp to vertex, upper eyelid, cornea, nose (bridge), dura
Ganglion: Ciliary ganglion (parasympathetic to iris, ciliary body)
V2 — MAXILLARY (sensory only) Exits: Foramen Rotundum → pterygopalatine fossa
Branches: Infraorbital, Zygomatic, Posterior superior alveolar, Palatine, Nasal
Sensory: Cheek, lower eyelid, upper lip, upper teeth, palate, nasopharynx
Ganglion: Pterygopalatine ganglion (parasympathetic to lacrimal, nasal, palatine glands)
V3 — MANDIBULAR (sensory + MOTOR) Exits: Foramen Ovale
Sensory branches: Inferior alveolar (→ mental nerve), Lingual, Auriculotemporal, Buccal
Motor branches: Masseteric, deep temporal, nerve to pterygoids, nerve to mylohyoid
Sensory: Chin, lower lip, lower teeth, anterior 2/3 tongue (general), scalp above ear
Ganglion: Otic ganglion (parasympathetic to parotid via auriculotemporal nerve)
Trigeminal Neuralgia (Tic Douloureux)

Severe paroxysmal unilateral facial pain along V2 or V3 distribution, triggered by light touch (trigger zones). Cause: vascular compression of trigeminal root (often superior cerebellar artery). Treatment: carbamazepine (1st line), microvascular decompression (surgical). Never bilateral.

CN VII — Facial Nerve ⭐ PYQ 2013, 2016, 2019, 2023
Mixed nerve. Motor to muscles of facial expression, stapedius, stylohyoid, posterior belly digastric. Sensory: taste anterior 2/3 tongue. Parasympathetic: lacrimal, submandibular, sublingual, nasal glands. Exits internal acoustic meatus → facial canal → exits skull at stylomastoid foramen.

UMN vs LMN Facial Palsy

UMN (central) palsy: Forehead spared (bilateral cortical supply to forehead). Contralateral lower face weakness. Cause: stroke.

LMN palsy (Bell's palsy): ALL muscles of face paralysed on same side — forehead + lower face. Cannot close eye → corneal exposure → ulceration. Cause: idiopathic (HSV-1 reactivation suspected).

Ramsay Hunt Syndrome

VZV reactivation in geniculate ganglion → LMN facial palsy + vesicular eruption in EAM (Ramsay Hunt area) + ipsilateral ear pain + sensorineural hearing loss. Treat: acyclovir + corticosteroids. Worse prognosis than Bell's palsy.

CN XII — Hypoglossal Nerve PYQ 2017, 2021
  • Motor to all intrinsic + extrinsic muscles of tongue — except palatoglossus (supplied by CN X via pharyngeal plexus)
  • Exits skull through hypoglossal canal
  • Loops around occipital artery, deep to digastric, passes forward between ICA and IJV
  • Runs on hyoglossus muscle → enters tongue
  • C1 fibres join CN XII → leave as nerve to thyrohyoid + superior root of ansa
CN XII Palsy — Tongue Deviation

LMN lesion → tongue deviates to same (ipsilateral) side as lesion when protruded (paralysed genioglossus cannot push tongue to opposite side).

Features: wasting + fasciculations of tongue (LMN). Cause: skull base tumour, carotid endarterectomy complication, penetrating neck injury.

Thyroid Gland ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2015, 2018, 2021, 2023
Largest endocrine gland. Two lobes + isthmus (overlies 2nd–4th tracheal rings). Pyramidal lobe present in ~40% (remnant of thyroglossal duct, attached to isthmus, ascends to hyoid). Enclosed in pretracheal fascia (false capsule).

Blood Supply:

  • Superior thyroid artery (1st branch of ECA) → upper pole. Runs with external laryngeal nerve (lateral to it). Ligated close to gland → nerve preserved.
  • Inferior thyroid artery (thyrocervical trunk of subclavian) → lower and posterior surface. Crosses RLN (behind or in front).
  • Thyroidea ima (3% of people) → from brachiocephalic trunk or arch of aorta → isthmus
  • Venous: Superior (→ IJV), Middle (→ IJV), Inferior (→ brachiocephalic vein)

Key Relations:

  • Posterior: Trachea (medial), esophagus (medial behind), CCA (lateral), parathyroid glands (posterior surface)
  • Posterolateral: Carotid sheath, recurrent laryngeal nerve in tracheoesophageal groove
  • Anterior: Infrahyoid strap muscles, investing fascia
  • Isthmus: 2nd–4th tracheal rings anteriorly
Why does a Thyroid Swelling move with Deglutition? PYQ 2014-S, 2018, 2023

Anatomical Basis: The thyroid gland is enclosed in the pretracheal fascia (false capsule). Posteriorly, this fascia is thickened to form the suspensory ligament of Berry (lateral ligament of the thyroid), which firmly anchors the thyroid gland's lobes to the cricoid cartilage and the upper tracheal rings.

Mechanism of Movement: During deglutition (swallowing), the larynx and trachea are elevated by the action of the suprahyoid muscles and longitudinal pharyngeal muscles. Since the thyroid gland is bound to the larynx and trachea by the pretracheal fascia and Berry's ligament, any enlargement of the thyroid (goiter, adenoma, carcinoma) is pulled upwards along with the trachea during swallowing. This is a crucial clinical test to differentiate thyroid masses from other neck masses (like branchial cleft cysts or lipomas, which do not move with deglutition).

Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve (RLN) SURGICAL DANGER ⭐ PYQ Every Year

Left RLN:

  • Branches from left vagus at aortic arch level
  • Loops under arch of aorta (lateral to ligamentum arteriosum)
  • Ascends in tracheoesophageal groove
  • Enters larynx behind cricothyroid joint

Right RLN:

  • Branches from right vagus at subclavian artery level
  • Loops under subclavian artery
  • Shorter course in tracheoesophageal groove
  • Non-recurrent RLN (rare, right side only) — when right subclavian is retroesophageal

Function: Motor to all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid (external laryngeal nerve). Sensory below vocal folds.

Injury: Unilateral RLN palsy → hoarse voice (breathy, bovine). Bilateral → aphonia + respiratory obstruction (emergency). Most dangerous relation: where inferior thyroid artery crosses RLN (varies widely — behind, in front, or between its branches).

Embryological Hooking of Recurrent Laryngeal Nerves PYQ 2008, 2016, 2018, 2023

During embryogenesis, the recurrent laryngeal nerves originally supply the 6th pharyngeal arch. As the neck elongates, the heart and its associated aortic arch arteries descend into the thorax, dragging the nerves with them, which loop around the 6th arch arteries.

Asymmetrical Fate of 6th Arch & Nerves:

  • Left Side: The distal part of the left 6th aortic arch persists as the ductus arteriosus (ligamentum arteriosum in adults). The left RLN stays hooked under this derivative and the arch of the aorta, ascending from the thorax into the neck.
  • Right Side: The distal part of the right 6th arch and the 5th arch regress completely. This allows the right RLN to "move up" until it catches on the next persisting cranial vessel — the 4th aortic arch (which becomes the right subclavian artery). Thus, the right RLN loops around the subclavian artery in the root of the neck.
The external laryngeal nerve runs with the superior thyroid artery — injured if artery ligated far from thyroid. Causes loss of cricothyroid muscle → cannot tense vocal cord → voice fatigue, loss of high notes. "Opera singer's nerve."

Embryological Paradox of Parathyroid Migration PYQ 2014-S, 2017-S

The Paradox: The superior parathyroid glands develop from the 4th pharyngeal pouch, while the inferior parathyroid glands develop from the 3rd pharyngeal pouch. This seems counter-intuitive since the 3rd pouch is superior to the 4th pouch.

Anatomical Basis:

  • 3rd Pouch Derivatives (Inferior Parathyroid & Thymus): The 3rd pouch endoderm differentiates into the inferior parathyroid and the thymus. As the thymus develops, it migrates caudally and medially into the anterior mediastinum of the thorax, pulling the inferior parathyroid along with it. The inferior parathyroid eventually detaches from the thymus and settles at the lower pole of the thyroid. Due to this long migration, ectopic inferior parathyroids can be found anywhere along this path (e.g., inside the thymus, mediastinum).
  • 4th Pouch Derivatives (Superior Parathyroid & Ultimobranchial Body): The 4th pouch endoderm differentiates into the superior parathyroid. Since it is not attached to a highly migratory organ like the thymus, it descends only a short distance, fusing with the thyroid's posterior surface at its upper/middle third. Its position is therefore much more constant.
Hypoparathyroidism Post-Thyroidectomy

Accidental removal or devascularisation of parathyroid glands during total thyroidectomy → hypocalcaemia → tetany (Chvostek's sign — tapping facial nerve → ipsilateral facial twitch; Trousseau's sign — BP cuff inflated → carpal spasm). Treated with calcium + vitamin D supplements.

Larynx — Structure ⭐ PYQ 2013, 2017, 2020, 2023
Entrance to the larynx, viewed from behind (Gray's Anatomy)
Larynx = airway + phonation organ. Extends C3–C6. Skeleton of cartilages connected by membranes and ligaments. Opened by glottis (rima glottidis).

Cartilages (9 total):

  • Thyroid — largest. V-shaped. "Adam's apple" more prominent in males. Notch at superior border.
  • Cricoid — only complete ring. At C6. Signet ring shape (wide posteriorly).
  • Epiglottis — leaf-shaped, elastic fibrocartilage. Attached to thyroid cartilage.
  • Arytenoids (paired) — sit on cricoid. Vocal process (attached to vocal cord) + muscular process (intrinsic muscles).
  • Corniculate (paired) — on apex of arytenoids (Santorini)
  • Cuneiform (paired) — in aryepiglottic folds (Wrisberg)

Interior of Larynx:

  • Vestibule: from inlet to vestibular (false) folds
  • Vestibular folds (false cords): do not vibrate, no phonation — contain glands
  • Ventricle (saccule): lateral recess between false and true cords
  • Vocal folds (true cords): white, avascular. Vibrate for phonation. Vocal ligament = upper free edge of cricothyroid membrane.
  • Rima glottidis: space between vocal folds + arytenoids. Narrowest in adult. Subglottis narrowest in children (croup here).
  • Infraglottic cavity: below rima, continuous with trachea

Laryngeal Applied Physiology: Safety Muscle & Cadaveric Position ⭐ PYQ 2015, 2016, 2020, 2025
The posterior cricoarytenoid is the only abductor of the vocal cords, making it the safety muscle of the larynx. Complete laryngeal nerve injury results in the passive cadaveric position.

The "Safety Muscle" of the Larynx:

  • Posterior Cricoarytenoid (PCA): Supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve. It is the only abductor of the vocal cords (opens the rima glottidis).
  • Clinical Importance: Bilateral palsy of the PCA (due to bilateral RLN injury) causes the vocal cords to fall into the midline, closing the airway completely and leading to acute asphyxiation.

Cadaveric Position of Vocal Cords:

  • Definition: When both the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) and the superior laryngeal nerve (SLN) are completely paralyzed bilaterally.
  • Position: The vocal folds assume a passive position of rest — midway between abduction and adduction, leaving a narrow 1.5 to 2 mm gap. Phonation is impossible (aphonia) and the airway is partially compromised.

Laryngeal Watershed Line:

  • The true vocal folds act as a lymphatic and sensory watershed line.
  • Sensory Watershed:
    • Above vocal folds: Supplied by the internal laryngeal nerve (sensory branch of superior laryngeal, CN X).
    • Below vocal folds: Supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve (CN X).
  • Lymphatic Watershed:
    • Supraglottic region: Drains superiorly along superior laryngeal artery to upper deep cervical nodes.
    • Subglottic region: Drains inferiorly along inferior thyroid artery to pretracheal, paratracheal, and lower deep cervical nodes.
    • Glottic region (vocal folds): Virtually devoid of lymphatic vessels. Consequently, early vocal cord cancer does not metastasize and remains localized.

Nasal Septum & Little's Area (Epistaxis) ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2014, 2017, 2022, 2024
The nasal septum divides the nasal cavity into right and left halves. Its anteroinferior portion contains a highly vascular zone called Little's area, the main site of epistaxis (nosebleeds).

Anatomy & Composition:

  • Bony Part: Formed mainly by the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone (superiorly) and the vomer (posteriorly/inferiorly).
  • Cartilaginous Part: Formed by the septal cartilage.
  • Cutaneous Part: The lower mobile end of the septum, formed by the septal branch of the greater alar cartilage and skin.

Kiesselbach's Plexus (Little's Area):

A highly vascular site on the anteroinferior septum where four arteries anastomose (representing a critical ICA-ECA anastomosis):

  • Sphenopalatine artery (septal branch — from Maxillary/ECA)
  • Greater palatine artery (septal branch — from Maxillary/ECA)
  • Superior labial artery (septal branch — from Facial/ECA)
  • Anterior ethmoidal artery (septal branch — from Ophthalmic/ICA)
Epistaxis & Little's Area

Pathogenesis: Little's area is exposed to drying air currents and is highly vulnerable to fingernail trauma ("nose picking"), leading to nasal bleeding (epistaxis).

Management: Direct compression (pinching) of the soft nose, sitting upright and tilting forward (to prevent swallowing or aspirating blood). Refractory cases are managed with chemical cauterization (silver nitrate) or anterior nasal packing.

Palatine Tonsil & Waldeyer's Ring ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2016-S, 2019-S, 2022, 2023
The palatine tonsil is a mass of lymphoid tissue in the lateral wall of the oropharynx, located in the tonsillar sinus between the palatoglossal and palatopharyngeal arches.

Anatomical Relations & Tonsillar Bed:

  • Anterior Pillar: Palatoglossal fold (contains palatoglossus muscle).
  • Posterior Pillar: Palatopharyngeal fold (contains palatopharyngeus muscle).
  • Tonsillar Bed (Lateral Relation): Formed from medial to lateral by:
    1. Pharyngobasilar fascia.
    2. Superior pharyngeal constrictor and styloglossus muscles.
    3. Buccopharyngeal fascia.
    4. External palatine (paratonsillar) vein: The main source of primary hemorrhage during tonsillectomy.
    5. Glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) and facial artery (tonsillar branch — main supply).

Waldeyer's Tonsillar Ring:

A circular band of lymphoid tissue surrounding the pharynx, providing local immunological defense:

  • Pharyngeal tonsil (Adenoid): Located in the roof of the nasopharynx.
  • Tubal tonsils: Located around the auditory tube opening.
  • Palatine tonsils: Located in the oropharynx.
  • Lingual tonsil: Located on the posterior 1/3 of the tongue.
Referred Ear Pain in Tonsillitis

Mechanism: The palatine tonsil is innervated by the tonsillar branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX). CN IX also supplies sensory innervation to the middle ear cavity via the tympanic branch (Jacobson's nerve).

Clinical Sign: In tonsillitis or after a tonsillectomy, patients frequently complain of pain referred to the ear on the same side. This is referred pain mediated by CN IX.

Otology — Ear & Facial Canal

Tympanic Membrane & Myringotomy ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2013, 2017
The tympanic membrane (eardrum) separates the external auditory canal from the middle ear cavity. It is set obliquely at a 55-degree angle to the floor of the canal.

Structure & Histological Layers:

  • Outer Layer (Cuticular): Stratified squamous epithelium (ectodermal).
  • Middle Layer (Fibrous): Collagen fibers. Absent in pars flaccida.
  • Inner Layer (Mucous): Simple cuboidal epithelium (endodermal).
  • Pars Tensa: Large inferior part, tense, has well-developed fibrous layer.
  • Pars Flaccida (Shrapnell's): Small superior flaccid part, lacks fibrous layer.
  • Umbo: A central depression where the handle of the malleus is attached.

Nerve Supply:

  • External Surface: Supplied by the auriculotemporal nerve (CN V3) (anterior part) and the auricular branch of vagus (CN X — Arnold's nerve) (posterior part).
  • Internal Surface: Supplied by the tympanic branch of glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX — Jacobson's nerve).
Myringotomy — Surgical Safety Zone

Definition: Surgical incision of the tympanic membrane to relieve pressure and drain pus/fluid in acute otitis media, or to insert a grommet (ventilation tube).

Safety Zone: The incision is always made in the posteroinferior quadrant.

Anatomical Rationale: The posteroinferior quadrant is the safest because the chorda tympani nerve and the auditory ossicles (incus and stapes) lie superiorly, and the tympanic branch of CN IX lies anteriorly. This prevents injury to these crucial structures.

Middle Ear Cavity & Facial Canal Course ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2022, 2023
The middle ear (tympanic cavity) is an air-filled space in the petrous temporal bone. It contains three auditory ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) and two muscles (tensor tympani, stapedius).

Six Boundaries (Walls):

  • Roof (Tegmental Wall): Tegmen tympani (thin bone plate separating it from the temporal lobe in the middle cranial fossa).
  • Floor (Jugular Wall): Thin bone separating it from the internal jugular vein.
  • Anterior (Carotid Wall): Separates it from the internal carotid artery. Has openings for the auditory (Eustachian) tube.
  • Posterior (Mastoid Wall): Has the aditus ad antrum (opening to mastoid air cells) and the pyramid (houses the stapedius).
  • Lateral (Membranous Wall): Tympanic membrane.
  • Medial (Labyrinthine Wall): Promontory (cochlea basal turn), fenestra vestibuli (oval window — stapes footplate), and fenestra cochleae (round window).

Facial Nerve Intratemporal Course:

  • Enters the internal acoustic meatus, runs in the facial canal of petrous temporal bone.
  • Turns sharply backwards at the geniculate ganglion (geniculum).
  • Passes posteriorly in the medial wall of the middle ear (above the oval window).
  • Turns vertically downwards behind the posterior wall of the middle ear, exiting at the stylomastoid foramen.
  • Branches in Canal: Greater petrosal nerve (lacrimation), nerve to stapedius (paralysis causes hyperacusis), chorda tympani (taste to anterior 2/3 tongue).

Ophthalmology — Orbit & Eyeball

The Orbit & Extraocular Muscles ⭐ PYQ 2018-S, 2023
The orbit is a quadrangular bony cavity containing the eyeball, extraocular muscles, nerves, vessels, and the lacrimal apparatus.

Orbit Boundaries:

  • Roof: Orbital plate of frontal bone + lesser wing of sphenoid.
  • Floor: Orbital surface of maxilla + zygomatic + palatine bones.
  • Medial Wall (thinnest): Frontal process of maxilla + lacrimal bone + ethmoid (lamina papyracea) + body of sphenoid.
  • Lateral Wall (strongest): Zygomatic bone + greater wing of sphenoid.
  • Apertures: Optic canal (optic nerve, ophthalmic artery); Superior orbital fissure (CN III, IV, V1 branches, VI, superior ophthalmic vein).

Extraocular Muscles & Innervation:

  • 4 Recti: Superior, Inferior, Medial, Lateral.
  • 2 Obliques: Superior, Inferior.
  • Levator Palpebrae Superioris: Elevates upper eyelid.
  • Innervation Mnemonic: LR6SO4Rest3
    • LR6: Lateral Rectus supplied by CN VI (Abducens).
    • SO4: Superior Oblique supplied by CN IV (Trochlear).
    • Rest3: All other muscles (SR, IR, MR, IO, and LPS) supplied by CN III (Oculomotor).
The Eyeball & Aqueous Humor Circulation ⭐ PYQ 2011-S, 2022
The eyeball consists of three layers: outer fibrous coat (cornea and sclera), middle vascular pigmented coat (uvea), and inner nervous coat (retina).

Layers of the Cornea & Retina:

  • Five Layers of the Cornea:
    1. Anterior epithelium (non-keratinized stratified squamous)
    2. Bowman's membrane (anterior limiting lamina)
    3. Substantia propria (stroma — 90% thickness, collagen fibers)
    4. Descemet's membrane (posterior limiting lamina)
    5. Posterior endothelium (simple squamous — pumps fluid out to maintain transparency)
  • Retina: Contains 10 layers, including photoreceptors (rods and cones), bipolar cells, and ganglion cells whose axons form the optic nerve.

Aqueous Humor Circulation:

  • Production: Secreted by the ciliary epithelium of the ciliary processes into the **posterior chamber**.
  • Pathway: Passes from the posterior chamber, through the pupil, and into the **anterior chamber**.
  • Drainage: Drained at the iridocorneal angle through the trabecular meshwork into the canal of Schlemm (scleral venous sinus). From there, it enters the aqueous veins and episcleral veins.
  • Glaucoma: Blocked drainage raises intraocular pressure (IOP), damaging the optic nerve (blindness).
Lacrimal Apparatus PYQ 2012, 2023
The lacrimal apparatus produces, distributes, and drains lacrimal fluid (tears) to protect and lubricate the cornea and conjunctiva.

Lacrimal Gland:

  • Location: In the lacrimal fossa in the anterolateral part of the orbital roof.
  • Parts: Orbital and palpebral parts, separated by the aponeurosis of the levator palpebrae superioris.
  • Secretory Ducts: 10–12 ducts open into the conjunctival sac. Secretomotor supply is parasympathetic via CN VII.

Tear Drainage Pathway:

  • Tears cross the cornea medially and accumulate in the lacus lacrimalis.
  • Drain through the **lacrimal puncta** (minute openings on eyelid margins) into the **lacrimal canaliculi**.
  • Canaliculi join and enter the **lacrimal sac** (in the lacrimal groove).
  • The sac continues inferiorly as the **nasolacrimal duct**, which opens into the **inferior meatus of the nasal cavity** (opening is guarded by the valve of Hasner).

What is pterion? Why is it clinically important?PYQ 2013, 2020, 2024

Pterion is the H-shaped junction of four bones: frontal, parietal, squamous temporal, and greater wing of sphenoid. It is located 4 cm above the zygomatic arch midpoint.

  • It is the thinnest and weakest part of the skull vault
  • Deep to it runs the anterior branch of the middle meningeal artery
  • Trauma → fracture → arterial laceration → extradural haematoma (lenticular/biconvex on CT)
  • Classic story: lucid interval after head injury → rapid neurological deterioration
  • Surgical landmark: burr hole placed here for emergency decompression

Exam tip: pterion = 4 bones meeting. Asterion = 3 bones. Lambda = 3 sutures meeting (lambdoid + sagittal).

Describe the layers of the scalp. Which layer is the "dangerous layer" and why?PYQ 2012, 2016, 2021

Mnemonic: SCALP — Skin, dense Connective tissue, Aponeurosis, Loose areolar, Pericranium.

  • Dangerous layer = Loose areolar tissue (4th layer)
  • Contains emissary veins — connections between scalp veins and intracranial dural sinuses
  • Infection/blood spreads freely in this plane (no fibrous septa) in all directions
  • Limited by attachment of pericranium to sutures — infections cannot spread across sutures deep to pericranium
  • Blood from scalp injury gravitates here — "black eye" from frontal scalp haematoma tracks down under gravity
  • Scalp infections → emissary veins → intracranial dural sinuses → cavernous sinus thrombosis

What are the contents of the posterior triangle of the neck?PYQ 2015, 2019, 2023

Boundaries: SCM (ant), Trapezius (post), clavicle (inf). Floor: prevertebral muscles.

Contents:

  • CN XI (accessory nerve) — most important. Crosses obliquely from SCM → trapezius. Vulnerable to biopsy.
  • Cutaneous branches of cervical plexus from Erb's point (posterior border SCM): lesser occipital (C2), great auricular (C2,3), transverse cervical (C2,3), supraclavicular (C3,4)

What is ansa cervicalis? Give its formation, course and distribution.PYQ 2016, 2019

Ansa cervicalis is a nerve loop on the anterior wall of the carotid sheath.

  • Superior root: C1 fibres that descend with CN XII (hypoglossal nerve) — then leave it as the superior root
  • Inferior root: C2 and C3 fibres directly from cervical plexus
  • Both roots join to form the ansa loop on the anterior surface of the carotid sheath (overlying CCA/IJV)
  • Distribution: All infrahyoid (strap) muscles — sternohyoid, sternothyroid, omohyoid
  • Exception: Thyrohyoid — supplied by C1 fibres directly via hypoglossal nerve (before they separate)

Clinical: ansa cervicalis nerve transfer used to reinnervate paralysed vocal cord (CN XII–RLN anastomosis).

What are the branches of the facial nerve inside and outside the parotid gland?PYQ 2015, 2018, 2024

Facial nerve exits stylomastoid foramen → enters posterior surface of parotid → forms parotid plexus.

Branches WITHIN temporal bone (before stylomastoid):

  • Greater petrosal nerve → pterygopalatine ganglion → lacrimal + nasal glands
  • Nerve to stapedius (middle ear)
  • Chorda tympani → joins lingual nerve → taste ant 2/3 tongue + parasympathetic to submandibular/sublingual glands

Terminal branches (within/outside parotid) — "To Zanzibar By Motor Car":

  • Temporal → frontalis, orbicularis oculi (upper)
  • Zygomatic → orbicularis oculi (lower)
  • Buccal → buccinator, orbicularis oris
  • Marginal mandibular → depressors of lower lip + angle of mouth
  • Cervical → platysma

All terminal branches are purely MOTOR. Facial nerve entering parotid carries no fibres for the gland — parotid secretomotor is via auriculotemporal nerve (V3).

What nerves are at risk during thyroidectomy? How are they identified?PYQ 2018, 2021, 2023

Two nerves at risk:

  1. Recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN):
    • Runs in tracheoesophageal groove, crossed by inferior thyroid artery
    • Supplies all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid
    • Injury → unilateral: hoarseness; bilateral: aphonia + airway obstruction
    • Identified: at level of Berry's ligament (ligament of Gruber) where it enters larynx — lateral ligament of thyroid gland. Routine identification before ligation of inferior thyroid artery.
  2. External laryngeal nerve:
    • Runs with superior thyroid artery → near upper pole of thyroid
    • Supplies cricothyroid only → tenses vocal cord (pitch control)
    • Injury → monotonous voice, loss of high notes. "Operative injury to the superior laryngeal nerve."
    • Avoided by ligating superior thyroid artery close to gland (not high up)

Non-recurrent RLN (right side) — rare variant where right subclavian is retroesophageal → RLN takes direct course → mistaken for a vein and cut. Always look for this variant.

Describe the surgical anatomy of the parotid gland.PYQ 2012, 2015, 2021

Parotid gland — largest salivary gland, purely serous, located in parotid region.

Parotid bed:

  • Anteriorly: masseter, ramus of mandible, medial pterygoid
  • Posteriorly: mastoid process, SCM, posterior belly digastric
  • Medially: styloid apparatus (3 muscles + vessels = bouquet of Riolan), ICA, IJV, CN X, XI, XII

Structures within parotid (surgical importance):

  • CN VII (facial nerve) — divides gland into superficial and deep lobes. MOST IMPORTANT in parotidectomy. Identified at: (i) stylomastoid foramen, (ii) tragal pointer (cartilage of tragus points to it), (iii) tympanomastoid fissure, (iv) posterior belly of digastric
  • Retromandibular vein + ECA (maxillary + superficial temporal)
  • Parotid lymph nodes

Stensen's duct: 5 cm, opens opposite upper 2nd molar. Crossed superficially by facial nerve branches and transverse facial artery.

Frey's syndrome: gustatory sweating post-parotidectomy — auriculotemporal nerve regenerates to sweat glands instead of parotid.

EQ — Describe the triangles of the neck [PYQ 2013, 2017, 2020]
"Describe the triangles of the neck with their boundaries and contents."
  1. Division: SCM divides neck into Anterior Triangle (medial) and Posterior Triangle (lateral). Each is further subdivided by smaller muscles.
  2. Anterior Triangle — Boundaries: Medial: anterior midline. Lateral: anterior border of SCM. Superior: lower border of mandible. Contains all major vessels, strap muscles, thyroid gland, larynx, pharynx.
  3. Anterior Triangle Sub-divisions: (a) Submental — below chin, between digastrics. (b) Digastric (submandibular) — contains submandibular gland. (c) Carotid — carotid arteries, IJV, vagus, CN XII. (d) Muscular — strap muscles, thyroid, trachea.
  4. Posterior Triangle — Boundaries: Anterior: posterior border SCM. Posterior: anterior border trapezius. Inferior: middle 1/3 clavicle. Floor: prevertebral muscles. Roof: investing fascia.
  5. Posterior Triangle Contents: CN XI (crosses obliquely — most vulnerable). Cervical plexus (cutaneous branches from Erb's point). 3rd part subclavian artery + brachial plexus trunks (in omoclavicular triangle). EJV.
  6. Clinical Importance: CN XI injury in posterior triangle → trapezius paralysis → drooping shoulder. Carotid triangle access for carotid endarterectomy, carotid sinus nerve block.
EQ — Surgical anatomy of thyroid gland [PYQ 2011, 2015, 2021, 2023]
"Describe the surgical anatomy of the thyroid gland."
  1. Introduction: Thyroid is the largest endocrine gland. Two lobes connected by isthmus (overlying 2nd–4th tracheal rings). Enclosed in pretracheal fascia (false capsule). True capsule = fibrous, sends septa (trabeculae) into gland.
  2. Blood Supply: Superior thyroid artery (1st branch ECA) to upper pole. Inferior thyroid artery (thyrocervical trunk) to lower pole. Thyroidea ima (3%) from brachiocephalic/aorta to isthmus. Venous: superior + middle → IJV; inferior → brachiocephalic vein.
  3. Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve (RLN): Runs in tracheoesophageal groove, crossed by inferior thyroid artery (relation variable: artery may be anterior, posterior or between branches). Supplies all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid. Identified at Berry's ligament. Injury: unilateral = hoarseness; bilateral = aphonia + stridor.
  4. External Laryngeal Nerve: Runs with superior thyroid artery to reach cricothyroid muscle. Injured if artery ligated far from thyroid. Consequences: loss of pitch, voice fatigue.
  5. Parathyroid Glands: 4 glands (2 superior, 2 inferior) on posterior surface within fascial sheath. Removed accidentally during total thyroidectomy → hypoparathyroidism → hypocalcaemia → tetany.
  6. Isthmus Relations: Lies in front of 2nd–4th tracheal rings. Tracheostomy performed in this area — vessels must be identified and ligated. Thyroidea ima artery, if present, bleeds profusely.
EQ — Scalp anatomy and clinical importance [PYQ 2016, 2021]
"Describe the layers of the scalp. Add notes on its clinical significance."
  1. Layers (SCALP): Skin (hair-bearing, sebaceous glands, sites of sebaceous cysts) → dense Connective tissue (contains vessels and nerves, doesn't retract → profuse bleeding when cut) → Aponeurotic layer (epicranial aponeurosis connecting frontalis and occipitalis muscles) → Loose areolar tissue (dangerous layer) → Pericranium (periosteum, fuses at sutures).
  2. Dangerous Layer: Loose areolar layer contains emissary veins connecting scalp veins to dural sinuses. Infection spreads freely here but is blocked by sutures deep to pericranium. Infection can track to cavernous sinus thrombosis via ophthalmic veins.
  3. Blood Supply: Five pairs of arteries from periphery (supratrochlear, supraorbital, superficial temporal, posterior auricular, occipital). Arteries in dense connective tissue layer — walls are tethered and cannot retract → scalp wounds bleed profusely.
  4. Nerve Supply: Trigeminal (V1, V2, V3) for anterior and lateral. Greater occipital nerve (C2 dorsal ramus) for posterior — single most important nerve of scalp (occipital neuralgia).
  5. Surgical Relevance: Scalp tolerates large flaps (excellent blood supply, anastomoses). Subgaleal (subponeurotic) plane used for scalp flap elevation. Tension on scalp wound closure must be on aponeurosis, not skin.
