01

Anatomical Terms & Planes

Anatomy has its own language. Get these terms wrong in a viva and you'll lose marks fast — not because you don't know the anatomy, but because you said "up" instead of "superior." Learn these once, use them forever.
Anatomical Position + 3 Planes Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
Anatomical Position — The Baseline

Everything in anatomy is described relative to the standard anatomical position: body erect, face forward, feet together, arms by the sides, palms facing forward (supinated). Every directional term assumes this position — even if the body is lying down or upside down when you examine it.

The palms-forward rule catches everyone. In anatomical position, the radius is lateral and the ulna is medial. The moment someone crosses their arms or pronates, the bones cross — but anatomical descriptions stay the same regardless.

Directional Terms ⭐ Viva Favourite

Body Pairs

  • Superior / Inferior — toward head / toward feet
  • Anterior / Posterior — front / back (= ventral/dorsal)
  • Medial / Lateral — toward midline / away from midline
  • Superficial / Deep — toward surface / toward core
  • Ipsilateral / Contralateral — same side / opposite side

Limb-Specific Pairs

  • Proximal / Distal — nearer to / farther from the limb's root
  • Flexion / Extension — decreasing / increasing joint angle
  • Abduction / Adduction — away from / toward midline
  • Pronation / Supination — forearm rotation (palm down / up)
  • Inversion / Eversion — sole faces medially / laterally (foot)
Why Terms Matter Clinically

A fracture of the distal radius (Colles') vs proximal radius — entirely different mechanism, treatment, and nerve injury risk. Miscommunication between clinicians using "upper" vs "superior" wastes time. Surgeons are precise because anatomy demands precision.

Bone Tissue Types

Compact (Cortical) Bone

  • Organised into Haversian systems (osteons)
  • Central Haversian canal (vessels + nerves) surrounded by concentric lamellae
  • Canaliculi connect osteocytes to each other and to canal
  • Forms the outer shell of all bones; dominant in diaphysis
  • Volkmann's canals run perpendicularly, connect Haversian canals

Cancellous (Trabecular / Spongy) Bone

  • Lattice of bony trabeculae aligned along stress lines
  • No Haversian systems — no true osteons
  • Spaces filled with red/yellow marrow
  • Dominant in epiphyses, flat bones, vertebral bodies
  • First to be lost in osteoporosis

Ossification — How Bone Forms

Two ways to build a bone — either directly from mesenchyme (fast, for flat bones that need to protect brain quickly) or via a cartilage scaffold first (endochondral, for long bones that need to grow in length). The difference matters for how fractures heal and why growth plate injuries are serious.

Fontanelles — A Common Viva Trap

Anterior fontanelle (bregma): largest, diamond-shaped, closes by 18 months. Posterior fontanelle (lambda): small, triangular, closes by 6–8 weeks after birth. Bulging fontanelle = raised ICP (meningitis, hydrocephalus). Sunken = dehydration. Delayed closure = hypothyroidism, rickets, Down syndrome, osteogenesis imperfecta.

Clavicle — The Exception

The clavicle is the first bone to ossify (5th–6th week intrauterine) and the last to fuse its epiphysis (~25 years — medial end). It ossifies partly by intramembranous (shaft) and partly endochondral (ends). Also the most commonly fractured bone overall.

Cartilage — Three Types, One Key Rule

The key rule for cartilage: avascular, aneural, alymphatic. This is why cartilage heals so poorly — no blood supply means no inflammatory response, no clot formation, no organised repair. It also means cartilage pain is referred pain, not local pain.

Joints — Classification & Structure

Every joint question in anatomy comes down to: what type is it, what movements does it permit, which ligaments stabilise it, and what can go wrong. Build that framework here — apply it to every regional module later.
Fibrous
Held by dense fibrous tissue

No joint cavity. Movement minimal/absent. Subtypes: Suture (skull), Syndesmosis (inferior tibiofibular), Gomphosis (tooth in socket)

Cartilaginous
United by cartilage

No joint cavity. Primary (synchondrosis, hyaline) or Secondary (symphysis, fibrocartilage). Some movement possible in symphyses.

Synovial
Free-moving; has a joint cavity

Articular cartilage, joint cavity with synovial fluid, synovial membrane, fibrous capsule, ligaments. 6 subtypes by shape.

Joint Fluid Analysis — The Examiner's Favourite Viva

Normal synovial fluid: viscous, straw-coloured, WBC <200/mm³, glucose ≈ blood glucose. Septic arthritis: turbid, WBC >50,000 (neutrophil dominant), low glucose — surgical emergency (destroys cartilage within hours). Gout: monosodium urate crystals (needle-shaped, negatively birefringent). Pseudogout: calcium pyrophosphate crystals (rhomboid, positively birefringent).

Muscle Contraction — Step by Step
Step 1 — Action Potential Motor neuron AP → reaches neuromuscular junction → ACh released → binds nAChR → muscle AP generated → spreads along sarcolemma → travels down T-tubules
↓
Step 2 — Ca²⁺ Release T-tubule AP → triggers ryanodine receptor (RyR1) in SR terminal cisternae → Ca²⁺ floods into cytoplasm from SR Resting Ca²⁺ ≈ 0.1 µM → rises to ~10 µM during contraction
↓
Step 3 — Troponin Activation Ca²⁺ binds troponin C (TnC) → conformational change in troponin complex → tropomyosin shifts off actin's myosin-binding site → active sites on actin exposed
↓
Step 4 — Cross-Bridge Cycling Myosin head (already has ADP + Pi) binds actin → power stroke (Pi released → ADP released → myosin pulls actin toward M line) → ATP binds myosin → detaches from actin → ATP hydrolysed → myosin re-cocks (returns to high-energy position)
↓
Step 5 — Relaxation Motor neuron stops firing → ACh degraded by AChE → AP stops → Ca²⁺ pumped back into SR (SERCA pump, ATP-dependent) → troponin-tropomyosin blocks actin → cross-bridges detach → muscle lengthens
The A band does NOT change length during contraction — this is the most common exam mistake. The A band = myosin filament length = constant. What changes: I bands (narrow), H zone (narrows/disappears), sarcomere length (shortens). Bands A stays the Alpha (first, unchanged).
What Changes in Contraction
"I Have Shortened" → I band (narrows) · H zone (narrows) · Sarcomere (shortens)

A band stays constant. Always. No exceptions. If an MCQ says "A band shortens" — it's wrong.

Fascia & Bursae

Fascia is one of those topics that gets a paragraph in most notes and an entire question in the exam. The deep fascia is a surgical landmark — it separates compartments, carries nerves and vessels, and determines how infections spread. Bursae are pockets of synovial fluid that reduce friction — they inflame, they fill with blood, and they communicate with joints in ways that can mislead you clinically.

Bursae PYQ 2017, 2022

A bursa is a closed sac of synovial membrane containing a thin film of synovial fluid. It exists wherever a structure (tendon, ligament, skin, muscle) would otherwise rub against bone or another structure during movement.

Types of Bursae

  • True bursae: Present from birth, constant, always synovial-lined
  • Adventitious bursae: Form in response to abnormal friction/pressure (e.g., bunion over 1st MTP, olecranon bursa in miners)
  • Bursae communicating with joints: Suprapatellar bursa ↔ knee joint; subscapular bursa ↔ shoulder

Clinical — Bursitis

  • Prepatellar bursitis (housemaid's knee) — repeated kneeling. Bursa between skin and patella
  • Infrapatellar bursitis (clergyman's knee) — more distal, between patellar ligament and tibia
  • Subacromial bursitis — painful arc syndrome; shoulder abduction 60–120° painful
  • Olecranon bursitis — student's elbow (leaning on desk); gout common here

Nerve Injury Classification — Seddon's & Sunderland's

Neuropraxia (Sunderland 1): Myelin damaged, axon intact. Conduction block only. Full recovery. Example: "Saturday night palsy" (radial nerve compressed against humerus).

Axonotmesis (Sunderland 2–4): Axon disrupted but connective tissue sheaths intact. Wallerian degeneration distal to injury. Regeneration possible (1mm/day). Example: fracture-related nerve injury.

Neurotmesis (Sunderland 5): Complete nerve division. No spontaneous recovery. Surgical repair needed. Example: knife laceration.

Viva Q&A

Planes, Bones & Ossification
What is the difference between the median plane and a sagittal plane?Viva Classic

The median plane is a single specific sagittal plane that passes exactly through the midline of the body, dividing it into equal left and right halves. A sagittal plane is any vertical plane parallel to the median plane — there are infinite sagittal planes, but only one median plane. In clinical practice, "midsagittal" = median. Any parasagittal plane runs parallel to the median but does not bisect the body symmetrically.

Examiner follow-up: "What movement occurs in the sagittal plane?" → Flexion and extension (about a transverse axis).

Why is the metaphysis the most common site for osteosarcoma and osteomyelitis?PYQ 2019, 2023

The metaphysis has a rich capillary sinusoidal blood supply with sluggish flow — blood velocity is low because capillaries are wide and tortuous near the growth plate. This creates two conditions:

  • Osteomyelitis: Sluggish blood → bacteria settle and multiply easily. Phagocytes are less effective here. Most common in the distal femur and proximal tibia metaphyses in children.
  • Osteosarcoma: The rapidly dividing osteoblasts near the growth plate are genetically vulnerable. Tumours arising here are the aggressive high-grade type that affects adolescents during their growth spurt. Distal femur metaphysis is the single most common site.

Memory hook: Metaphysis = "Metaphor for trouble" — rich supply + slow flow = ideal breeding ground.

What bones ossify by intramembranous ossification and why does it matter?PYQ 2014, 2022

Bones forming by intramembranous ossification: flat bones of the skull vault (frontal, parietal, squamous occipital, squamous temporal), mandible, and the shaft of the clavicle. These have no cartilage template — mesenchymal cells directly differentiate into osteoblasts.

Why it matters clinically:

  • Fontanelles exist because these bones start as islands and haven't yet fused — allows brain growth and birth canal moulding
  • Skull fractures heal differently — periosteum regenerates without cartilage intermediate
  • Fibrous dysplasia and Paget's disease preferentially affect these bones
Cartilage, Joints & Muscles
Why does articular cartilage not heal well after injury?Viva Favourite

Three reasons work together:

  • Avascular: No blood vessels → no inflammatory response → no clot formation → no organised repair cascade. Nutrients come only from synovial fluid.
  • No perichondrium: Articular cartilage has no perichondrium (unlike costal or nasal cartilage) → no reservoir of chondroprogenitor cells to mount a repair
  • Low cell turnover: Chondrocytes are post-mitotic (divide very slowly) → minimal capacity for cell replacement

Superficial lacerations that don't penetrate subchondral bone never heal. Full-thickness defects that bleed from bone may fill with fibrocartilage (inferior to hyaline) via the inflammatory response from bone marrow cells — this is the basis of microfracture surgery.

What is the difference between a ligament and a tendon?1st Year Viva

Both are dense regular fibrous connective tissue made of type I collagen with fibroblasts between fibres.

  • Tendon: Connects muscle to bone. Transmits the force of muscle contraction to produce movement. Fibres run strictly parallel (maximises tensile strength in one direction). Has a paratenon or synovial sheath where it bends around pulleys.
  • Ligament: Connects bone to bone. Stabilises joints. Fibres run in multiple directions (joints are loaded in multiple planes). Contains more elastin than tendons (some stretch needed).

Key difference examiners test: Tendons are more metabolically active, heal better (have some vascularity). Ligaments have poorer blood supply → slower healing → why ACL reconstruction often uses tendon grafts rather than waiting for ligament to heal.

What is rigor mortis and what is its biochemical basis?PYQ 2015, 2020

Rigor mortis is post-mortem stiffening of muscles due to sustained cross-bridge formation without relaxation.

Mechanism:

  • After death, ATP production ceases (no mitochondrial activity)
  • Without ATP, myosin cannot detach from actin (ATP is required for cross-bridge detachment, not formation)
  • Simultaneously, Ca²⁺ leaks from SR → troponin activated → cross-bridges form
  • Result: Muscles locked in semi-contracted, rigid state

Timeline: Onset 2–6 hours post-mortem (as ATP depletes). Full rigor 12 hours. Resolves 24–48 hours (as muscle proteins begin to decompose).

Used in forensic medicine: Time since death estimation. Warm body + no rigor = <2h. Full rigor = ~12h. Rigor resolved = >48h.

Classify bursae and give two examples of clinically important bursae around the knee.PYQ 2017, 2022

Classification:

  • True (constant) bursae: Present from development, always synovially lined, in predictable locations
  • Adventitious bursae: Form in response to abnormal pressure/friction over bony prominences
  • Subcutaneous: Between skin and underlying structure (prepatellar)
  • Submuscular/subfascial/subtendinous: Deeper layers

Important knee bursae:

  • Suprapatellar bursa: Between femur and quadriceps tendon, above patella. Communicates with the knee joint cavity — so joint effusions fill it first and it can be aspirated here. Palpable above patella when distended.
  • Prepatellar bursa: Between anterior surface of patella and overlying skin. Does NOT communicate with joint. Inflamed by kneeling → prepatellar bursitis (housemaid's knee).

Essay Question Templates

EQ — Classify Joints. Describe the Structure of a Synovial Joint with Examples PYQ 2016, 2019, 2023
"Classify joints. Describe the general structure of a synovial joint and give the classification of synovial joints with examples."
  1. Classification of joints: Three structural classes — Fibrous (suture, syndesmosis, gomphosis), Cartilaginous (primary synchondrosis, secondary symphysis), Synovial (6 subtypes). Functionally classified as synarthrosis (immovable), amphiarthrosis (slightly movable), diarthrosis (freely movable).
  2. General structure of a synovial joint: Joint cavity present (distinguishing feature). Articular surfaces covered by hyaline cartilage (no perichondrium). Fibrous capsule encloses joint; inner layer = synovial membrane. Synovial fluid produced by synoviocytes — lubricates and nourishes. Ligaments (intrinsic or extrinsic) stabilise. Some joints have articular discs (fibrocartilage — knee menisci, TMJ disc, sternoclavicular disc).
  3. Synovial fluid composition: Ultrafiltrate of plasma + hyaluronic acid (gives viscosity). Normal: straw-coloured, viscous, WBC <200/mm³. Changed in infection, gout, pseudogout (crystal analysis key).
  4. Classification of synovial joints: Plane (intercarpal), Hinge (elbow), Pivot (atlantoaxial), Condyloid (wrist), Saddle (1st CMC), Ball-and-socket (hip, shoulder). Mnemonic: "Please Help Prehistoric Creatures Survive Brutally."
  5. Clinical significance: Synovial joints are susceptible to arthritis (osteo and rheumatoid), septic arthritis, haemarthrosis (knee in haemophilia), crystal deposition (gout, pseudogout). Joint aspiration is diagnostic and therapeutic.
EQ — Sliding Filament Theory of Muscle Contraction PYQ 2013, 2018, 2021
"Describe the sliding filament theory of muscle contraction. What changes occur in the sarcomere bands during contraction?"
  1. Structural basis — Sarcomere: Functional unit of striated muscle. From Z line to Z line. Contains thick filaments (myosin, in A band), thin filaments (actin + troponin + tropomyosin, spanning I band into A band). I band = only thin filaments. H zone = only thick filaments. M line = mid-sarcomere, anchors myosin.
  2. Molecular players: Myosin head has ATPase activity + actin-binding site. Actin has exposed binding sites masked by tropomyosin at rest. Troponin complex (TnC binds Ca²⁺, TnI inhibitory, TnT tropomyosin-binding).
  3. Contraction sequence: AP → T-tubule → Ca²⁺ release from SR → Ca²⁺ binds TnC → tropomyosin shifts → actin binding sites exposed → myosin cross-bridge attaches → power stroke → ATP binds → detachment → ATP hydrolysis → re-cocking → cycle repeats.
  4. Band changes during contraction: A band — NO CHANGE (myosin filament length constant). I bands — narrow (actin slides into A band). H zone — narrows then disappears (thin filaments reach M line). Sarcomere shortens. Z lines move closer together.
  5. Energy source: ATP required at two steps — (1) cross-bridge detachment (myosin-ADP-Pi → binds actin → releases Pi then ADP during power stroke → then ATP binds to detach), (2) Ca²⁺ re-uptake into SR by SERCA pump for relaxation. Sustained contraction without ATP = rigor.
  6. Clinical application: Rigor mortis (ATP depletion post-mortem). Malignant hyperthermia (uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release from SR due to RyR1 mutation → uncontrolled contraction → hyperthermia). Myasthenia gravis (AChR antibodies → impaired AP initiation → weakness despite intact sarcomere machinery).
EQ — Classify Cartilage with Examples and Clinical Applications PYQ 2015, 2020
"Classify cartilage. Give the differences between hyaline, elastic, and fibrocartilage. Add a note on the poor healing of articular cartilage."
  1. General properties: Avascular, aneural, alymphatic. Chondrocytes in lacunae. Matrix: chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans + water (70–80%) + collagen. Perichondrium present in most types (not articular cartilage). Growth: appositional (from perichondrium) + interstitial (chondrocyte division within matrix).
  2. Hyaline cartilage: Type II collagen (fibres invisible). Glassy, pearly blue-white. Sites: articular surfaces, growth plates, costal cartilages, respiratory tract (trachea, bronchi), nose, larynx (most). Tends to calcify with age. Weakest of three types.
  3. Elastic cartilage: Type II collagen + abundant elastic fibres (visible in H&E). Flexible, returns to shape. Does NOT calcify. Sites: pinna, epiglottis, cuneiform/corniculate laryngeal cartilages, Eustachian tube. Mnemonic: All begin with "E".
  4. Fibrocartilage: Type I collagen (thick, visible bundles). No perichondrium. Strongest, resists compression and tension. Sites: intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis, menisci of knee, articular discs of TMJ and sternoclavicular joints, labra of hip and shoulder.
  5. Poor healing of articular cartilage: Three reasons — (1) Avascular: no inflammatory response, no clot, no vascular ingrowth. (2) No perichondrium: no chondroprogenitor cell source. (3) Chondrocytes are post-mitotic: minimal regenerative capacity. Superficial injuries never heal. Full-thickness defects may fill with inferior fibrocartilage via subchondral bone bleeding (basis of microfracture surgery and marrow stimulation techniques).
