Development Timeline Overview

Embryology is divided into two major phases: Embryonic period (Weeks 1–8, organogenesis) and Fetal period (Weeks 9–38, growth and maturation). Teratogens are most dangerous during the embryonic period.
Week-by-Week Milestones (Carnegie Stages)

Days 1–3: Cleavage → Morula (16 cells, ~Day 3)
Days 4–5: Blastocyst formation (ICM + trophoblast + blastocoel)
Day 6–7: Blastocyst hatches from zona pellucida → begins implantation
↓ Blastocyst invades endometrium

02

Gametogenesis

Spermatogenesis PYQ 2014, 2019

Location & Timeline:

  • Site: Seminiferous tubules of testis
  • Begins at puberty, continues throughout life
  • Duration: ~64 days (spermatogonium → spermatozoon)
  • Temperature: requires 2–3°C below body temperature (cryptorchidism = infertility)
  • Hormonal control: FSH → Sertoli cells; LH → Leydig cells → testosterone

Stages (Mitosis → Meiosis → Spermiogenesis):

  • Spermatogonium (2n) → mitosis → primary spermatocyte (2n)
  • Primary spermatocyte → Meiosis I → 2 secondary spermatocytes (n)
  • Secondary spermatocytes → Meiosis II → 4 spermatids (n)
  • Spermiogenesis: Spermatid → Spermatozoon (no cell division — morphological transformation)
  • Sertoli cells: blood-testis barrier, nurse cells, secrete inhibin + ABP
Oogenesis — Arrested at Two Points ⭐ PYQ 2015, 2021
Oogenesis — Timeline & Arrest Points
Fetal Life (2nd month onward) Oogonia (2n) divide mitotically → Primary oocytes (2n) enter Meiosis I but ARREST at Prophase I (Diplotene stage) — remain arrested until puberty All primary oocytes are formed before birth (~700,000 at birth, ~400,000 at puberty)
↓ ARREST 1 — Diplotene stage, maintained by Meiosis-Inhibiting Factor (MIF) from granulosa cells
Puberty → each cycle: LH surge triggers resumption Primary oocyte completes Meiosis I just before ovulation → 2 cells:
Secondary oocyte (n) — large, gets most cytoplasm
First polar body (n) — small, discarded
Secondary oocyte enters Meiosis II but ARRESTS at Metaphase II
↓ ARREST 2 — Metaphase II, until fertilization
Fertilization triggers completion of Meiosis II Secondary oocyte → Mature ovum (n) + Second polar body (n)
If not fertilized: secondary oocyte degenerates without completing Meiosis II
Exam trap: The structure released at ovulation is the SECONDARY oocyte, NOT the ovum. The mature ovum only forms after fertilization completes Meiosis II.

03

Fertilization

Fertilization = fusion of secondary oocyte with spermatozoon. Site: Ampulla of uterine tube (most common). Window: secondary oocyte viable ~24 hours post-ovulation; spermatozoa viable 2–3 days in female tract.
Steps of Fertilization — Sequential Events
Step 1 — Capacitation Sperm undergo capacitation in female genital tract (~7 hours): cholesterol removed from plasma membrane → hypermotility (whiplash movement) → ability to undergo acrosome reaction
↓ Sperm reaches corona radiata of secondary oocyte
Step 2 — Acrosome Reaction (triggered by contact with zona pellucida) ZP3 (zona pellucida glycoprotein) binds sperm receptor → Ca²⁺ influx → Acrosome reaction: fusion of acrosomal membrane with plasma membrane → release of hyaluronidase + acrosin → penetration of zona pellucida
↓ Sperm penetrates zona pellucida
Step 3 — Cortical Reaction (prevents polyspermy) Sperm head fuses with oocyte membrane → Ca²⁺ wave → Cortical granules release from oocyte cortex → zona reaction: ZP2 and ZP3 modified → zona pellucida hardens → prevents entry of additional sperm This is the primary block to polyspermy in humans
↓ Secondary oocyte resumes and completes Meiosis II
Step 4 — Completion of Meiosis II Secondary oocyte → mature ovum + 2nd polar body
Pronuclei form: female pronucleus (from ovum) + male pronucleus (from sperm head)
Each pronucleus = haploid (23 chromosomes)
↓ Syngamy — pronuclei fuse → zygote (2n, 46 chromosomes)
Step 5 — Zygote Formation Diploid number restored (46, XX or 46, XY)
Sex determined at fertilization: Y-bearing sperm → male (SRY gene on Y chromosome)
Species variation: human zygote begins first cleavage division within ~30 hours
Significance of Fertilization PYQ 2016, 2022
  • Restores diploid number (2n = 46) — species continuity
  • Determines chromosomal sex — XX (female) or XY (male)
  • Initiates cleavage — zygote begins mitotic division
  • Completes Meiosis II of secondary oocyte
  • Increases metabolic activity of oocyte
  • Determines bilateral symmetry — sperm entry point marks future dorsoventral axis
Ectopic Pregnancy

Implantation outside uterine cavity. Most common site: Ampulla of uterine tube (75–80%). Other sites (decreasing frequency): isthmus, fimbrial end, cornual/interstitial, ovarian, abdominal, cervical. Risk: PID (adhesions), previous ectopic, IUD, tubal surgery. Presents with amenorrhoea → lower abdominal pain → vaginal bleeding. Risk of rupture → haemoperitoneum (emergency). Dx: transvaginal USG + serum β-hCG (lower than expected for gestational age).

Cleavage, Morula & Blastocyst

Cleavage = rapid mitotic division of zygote WITHOUT growth. Cells become smaller with each division (blastomeres). Zona pellucida maintained throughout cleavage until blastocyst hatches.
Cleavage to Blastocyst — Days 1–6
Day 1–2 — Early Cleavage (in uterine tube, moving toward uterus) Zygote (1 cell) → 2 blastomeres → 4 → 8 blastomeres
At 8-cell stage: Compaction — blastomeres flatten, maximize contact, gap junctions form
↓ Continued divisions → 16 cells
Day 3 — Morula (16 cells, solid ball) Enters uterine cavity
Outer cells: future trophoblast
Inner cells: future inner cell mass (ICM) / embryoblast
↓ Fluid accumulates → blastocoel forms
Day 4–5 — Blastocyst Fluid enters forming blastocoel (fluid-filled cavity)
Inner Cell Mass (ICM / embryoblast): at one pole → forms embryo proper + amnion + yolk sac + allantois
Trophoblast: outer cell layer → forms placenta + chorion + NO part of embryo Embryonic pole = pole with ICM; Abembryonic pole = opposite
↓ Zona pellucida thins and ruptures (~Day 5–6)
Day 6 — Hatching of Blastocyst Blastocyst hatches from zona pellucida → exposes trophoblast → blastocyst ready for implantation
Zona pellucida prevents implantation in tube — hatching is essential for normal uterine implantation
Mnemonic — Blastocyst Layers
"Trophoblast → Trophe (Greek = nourish) → nourishes = placenta. Inner Cell Mass → embryo Mass."

Trophoblast = "nurturing" outer layer → placenta. ICM = "inner core" → baby.

05

Implantation & Decidua

Implantation = embedding of blastocyst into endometrium. Begins Day 6–7, complete by Day 10–12. Normal site: posterior wall of body of uterus, superior part. Requires: progesterone, LIF (leukemia inhibitory factor), integrins.
Stages of Implantation PYQ 2013, 2018-S
  1. Apposition — Loose contact of blastocyst (embryonic pole) with endometrial epithelium (Day 6)
  2. Adhesion — Firm attachment of trophoblast to endometrium via cell adhesion molecules (L-selectin, integrins)
  3. Penetration (Invasion) — Syncytiotrophoblast (proteolytic) erodes endometrial epithelium → blastocyst sinks below surface → Day 10–12 endometrium grows over blastocyst
hCG — Rescue of Corpus Luteum

Syncytiotrophoblast secretes hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) from Day 8 onwards. hCG rescues corpus luteum → continued progesterone production → maintains endometrium (prevents menstruation). Basis of pregnancy tests (urine/serum hCG). Peaks at 10 weeks, then placenta takes over progesterone production (placental shift = Luteo-placental shift at 8–10 weeks).

Trophoblast Differentiation PYQ 2020

Cytotrophoblast (CyT):

  • Inner layer — mononucleate cells
  • Germinative — divides mitotically
  • Gives rise to syncytiotrophoblast
  • Forms Langhans cells (visible in first trimester villi)

Syncytiotrophoblast (SyT):

  • Outer layer — multinucleate (no cell boundaries)
  • Invasive — erodes maternal tissue
  • Secretes hCG, hCS (human chorionic somatomammotropin), progesterone, estrogen
  • Does NOT divide — grows by fusion of CyT cells
  • Forms the placental barrier with fetal capillaries
06

2nd Week — Bilaminar Germ Disc

Week 2 = "Week of Twos": 2 layers in embryonic disc (epiblast + hypoblast), 2 trophoblast layers (CyT + SyT), 2 cavities form (amniotic + yolk sac), 2 layers of mesoderm... everything comes in pairs.
Events of 2nd Week — Bilaminar Disc Formation PYQ 2018

ICM Differentiates into 2 layers:

  • Epiblast (upper/dorsal, columnar) — adjacent to amniotic cavity. Will form the embryo proper and give rise to all 3 germ layers during gastrulation
  • Hypoblast (lower/ventral, cuboidal) — adjacent to blastocoel. Forms primary yolk sac roof and extraembryonic endoderm
  • Together: Bilaminar germ disc (embryonic disc)

Cavities Forming:

  • Amniotic cavity (Day 8): forms between epiblast and cytotrophoblast. Lined by amnioblasts (from epiblast). Initially small.
  • Primary yolk sac: blastocoel lined by Heuser's membrane (hypoblast-derived). Later replaced by secondary yolk sac.
  • Secondary yolk sac (Day 13): formed as primary yolk sac pinches off → smaller, definitive yolk sac
  • Extraembryonic coelom (chorionic cavity): space between extraembryonic mesoderm layers
Prochordal Plate (Day 14)

At the cranial end of the bilaminar disc, hypoblast cells are columnar and firmly adherent to epiblast — this thickening is the prochordal plate. Marks the future cranial end of the embryo and the site of the future buccopharyngeal (oropharyngeal) membrane. Used as a landmark — tells you "this end is the head." Cannot be invaded by the primitive streak — defines the rostral limit of mesoderm.

07

3rd Week — Gastrulation & Trilaminar Disc

Gastrulation = most critical event in embryology. Converts bilaminar disc into trilaminar disc. Begins at Day 15. Establishes all 3 primary germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm. Also establishes body axes (cranio-caudal, dorso-ventral, left-right).
Gastrulation — Step by Step
Day 15 — Primitive Streak Appears Epiblast cells at caudal midline proliferate → thickening = Primitive streak
Cranial end of streak: Primitive node (Hensen's node) — organiser region
Defines cranio-caudal axis and bilateral symmetry Any remnant of primitive streak = Sacrococcygeal teratoma (most common neonatal tumour)
↓ Epiblast cells migrate inward through primitive streak
Endoderm Formation First wave: Epiblast cells invaginate → displace hypoblast → form definitive Endoderm (lining of gut, respiratory tract, etc.)
↓ Second wave of epiblast cells
Mesoderm Formation Second wave: Epiblast cells migrate laterally between epiblast and new endoderm → form Intraembryonic Mesoderm
Mesoderm does NOT extend into: prochordal plate region (future mouth) and cloacal membrane region (future anus)
↓ Remaining epiblast = surface ectoderm
Ectoderm = Remaining Epiblast Epiblast cells that do NOT migrate through primitive streak → become surface Ectoderm
Trilaminar disc now established: Ectoderm (dorsal) + Mesoderm (middle) + Endoderm (ventral)
↓ Notochord formation from Hensen's node
Notochord — The Embryonic Organiser Cells from Hensen's node proliferate cranially → form Notochord (midline rod of mesoderm)
Function: induces overlying ectoderm to become Neural plate (primary induction / neurulation)
Also defines the axial skeleton positionally
Fate: persists as Nucleus pulposus (centre of intervertebral discs) in adults Notochord remnants elsewhere → Chordoma (rare tumour, most common at clivus/sacrococcygeal region)
Neurulation — Neural Tube Formation ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2019, 2024
Human embryo 2mm long (Gray's Anatomy)

Steps (Week 3–4):

  • Notochord induces overlying ectoderm → Neural plate (Week 3)
  • Neural plate folds: lateral edges elevate → Neural folds
  • Neural folds meet at midline → fuse → Neural tube
  • Fusion begins in cervical region → extends rostrally and caudally (like a zipper)
  • Rostral neuropore closes: Day 25
  • Caudal neuropore closes: Day 27

Neural Crest Cells (NCCs):

  • Arise from lateral edges of neural plate where folds meet
  • Migrate throughout embryo
  • Derivatives: DRG neurons, sympathetic ganglia, Schwann cells, melanocytes, adrenal medulla, craniofacial bones/cartilage, cardiac septum, thyroid C-cells, enteric neurons
  • Called "4th germ layer" due to extensive derivatives

Intra-embryonic Coelom & Derivatives PYQ 2023

The Intra-embryonic Coelom is a horseshoe-shaped cavity that forms in the lateral plate mesoderm during the 3rd week, splitting it into somatic (parietal) and splanchnic (visceral) layers.

Formation & Structure:

  • Appearance: Small spaces appear in the lateral plate mesoderm. These spaces coalesce to form a single horseshoe-shaped cavity.
  • Cranial Curve: Lies at the rostral end of the embryo (future head region, anterior to the oropharyngeal membrane).
  • Lateral Limbs: Extend caudally on either side of the neural tube and communicate with the extra-embryonic coelom.
Adult Derivatives

The horseshoe-shaped cavity is partitioned to form three major body cavities:

  • Pericardial Cavity: Derived from the central/cranial curve of the horseshoe.
  • Pleural Cavity (left & right): Derived from the pericardioperitoneal canals (limbs of the horseshoe).
  • Peritoneal Cavity: Derived from the distal portions of the lateral limbs.
Folding of the Embryo ⭐ PYQ 2024

During the 4th week, rapid growth of the embryonic disc (particularly the neural tube) causes the embryo to fold in both cephalocaudal (longitudinal) and lateral (transverse) directions, converting it from a flat disc into a 3D cylinder.

Plane of FoldingMechanism & Key MovementsKey Anatomical Results
Longitudinal (Cephalocaudal) Driven by rapid elongation of the neural tube.
• Head Fold: Swings the neural plate, cardiogenic area, and septum transversum ventrally.
• Tail Fold: Swings the cloacal membrane and connecting stalk ventrally. 		1. Moves the heart, septum transversum, and future mouth (oropharyngeal membrane) into their anatomical thoracic positions.
2. Incorporates part of the yolk sac as the Foregut and Hindgut.
Transverse (Lateral) Driven by the rapid growth of somites, pushing the lateral margins of the disc ventrally to meet in the midline. 1. Closes the anterior body wall (except at the umbilical ring).
2. Incorporates the dorsal part of the yolk sac as the Midgut, suspended by the dorsal mesentery.
3. Encloses the intra-embryonic coelom within the body.

Derivatives of Germ Layers

Germ layer derivatives = THE most frequently tested topic in embryology. Know which layer gives what. Rule: Ectoderm = skin + nervous system. Mesoderm = "middle stuff" (muscle, bone, blood, kidneys). Endoderm = gut lining + glands.
Ectoderm Derivatives ⭐ PYQ EVERY YEAR

Surface Ectoderm:

  • Epidermis, hair, nails, sebaceous glands, sweat glands, mammary glands
  • Enamel of teeth
  • Lens + corneal epithelium of eye
  • Internal ear epithelium (otic placode → cochlea, semicircular canals)
  • Adenohypophysis (Rathke's pouch — oral ectoderm)
  • Lower 2/3 of anal canal epithelium (ectodermal cloaca)
  • External auditory meatus epithelium

Neuroectoderm (Neural Tube + Neural Crest):

  • Neural tube: brain, spinal cord, retina (neural retina + pigment layer), posterior pituitary, pineal gland
  • Neural crest: DRG, autonomic ganglia, Schwann cells, meninges (pia + arachnoid), melanocytes, adrenal medulla (chromaffin cells), C-cells of thyroid, enteric nervous system, craniofacial bones/cartilage, cardiac neural crest → conotruncal septum
  • Chromaffin cells → phaeochromocytoma if tumour

Master Mnemonic — Germ Layers
"Ecto = Extremes (outside skin + inner nervous system). Meso = Middle (muscle, marrow, mesothelium). Endo = Entrails (gut lining, glands, airways)."

Placenta — Structure & Development ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2014, 2017, 2020, 2023

Macroscopic Features (Term Placenta):

  • Discoid shape, ~22 cm diameter, ~2.5 cm thick, ~500 g weight
  • Fetal surface: smooth, covered by amnion, umbilical cord inserts centrally (eccentric/marginal/velamentous variants)
  • Maternal surface: rough, lobulated — 15–30 cotyledons (each = a unit of villous tree covered by decidual septa)
  • Cord: ~50 cm long, 1.5 cm diameter; 2 umbilical arteries (deoxygenated) + 1 umbilical vein (oxygenated)

Villous Structure:

  • Stem villi → branch into free villi → terminal villi (site of exchange)
  • Terminal villus = fetal capillary + cytotrophoblast + syncytiotrophoblast
  • Intervillous space = filled with maternal blood (from spiral arteries)
  • Chorionic plate (fetal side) + Basal plate (maternal side = decidua basalis)
Placental Circulation — Dual Blood Supply
FETAL CIRCULATION Umbilical arteries (2) → Chorionic plate → Villous capillaries → Umbilical vein (1) → Fetus Arteries carry deoxygenated blood TO placenta. Vein carries oxygenated blood FROM placenta.
⇌
MATERNAL CIRCULATION Uterine spiral arteries → Intervillous space → Endometrial veins → Maternal circulation Maternal blood BATHES the villi directly (haemomonochorial placenta)
Umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood (reverse of systemic arteries). Umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood (reverse of systemic veins). Examiner classic trap.

What Crosses the Placenta

Crosses freely: O₂, CO₂, H₂O, glucose, lipids, IgG (passive immunity), most drugs, viruses (rubella, CMV, HIV, parvovirus B19), T. pallidum. Does NOT cross: IgM, bacteria (mostly), heparin, insulin. Crosses with risk: alcohol, thalidomide, phenytoin, warfarin, tetracyclines, ACE inhibitors (teratogens). TORCH = Toxoplasma, Other (syphilis, VZV, parvovirus), Rubella, CMV, Herpes/HIV

Functions of Placenta — "REPORT CARD" PYQ 2015, 2021
  • Respiratory: O₂ transfer (fetal Hb has higher O₂ affinity — Bohr effect/double Bohr effect), CO₂ excretion
  • Excretory: Urea, uric acid, bilirubin, creatinine transferred to maternal blood
  • Protective/barrier: Partial — blocks large molecules, some microbes, but NOT all teratogens
  • Nutritive: Glucose (GLUT1, facilitated diffusion), amino acids (active transport), fatty acids, vitamins, minerals (Fe, Ca — actively transported against gradient)
  • Endocrine: hCG (rescues corpus luteum), hCS/hPL (insulin antagonist, mobilises maternal FA), progesterone (from Week 8 onward — luteoplacental shift), oestrogen (requires fetal adrenal DHEAS → placenta converts → oestriol → maternal urine — marker of fetal wellbeing)
  • Immunological: Transfers maternal IgG → passive immunity for newborn (lasts 3–6 months). No IgA, IgM transferred.
Umbilical Cord PYQ 2013-S
  • Length: ~50 cm. Diameter: ~1.5 cm. Covered by amnion
  • Contains: 2 umbilical arteries + 1 umbilical vein embedded in Wharton's jelly (specialised myxoid connective tissue = mucoid connective tissue)
  • Single umbilical artery (SUA) — in 1% of births — associated with renal anomalies and congenital heart defects
  • Cord insertion: central (normal), eccentric, marginal (battledore placenta), velamentous (vessels traverse membranes — risk of vasa praevia)
  • Wharton's jelly = proteoglycans + fibroblasts — protects vessels from compression
Placenta Previa & Abnormal Positions PYQ 2014, 2025

Normally, the placenta implants in the upper uterine segment. **Placenta Previa** occurs when the blastocyst implants in the lower uterine segment, causing the placenta to form over or near the internal cervical os.

Classification & Types:

  • Type I (Low-Lying): Placenta is in the lower segment, but its edge does not reach the internal cervical os (typically within 2 cm).
  • Type II (Marginal): Placenta reaches the margin of the internal os, but does not cover it.
  • Type III (Partial): Placenta partially covers the internal cervical os.
  • Type IV (Complete/Total): Placenta completely covers the internal os.
Clinical Presentation & Danger

Characterized by **sudden, painless, bright-red vaginal bleeding** in late pregnancy (third trimester). Digital vaginal examination is strictly contraindicated because it can cause massive, fatal hemorrhage. Diagnosis must be confirmed by ultrasound. Delivery is typically via Caesarean section.

Immunological Non-Rejection of the Conceptus PYQ 2023

The conceptus is an allograft containing 50% paternal DNA, yet it is not rejected by the mother. This is achieved via multiple mechanisms:

  • Lack of Classical MHC Expression: Trophoblast cells do not express classical MHC Class I (HLA-A, HLA-B) or MHC Class II molecules, preventing direct maternal CD8+ T-cell attack.
  • HLA-G Expression: Extravillous trophoblasts express non-classical HLA-G, which binds to inhibitory receptors on maternal NK cells and deciduoma macrophages, halting cell lysis.
  • Decidual Barrier: The decidua contains high concentrations of immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β) and active regulatory T-cells (Tregs).
  • Tryptophan Depletion: Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) degrades tryptophan in the decidua, suppressing local maternal T-cell activation.
  • Syncytiotrophoblast Barrier: A continuous syncytium without intercellular clefts acts as a physical shield preventing direct cell-to-cell contact between maternal lymphocytes and fetal capillary beds.

Medical Genetics

Karyotyping & Chromosomal Aberrations PYQ 2013, 2016, 2018-S, 2019, 2023, 2024

Karyotyping is the process of pairing and ordering all the chromosomes of an organism, providing a genome-wide photo of an individual's chromosomes. It is used to detect chromosomal aberrations.

Types of Chromosomal Abnormalities:

  • Aneuploidy: An abnormal number of chromosomes due to non-disjunction (failure of homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate during meiosis I or II). Examples: Trisomy 21 (Down's), Trisomy 18 (Edwards), Trisomy 13 (Patau), Monosomy X (Turner's).
  • Translocation: Transfer of a chromosomal segment from one chromosome to a non-homologous chromosome.
    • Reciprocal translocation: Two-way exchange of segments.
    • Robertsonian translocation: Fusion of the long arms (q-arms) of two acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22) with loss of their short arms. Robertsonian translocation t(14;21) accounts for ~4% of Down syndrome cases and can be inherited from a carrier parent.
Down Syndrome Etiology Differentiator

Down syndrome (47, XX/XY +21) arises from:

  • Meiotic Non-disjunction (95%): Correlates strongly with advanced maternal age. Usually maternal Meiosis I error.
  • Robertsonian Translocation (4%): Inherited, maternal age-independent. Carrier parent has 45 chromosomes but normal phenotype.
  • Mosaicism (1%): Mitotic non-disjunction after fertilization. Milder clinical features.
Sex-Linked Inheritance & Pedigree Analysis PYQ 2017-S, 2023

X-Linked Recessive Inheritance is the most common pattern of sex-linked inheritance. It is characterized by transmission of the disease allele on the X chromosome.

Inheritance Pattern Rules:

  • Expressed fully in hemizygous males (XY) who inherit the mutant X from their mother.
  • Females are typically asymptomatic carriers (XX) due to the presence of a normal backup X chromosome.
  • No male-to-male transmission: A father passes his Y chromosome to his sons, never his X.
  • An affected father passes his mutant X to all of his daughters, making them carriers.
  • Hemophilia Carrier Mother Pedigree: For a carrier mother (XHXh) and normal father (XHY):
    • 50% chance of sons being affected (XhY).
    • 50% chance of daughters being carriers (XHXh).

Barr Bodies & The Lyon Hypothesis PYQ 2011, 2012, 2016-S, 2019, 2025

A Barr Body is a condensed, inactivated X chromosome found in the somatic cell nuclei of female mammals. It appears as a small, darkly staining structure (sex chromatin) against the inner nuclear membrane.

The Lyon Hypothesis:

  • One of the two X chromosomes in each female somatic cell is randomly and permanently inactivated during early embryonic life (blastocyst stage).
  • Inactivation is mediated by the XIST gene (X-inactive specific transcript) which produces a non-coding RNA that coats and silences the chromosome (forming heterochromatin).
  • This ensures dosage compensation, so that males and females have equal expression of X-linked genes.
The N - 1 Rule for Barr Bodies

The number of Barr bodies in a cell is always equal to Total X chromosomes (N) minus 1.

  • Normal Male (46, XY): 0 Barr bodies.
  • Normal Female (46, XX): 1 Barr body.
  • Turner Syndrome (45, X0): 0 Barr bodies.
  • Klinefelter Syndrome (47, XXY): 1 Barr body.
  • Double Barr Body variant (48, XXXY): 2 Barr bodies.
  • Triple X Syndrome (47, XXX): 2 Barr bodies.

Fertilization & Early Development
What is the site of fertilization and why is this clinically important?PYQ 2016, 2022

Normal site: Ampulla of the uterine tube — widest, most capacious part. The secondary oocyte is viable for ~24 hours post-ovulation; sperm are viable for 2–3 days.

Clinical importance: If the fertilised ovum implants OUTSIDE the uterine cavity → ectopic pregnancy. Most common ectopic site = ampulla (75–80%). Other sites: isthmus (narrow → ruptures early), interstitial/cornual (ruptures later, more haemorrhage), ovarian, abdominal (can reach viability). Ectopic pregnancy causes haemoperitoneum if ruptured — surgical emergency. Serum β-hCG rises but is lower than expected for gestational age, and no intrauterine gestation sac on transvaginal USG.

Examiner tip: always state "ampulla" specifically, not just "uterine tube."

What is the acrosome reaction? How is polyspermy prevented?PYQ 2019

Acrosome reaction: ZP3 on zona pellucida binds sperm receptor → Ca²⁺ influx → fusion of acrosomal membrane with sperm plasma membrane → release of acrosomal enzymes (hyaluronidase, acrosin, neuraminidase) → allows sperm to penetrate zona pellucida.

Prevention of polyspermy — Cortical reaction: When sperm head fuses with oocyte plasma membrane → Ca²⁺ wave from inside-out → cortical granules release their contents into perivitelline space → modify ZP2 and ZP3 glycoproteins (zona reaction) → zona pellucida hardens → no other sperm can bind or penetrate. This is the primary block to polyspermy in humans.

Secondary (fast) block: Membrane depolarisation immediately after sperm fusion — prevents additional sperm membrane fusion transiently.

Polyspermy results in triploid embryo — invariably lethal (triploidy).

Placenta & Fetal Circulation
Describe the structure and functions of the placenta.PYQ 2012, 2017, 2020, 2023

Structure at term: Discoid, ~22 cm diameter, ~500 g. Fetal surface = smooth (covered by amnion) with umbilical cord inserting centrally. Maternal surface = rough, 15–30 cotyledons separated by decidual septa arising from decidua basalis. Each cotyledon = a stem villus with its branches bathed in maternal blood in the intervillous space.

Histology: Chorionic villi — core of fetal capillaries and Hofbauer cells (macrophages) covered by cytotrophoblast (Langhans cells, sparse at term) and syncytiotrophoblast (multinucleate, functional layer). Intervillous space contains maternal blood.

Functions (REPORT):

  • Respiratory: O₂/CO₂ exchange (facilitated by fetal Hb's higher O₂ affinity)
  • Excretory: urea, bilirubin, creatinine transfer to maternal blood
  • Protective barrier: partial — blocks bacteria, large molecules, but not all drugs/viruses
  • Nutritive: glucose (facilitated diffusion), amino acids (active transport), fatty acids, Fe and Ca (active transport)
  • Endocrine: hCG, hCS/hPL, progesterone (from Week 8), oestrogen (requires fetal adrenal DHEAS)
  • Immunological: IgG transfer (passive immunity for 3–6 months post-birth)

Key exam point: maternal and fetal blood NEVER mix under normal circumstances — they are separated by the placental barrier.

Name the fetal shunts. What happens to each at birth?PYQ 2014, 2019, 2023

Three fetal shunts:

  • Foramen Ovale (RA → LA): bypasses lungs at atrial level. At birth: first breath → ↑ PO₂ → ↓ pulmonary resistance → ↑ LA pressure → flap valve closes. Becomes Fossa Ovalis. 25% of adults have probe-patent FO (PFO — usually harmless but can cause paradoxical emboli in context of DVT).
  • Ductus Arteriosus (PA → descending aorta): bypasses lungs at great vessel level. At birth: ↑ PO₂ + ↓ prostaglandins → smooth muscle constriction → functional closure within hours → anatomical closure (fibrous obliteration) within weeks. Becomes Ligamentum Arteriosum. Patent DA in premature infants: indomethacin (PG inhibitor). Keep open with PGE1 in ductal-dependent CHDs.
  • Ductus Venosus (umbilical vein → IVC, bypasses liver): closes with cord clamping → loss of flow → collapses → becomes Ligamentum Venosum on posterior surface of liver.

Also: Umbilical vein → Ligamentum Teres Hepatis. Umbilical arteries → Medial Umbilical Ligaments (distal) + Superior Vesical Arteries (proximal).

What is a sacrococcygeal teratoma? What is its embryological basis?PYQ 2021

Embryological basis: The primitive streak normally disappears by Week 4. Any remnant of the primitive streak may persist in the sacrococcygeal region and proliferate into a teratoma (as it contains pluripotent epiblast cells capable of forming all three germ layers → tissues from all germ layers appear in the tumour).

Sacrococcygeal teratoma: Most common tumour of neonates (1 in 40,000 live births). 4× more common in females. Usually benign in neonates (mature teratoma); malignant potential increases if diagnosis is delayed beyond 6 months (immature/malignant elements). Presents as a mass protruding from the sacrococcygeal region. May be diagnosed on antenatal USG. Treatment: surgical excision (include coccyx to prevent recurrence).

Describe the development and closure of the neural tube. What anomalies result from failure?PYQ 2012, 2019, 2024

Development: Notochord (Week 3) induces overlying ectoderm → neural plate (Week 3) → neural plate folds laterally → neural folds elevate and meet → neural tube forms (Week 4). Fusion begins in the cervical region and zips rostrally + caudally simultaneously.

Neuropore closure: Rostral neuropore closes Day 25 (Carnegie Stage 11). Caudal neuropore closes Day 27 (Carnegie Stage 12). Neural crest cells migrate away from the closing folds.

Failure anomalies:

  • Rostral neuropore failure: Anencephaly (no forebrain). Polyhydramnios. Lethal. ↑↑ AFP.
  • Caudal neuropore failure: Spina bifida spectrum — occulta (skin-covered, asymptomatic) → meningocele (meninges herniate) → meningomyelocele (cord + roots herniate → paralysis + hydrocephalus).

Prevention: periconceptional folate (0.4 mg/day) reduces NTD risk by ~70%.

EQ — Fetal Circulation [PYQ 2014, 2019, 2023]
"Describe fetal circulation. What changes occur at birth?"
  1. Why fetal circulation differs: Lungs are collapsed and non-functional; liver partially bypassed; gas exchange occurs at placenta. Three shunts exist to redirect blood: Ductus Venosus (bypass liver), Foramen Ovale (bypass lungs at atrial level), Ductus Arteriosus (bypass lungs at great vessel level).
  2. Pathway of oxygenated blood: Placenta → Umbilical vein (PO₂ ~40 mmHg) → splits at porta hepatis: (a) Ductus Venosus → IVC; (b) Portal vein → liver → hepatic veins → IVC → Right Atrium.
  3. Foramen Ovale: Most oxygenated IVC blood directed by Eustachian valve through Foramen Ovale → Left Atrium → Left Ventricle → Ascending Aorta → brain and coronary arteries preferentially receive most oxygenated blood. SVC blood → Right Ventricle → Pulmonary Artery.
  4. Ductus Arteriosus: ~90% of pulmonary artery blood → Ductus Arteriosus → descending aorta (distal to left subclavian) → bypasses lungs. Only ~10% cardiac output reaches fetal lungs. Maintained open by prostaglandins (PGE2) + low PO₂.
  5. Return to placenta: Descending aorta → Internal iliac arteries → 2 Umbilical Arteries (deoxygenated) → placenta.
  6. Changes at birth — Shunt Closures: First breath → ↑ PO₂ + ↑ LA pressure → Foramen Ovale closes (→ Fossa Ovalis). ↑ PO₂ + ↓ prostaglandins → Ductus Arteriosus closes (→ Ligamentum Arteriosum). Cord clamping → Ductus Venosus collapses (→ Ligamentum Venosum). Umbilical vein → Ligamentum Teres Hepatis. Umbilical arteries → Medial Umbilical Ligaments.
EQ — Neural Tube Defects [PYQ 2016, 2024]
"Classify and describe the neural tube defects with their aetiology and clinical features."
  1. Embryological basis: Neural tube forms by fusion of neural folds (Week 4). Rostral neuropore closes Day 25; caudal neuropore Day 27. Failure of closure → neural tube defects (NTDs). Overall incidence: 1–2/1000 live births. Aetiology: multifactorial — folate deficiency, valproate, genetic factors (chromosomal trisomies 13, 18).
  2. Anencephaly: Rostral neuropore failure → absence of brain (forebrain, cerebral hemispheres) and cranial vault. Incompatible with life. Frog-like facies. Associated with polyhydramnios (absent swallowing reflex). Markedly elevated AFP in maternal serum + amniotic fluid. Most can be detected on USG at 11–14 weeks.
  3. Spina Bifida: Caudal neuropore failure → vertebral arch defect at lumbar/lumbosacral region. (a) Spina Bifida Occulta: posterior arch defect covered by skin; tuft of hair/dimple/angioma; usually asymptomatic. (b) Meningocele: meninges herniate through bony defect → fluid-filled sac, no neural tissue inside → better prognosis; surgically correctable. (c) Meningomyelocele: meninges + spinal cord/nerve roots herniate → most clinically significant form; ↑ AFP; lower limb sensorimotor deficits, bladder/bowel dysfunction, hydrocephalus (Arnold-Chiari II malformation — hindbrain herniation into foramen magnum).
  4. Prenatal Diagnosis: ↑ maternal serum AFP (weeks 15–20); USG (lemon sign + banana sign in meningomyelocele). Amniocentesis: AFP + acetylcholinesterase (AChE — specific for open NTDs with neural tissue exposure).
  5. Prevention: Periconceptional folic acid: 0.4 mg/day for all women planning pregnancy (4 weeks before to 12 weeks after conception) → reduces NTD risk by ~70%. High risk (prior NTD, antiepileptic drugs): 5 mg/day. Mechanism: folate required for DNA synthesis (one-carbon metabolism) → adequate cell proliferation during neural tube closure.
