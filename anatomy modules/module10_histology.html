Staining Techniques

Haematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) is the universal routine stain. Haematoxylin = basophilic structures (blue-purple); Eosin = acidophilic/eosinophilic structures (pink). Everything else is a special stain for specific targets.

H&E Colour Logic — Basophilia vs Eosinophilia

Basophilic = BLUE-PURPLE (negative charge = binds + ve haematoxylin):

  • Nuclei (DNA/RNA — phosphate groups)
  • Rough ER (ribosomes)
  • Cytoplasm of plasma cells (abundant RER)
  • Cartilage matrix (chondroitin sulphate GAGs)
  • Mast cell granules
  • Calcium deposits

Eosinophilic = PINK (+ charge = binds − ve eosin):

  • Cytoplasm generally (proteins)
  • Collagen fibres
  • RBCs (haemoglobin)
  • Muscle fibres
  • Eosinophil granules (major basic protein)
  • Mitochondria-rich cells (PCT kidney, hepatocytes)
Congo Red = Amyloid Trap
"Congo turns APPLE-GREEN under POLARISED light — nothing else does this"

Salmon-pink under routine light → apple-green birefringence under polarised light = pathognomonic for amyloid. Asked in practicals and MCQs every year.

Epithelial Tissue

Epithelium = avascular tissue covering surfaces and lining cavities. Classified by number of layers (simple/stratified) and shape of surface cells (squamous/cuboidal/columnar). Rests on a basement membrane (Type IV collagen + laminin + heparan sulphate proteoglycans).

Elastic Cartilage Locations
"EAR — External Auricle is elastic, Epiglottis is elastic"

Elastic because these structures need flexibility that won't fatigue: the ear springs back when deformed; the epiglottis flaps repeatedly during swallowing.

Relapsing Polychondritis

Autoimmune destruction of cartilage (anti-type II collagen antibodies). Affects: ear (auricular chondritis → floppy ear), nose (saddle nose deformity), trachea (respiratory compromise), joints. Spares fibrocartilage (intervertebral discs) — no type II collagen in fibrocartilage.

Bone & Ossification

Bone = specialised CT with mineralised matrix (65% hydroxyapatite + 35% collagen). Hardest yet dynamic tissue — constantly remodelled by osteoblasts (form) and osteoclasts (resorb). Contains osteocytes in lacunae connected by canaliculi.

RANK-RANKL-OPG Axis — Key Regulatory System

Osteoblasts/osteocytes express RANKL → binds RANK on osteoclast precursors → osteoclast differentiation and activation. Osteoblasts also express OPG (osteoprotegerin) as a decoy receptor → binds RANKL → prevents osteoclast activation.
PTH/1,25(OH)₂D ↑ RANKL → ↑ bone resorption. Denosumab = anti-RANKL antibody (treatment for osteoporosis).

Ossification — Intramembranous vs Endochondral ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2016, 2019, 2024

Intramembranous Ossification

  • Direct bone formation from mesenchymal cells → NO cartilage intermediate
  • Mesenchymal cells condense → differentiate directly into osteoblasts → secrete osteoid → mineralisation → woven bone → remodelled to lamellar
  • Forms: skull vault (frontal, parietal, occipital squama), mandible, clavicle (partial), facial bones, some wrist bones
  • Primary ossification centres appear in membrane → radiate outward
  • Periosteum derived from surrounding mesenchyme

Endochondral Ossification

  • Bone replaces a pre-existing hyaline cartilage model
  • Forms: long bones (femur, humerus), short bones (carpals), vertebrae, ribs, most of skeleton
  • Primary ossification centre: diaphysis → vascularisation → cartilage matrix calcified → osteoblasts lay bone on calcified cartilage → marrow cavity
  • Secondary ossification centres: epiphyses (after birth, except distal femur and proximal tibia = at birth)
  • Growth plate (epiphyseal plate) zones: R-P-H-C-O → Resting, Proliferating, Hypertrophic, Calcified, Ossifying

Rickets vs Osteomalacia

Rickets: Vit D deficiency in children (growth plate still open). Hypertrophic zone EXPANDS (chondrocytes accumulate but cannot be replaced) → wide, irregular growth plate → bowing of legs, craniotabes, rachitic rosary. Osteomalacia: same deficiency in adults (no growth plate) → excess unmineralized osteoid → bone pain, fractures, pseudo-fractures (Looser's zones).

Sarcomere Structure — Skeletal & Cardiac ⭐ PYQ 2014, 2018, 2022

Bands and Lines (Z to Z):

  • Z line (Z disc): Boundary of sarcomere; α-actinin; anchors actin thin filaments; titin attached
  • I band: Only thin filaments (actin); LIGHT band; spans Z line; NARROWS during contraction
  • A band: Thick myosin filaments + overlap zone; DARK band; DOES NOT CHANGE LENGTH during contraction
  • H zone: Centre of A band; only thick filaments (no overlap); NARROWS during contraction
  • M line: Centre of H zone; connects myosin thick filaments; M-protein, myomesin

Thin vs Thick Filaments:

  • Thin filament: F-actin (double helix of G-actin) + Tropomyosin (runs in groove) + Troponin complex (TnT — anchors; TnI — inhibits; TnC — binds Ca²⁺)
  • Thick filament: Myosin II (two heavy chains + four light chains); S1 head = ATPase + actin binding site
  • Titin: Giant elastic protein; connects Z line to M line; acts as molecular spring; maintains sarcomere integrity
  • Nebulin: Thin filament ruler in skeletal muscle; sets thin filament length
What changes during contraction?
"I Hate Shortening" — I band gets shorter, H zone gets shorter (disappears), A band = Always the same
Intercalated Discs — Cardiac Unique Feature PYQ 2016, 2019

Transverse Component (Mechanical):

  • Fasciae adherentes (step-like junctions): actin filament attachment; = zonula adherens equivalent but for cardiac muscle; N-cadherin; transmits contractile force
  • Maculae adherentes (Desmosomes): intermediate filament (desmin) attachment; mechanical stability; contain desmoglein/desmoplakin; Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy = desmosomal protein mutations

Lateral Component (Electrical):

  • Gap junctions (Nexus): Connexin 43 predominant in ventricle; connexin 40 in conduction system; allow electrical impulse to spread directly between cardiomyocytes → functional syncytium
  • No tight junctions in intercalated discs (present in epithelium only)

Nervous Tissue & Neuroglia

Nervous tissue = neurons (excitable cells) + neuroglia (supporting cells). CNS neuroglia: astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, ependymal cells. PNS neuroglia: Schwann cells, satellite cells.
Neuron Structure ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2015, 2018, 2021

Cell Body (Perikaryon/Soma):

  • Nissl bodies = granular basophilic structures = rough ER + free ribosomes; scattered throughout soma and dendrites; used for protein synthesis; ABSENT in axon and axon hillock (hallmark of axon)
  • Large pale nucleus with prominent NUCLEOLUS (high transcriptional activity)
  • Neurofilaments, microtubules, mitochondria (abundant)
  • Lipofuscin granules (normal wear-and-tear pigment; increase with age)

Processes:

  • Dendrites: Multiple; taper from soma; contain Nissl bodies; receive input; dendritic spines = sites of excitatory synapses; increase receptive surface area
  • Axon: Single; from axon hillock (no Nissl bodies); constant diameter; myelin sheath (if myelinated); ends in terminal boutons (synaptic knobs); can be up to 1 meter long
  • Axon hillock: Origin of axon; trigger zone for action potential generation (highest density of Na⁺ channels)

Staining & Epithelium
What is metachromasia? Give two examples of metachromatic staining.PYQ 2018, 2022

Metachromasia: A phenomenon where a tissue component stains a different colour from the dye used — the stain undergoes a colour shift due to molecular interaction with the tissue constituent.

Mechanism: The dye molecules (e.g., toluidine blue = blue at pH 7) undergo polymerisation when they bind to highly negatively charged polyanions (acidic mucopolysaccharides, heparin) → polymer absorbs at a different wavelength → appears red/purple instead of blue.

Examples:

  • Mast cell granules (contain heparin, heparan sulphate) → stain purple-red with toluidine blue (instead of blue)
  • Cartilage matrix (chondroitin sulphate proteoglycans) → metachromatically stains with toluidine blue
  • Also: amyloid with crystal violet (metachromatically stains red-violet)
Why does transitional epithelium have a different name from other epithelia? What is its unique histological feature?PYQ 2016, 2020

Transitional epithelium (urothelium) was named "transitional" because it was thought to represent a transition between stratified squamous and stratified columnar. Modern terminology prefers urothelium (limited to urinary tract).

Unique features:

  • Umbrella cells (surface dome cells): large, binucleate, dome-shaped cells that flatten when the bladder is full. They can cover 3–5 cells below them.
  • Asymmetric unit membrane (AUM): Specialized thickened luminal membrane with unique hexagonal plaques (uroplakins — UP1a, UP1b, UP2, UP3a). Creates an impermeable barrier to urine.
  • Changes with distension: relaxed = 4–6 cell layers, dome-shaped surface; distended = 2–3 layers, flattened surface cells.
Bone, Cartilage & Muscle
What is the difference between intramembranous and endochondral ossification? Which bones are formed by each?PYQ 2012, 2016, 2021

Key difference: Intramembranous ossification forms bone DIRECTLY from mesenchyme (no cartilage template). Endochondral ossification replaces a pre-existing hyaline cartilage model with bone.

Intramembranous bones: Skull vault (frontal, parietal, squamous occipital, squamous temporal), mandible, clavicle (partly), facial bones. These are all flat bones that form in fibrous membranes.

Endochondral bones: Long bones (femur, tibia, humerus, radius), short bones (carpals, tarsals), vertebrae, ribs, costal cartilages, base of skull, ethmoid. All have a cartilage model.

Clavicle is unique — forms partly by intramembranous AND partly endochondral (medial end). First bone to begin ossification (5th–6th fetal week) and last to complete (mid-20s).

Why does articular cartilage lack a perichondrium? What is its clinical significance?PYQ 2017, 2022

Articular cartilage has NO perichondrium because it requires a smooth, frictionless surface for joint movement. The perichondrium is a fibrous layer that would disrupt the smooth articulation and would be worn away rapidly.

Nutrition: Articular cartilage is nourished by diffusion from synovial fluid (no perichondrium needed for vascular supply) + from subchondral bone (some diffusion).

Growth: Articular cartilage can ONLY grow by interstitial growth (chondrocytes dividing within matrix). It CANNOT grow by appositional growth (which requires the inner perichondrium).

Clinical: Because of no perichondrium + avascularity → very poor repair capacity. Damaged articular cartilage heals poorly → osteoarthritis (cartilage degradation) progresses once initiated. Rationale for regenerative approaches (autologous chondrocyte implantation, microfracture, etc.).

What are the changes in sarcomere bands during muscle contraction? What is the sliding filament theory?PYQ 2014, 2019, 2023

Sliding Filament Theory (Huxley & Hanson, 1954): During contraction, thin actin filaments slide OVER thick myosin filaments toward the M line → Z lines move closer together → sarcomere shortens. Neither thick nor thin filaments themselves change length.

Band changes during contraction:

  • A band: NO CHANGE (Always the same) — entire length of myosin
  • I band: NARROWS (shortens) — actin slides toward M line, less actin exposed
  • H zone: NARROWS and disappears at maximal contraction — myosin overlap increases
  • Z lines: Move CLOSER together — sarcomere shortens

Mechanism: Action potential → SR releases Ca²⁺ → Ca²⁺ binds TnC → conformational change in troponin-tropomyosin → tropomyosin shifts → exposes myosin-binding sites on actin → myosin S1 heads bind actin → power stroke (ADP + Pi release → conformational change) → thin filament pulled toward M line → sarcomere shortens.

What are the zones of liver acinus and their clinical significance?PYQ 2014, 2017, 2020, 2023

The hepatic acinus (Rappaport) is the functional unit of the liver, diamond-shaped, centred on a terminal portal venule and hepatic arteriole with hepatic veins at its poles.

  • Zone 1 (periportal): Adjacent to portal tract; first to receive oxygenated blood (PO₂ ~60-65 mmHg); highest oxygen and nutrient concentration; most metabolically active (gluconeogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, albumin synthesis); most resistant to ischaemia; FIRST to be damaged by hepatotoxins (e.g., phosphorus, ferrous sulphate); FIRST to regenerate.
  • Zone 2 (midzonal): Intermediate. Affected in yellow fever (classic midzonal necrosis).
  • Zone 3 (perivenular/centrilobular): Adjacent to terminal hepatic vein; poorest oxygenation; FIRST to die in ischaemia (congestive heart failure → "nutmeg liver" = centrilobular congestion/necrosis); FIRST affected by CCl₄ toxicity (metabolically activated here by CYP2E1); least resistant to hypoxia.

Exam tip: Zone 1 = toxins entering (portal blood); Zone 3 = ischaemia (farthest from oxygen supply).

What are the histological features unique to cardiac muscle?PYQ 2013, 2016, 2019, 2022

Unique features of cardiac muscle:

  • Intercalated discs: Seen as dark transverse bands on H&E. Contain: fasciae adherentes (actin attachment), maculae adherentes/desmosomes (mechanical coupling), and gap junctions (electrical coupling). ONLY in cardiac muscle.
  • Central nuclei: 1–2 nuclei per cell, centrally placed (skeletal = peripheral, multiple; smooth = single central).
  • Branching fibers: Cardiac cells anastomose with adjacent cells via lateral branches → three-dimensional network (syncytium).
  • Purkinje fibres: Modified cardiomyocytes; conducting system; LARGER than regular cardiomyocytes; PALE, glycogen-rich cytoplasm; few myofibrils (at periphery); central nuclei; in subendocardial position.
  • Absent satellite cells: Very limited regeneration capacity (unlike skeletal muscle which has satellite cells).
What are Sertoli cells? Name all their functions.PYQ 2014, 2018, 2021

Sertoli cells are "nurse cells" of the testis — tall columnar supporting cells in seminiferous tubules that rest on the BM and extend to the lumen. Irregular nucleus with prominent nucleolus; contain Charcot-Böttcher crystalloids (unique).

Functions:

  • Support spermatogenesis: Provide mechanical support, nutrients, and regulatory factors to developing germ cells
  • Blood-testis barrier: Tight junctions between adjacent Sertoli cells separate basal compartment (spermatogonia) from adluminal compartment (spermatocytes, spermatids) → immunological privilege
  • Phagocytosis: Engulf residual cytoplasm (residual bodies) discarded during spermiogenesis; phagocytose abnormal/dead germ cells
  • ABP (Androgen-Binding Protein): Secreted into tubular lumen; binds testosterone → maintains high local testosterone concentration → needed for spermatogenesis. Stimulated by FSH.
  • Inhibin B secretion: → pituitary feedback → suppresses FSH
  • AMH/MIF (Anti-Müllerian Hormone): In fetal Sertoli cells → causes regression of Müllerian (paramesonephric) ducts in male embryo
  • Aromatase: Convert testosterone → oestradiol (locally important)

EQ — Compare Skeletal, Cardiac, and Smooth Muscle [PYQ 2013, 2019, 2022]
"Compare the histological features of skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle."
  1. Introduction: Muscle tissue is classified into three types based on location, structure, and control: skeletal (voluntary striated), cardiac (involuntary striated), and smooth (involuntary non-striated). All share the property of contractility via actin-myosin interaction.
  2. Shape and organisation: Skeletal = long unbranched cylinders; Cardiac = short branching cells forming a three-dimensional syncytium; Smooth = fusiform (tapered spindle) cells arranged in sheets.
  3. Nuclei: Skeletal = multiple, peripheral (beneath sarcolemma). Cardiac = 1–2, central, oval. Smooth = single, central, elongated, cigar-shaped.
  4. Striations: Skeletal and cardiac = transverse striations (A, I, H bands, Z lines) due to sarcomere organisation. Smooth = NO striations (thin and thick filaments not arranged in sarcomeres; instead attach to dense bodies).
  5. Unique structures: Cardiac only — intercalated discs (transverse bands containing fasciae adherentes + desmosomes + gap junctions); Skeletal only — satellite cells (myogenic stem cells for regeneration); Smooth — dense bodies, caldesmon/calponin regulatory proteins.
  6. T-tubules and SR: Skeletal — T-tubules at A-I junction, well-developed SR → fast twitch. Cardiac — T-tubules at Z lines, moderate SR → relies partly on extracellular Ca²⁺. Smooth — no true T-tubules (caveolae instead), sparse SR → slow sustained contraction.
  7. Regeneration: Skeletal — via satellite cells. Cardiac — minimal (cardiac progenitor cells; mainly fibrosis). Smooth — mitotically competent.
EQ — Haversian System of Compact Bone [PYQ 2013, 2017, 2021]
"Describe the Haversian system of compact bone."
  1. Definition: The Haversian system (osteon) is the structural and functional unit of compact (cortical) bone. It consists of a central canal surrounded by concentric lamellae of bone matrix with osteocytes in lacunae.
  2. Haversian (central) canal: Runs parallel to the long axis of bone. Contains blood vessels (arteriole and venule), unmyelinated nerve fibres, and loose connective tissue. Lined by endosteum.
  3. Lamellae: 4–20 concentric rings of mineralised bone matrix surrounding the Haversian canal. Collagen fibres run at alternating angles in adjacent lamellae → high torsional strength.
  4. Lacunae and osteocytes: Lacunae are lens-shaped spaces within lamellae containing osteocytes (trapped osteoblasts). Connected to Haversian canal and each other by canaliculi (tiny tunnels carrying osteocyte processes and tissue fluid).
  5. Cement line: Outer boundary of osteon; highly mineralised, refractile; marks where bone resorption was followed by deposition.
  6. Volkmann's canals: Perforating canals running perpendicular (transverse/oblique) to the long axis, connecting Haversian canals to each other and to periosteum/endosteum. Do NOT have their own concentric lamellae (this distinguishes from Haversian canals).
  7. Interstitial lamellae: Irregular fragments between osteons = remnants of old osteons that were remodelled. Show cement lines on their outer surface.
EQ — Describe the Histology of Skin [PYQ 2012, 2016, 2019, 2021, 2023]
"Describe the histology of skin. Name the cells of the epidermis."
  1. Introduction: Skin (integument) = body's largest organ. Composed of two main layers: epidermis (stratified squamous keratinized epithelium, ectodermal) + dermis (dense CT, mesodermal). Hypodermis (subcutaneous tissue) lies beneath.
  2. Epidermis — 5 layers (deep to superficial): Stratum basale (stem cells + melanocytes + Merkel cells) → Stratum spinosum (prickle cells + Langerhans cells) → Stratum granulosum (keratohyalin granules + lamellar bodies) → Stratum lucidum (ONLY in thick skin) → Stratum corneum (dead keratin-filled squames).
  3. Epidermal cells: Keratinocytes (90%, produce keratin, undergo terminal differentiation); Melanocytes (neural crest, DOPA+, produce melanin in melanosomes → donate to keratinocytes); Langerhans cells (CD1a+, S100+, Birbeck granules, APC); Merkel cells (CK20+, mechanoreceptor, SA1 type).
  4. Dermis: Papillary dermis (thin, loose CT, dermal papillae, Meissner's corpuscles, free nerve endings) + Reticular dermis (thick, dense irregular CT, type I+III collagen, Pacinian corpuscles, Ruffini endings).
  5. Skin appendages: Eccrine sweat glands (merocrine secretion, thermoregulation); Apocrine glands (modified merocrine, axilla/groin, puberty-dependent); Sebaceous glands (holocrine secretion, sebum); Hair follicles (pilosebaceous unit, arrector pili); Nails (hard keratin, nail plate + bed + fold + hyponychium + lunula).
  6. Differences — Thick vs Thin skin: Thick skin (palms, soles): has stratum lucidum, thicker corneum, no hair follicles, no sebaceous glands. Thin skin (rest of body): no stratum lucidum, thinner, has all appendages.
