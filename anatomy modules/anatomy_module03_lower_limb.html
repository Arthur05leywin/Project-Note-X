Hip Joint

Ball and socket joint — most stable joint in the body (deep acetabulum + strong ligaments). Type: Synovial, multiaxial. Femoral head (ball) fits into acetabulum (socket, deepened by fibrocartilaginous acetabular labrum).

Blood Supply to Head of Femur
  • Medial circumflex femoral artery (MCFA) — main supply in adults, branch of profunda femoris. Retinacular vessels run along femoral neck
  • Lateral circumflex femoral artery (LCFA) — minor contribution

Hip Dislocation — Direction Matters

Posterior dislocation (most common, 90%) — forced flexion + adduction (dashboard injury) → limb: shortened, flexed, adducted, medially rotated → sciatic nerve at risk.

Anterior dislocation — limb: extended, abducted, laterally rotated → femoral vessels and nerve at risk.

Posterior wall of acetabulum is weakest (smallest surface area of socket covers posterior-superior quadrant) → hence posterior dislocation is commonest.

Knee Joint

Largest and most complex joint in the body. Type: Compound synovial (tibiofemoral + patellofemoral). Primarily a hinge joint but allows slight rotation (especially in last 10° extension = "locking"). ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2016, 2019, 2023

Cruciate Attachments
"ACL = Anterior-to-Lateral" (anterior tibia, lateral femur) · "PCL = Posterior-to-Medial" (posterior tibia, medial femur)
"The ACL prevents Anterior tibial translation — 'A' for anterior" → when ACL ruptures, tibia slides FORWARD.
Collateral Ligaments & Menisci

Collateral Ligaments:

  • MCL (Medial/Tibial collateral): Medial femoral condyle → medial tibial shaft. Attached to medial meniscus. Prevents valgus stress. Taut in extension
  • LCL (Lateral/Fibular collateral): Lateral femoral condyle → fibular head. NOT attached to lateral meniscus. Cord-like. Prevents varus stress. Taut in extension

Menisci:

  • Medial meniscus: C-shaped, larger, less mobile (attached to MCL and capsule). More commonly injured
  • Lateral meniscus: O-shaped (nearly circular), more mobile, not attached to LCL. Less commonly torn
  • Menisci are fibrocartilage; deepen tibial plateau; load distribution; shock absorption; stability
  • Avascular (inner 2/3) → poor healing; outer 1/3 has blood supply
O'Donoghue's Unhappy Triad

ACL + MCL + Medial meniscus — classic sports injury from valgus force with lateral rotation while foot planted (tackle from side). MCL is attached to medial meniscus → when MCL torn, it pulls medial meniscus with it. LCL is separate from lateral meniscus → lateral meniscus spared.

Investigation: MRI is gold standard. McMurray's test for meniscal tears.

Locking & Unlocking of Knee PYQ 2018
  • Locking: In last 10° of extension → tibia medially rotates on femur → all ligaments tighten → knee "screwed home" → no muscular energy needed to stand
  • Unlocking: Initiated by popliteus muscle — laterally rotates femur on fixed tibia (or medially rotates tibia on femur) → releases the screw-home mechanism → allows flexion to begin
  • "Popliteus unlocks the knee" — classic viva answer

Ankle Joint & Supporting Ligaments PYQ 2011, 2012 supple, 2013 supple, 2018

The ankle joint (talocrural joint) is a hinge-type synovial joint that allows dorsiflexion and plantarflexion of the foot. It is stabilized by medial and lateral ligaments.

Deltoid (Medial) Ligament:

  • Extremely strong, triangular-shaped ligament on the medial side of the ankle joint.
  • Attachments: Apex attaches to the medial malleolus of the tibia. Base attaches to the navicular bone anteriorly, the talus (neck and body), and the sustentaculum tali of the calcaneus inferiorly.
  • Function: Prevents over-eversion of the foot and stabilizes the medial side of the joint against displacement. Almost never tears; instead, strong eversion force causes avulsion of the medial malleolus (Pott's fracture).

Spring (Plantar Calcaneonavicular) Ligament:

  • A thick, fibroelastic band that connects the sustentaculum tali of the calcaneus to the plantar surface of the navicular bone.
  • Function: Supports the head of the talus, forming the articular socket for the talocalcaneonavicular joint. It is the key passive supporter of the medial longitudinal arch of the foot.
  • Clinical: Laxity or rupture of this ligament causes the head of the talus to drop downward and medially, resulting in flat foot deformity (pes planus).

Popliteus Muscle Details PYQ 2010, 2011 supple

A thin, flat, triangular muscle located deep in the popliteal fossa, forming the lower part of its floor.

Attachments:

  • Origin: A strong tendon from the **lateral condyle of the femur** (intracapsular but extra-synovial) and the posterior horn of the lateral meniscus.
  • Insertion: Triangular area on the posterior surface of the tibia, above the soleal line.

Actions & Nerve Supply:

  • Nerve Supply: Tibial nerve (L4, L5, S1).
  • Unlocking of the Knee (Prime Mover):
    - In weight-bearing (closed-chain, foot on ground): rotates the femur laterally on the fixed tibia.
    - In non-weight-bearing (open-chain, foot free): rotates the tibia medially on the femur.
    - Releasing the locked "screw-home" mechanism allows the knee to flex.
  • Secondary Actions: Weak flexion of the knee; pulls the lateral meniscus posteriorward during flexion to prevent entrapment.

Trendelenburg Sign & Gait

Mechanism: Gluteus medius (+ minimus) contract on the stance side → elevate opposite pelvis during single-leg support. If superior gluteal nerve or gluteus medius is weak → opposite pelvis drops → positive Trendelenburg sign.

Trendelenburg gait (lurch): Patient leans over to the weak side (to bring CoG over stance leg) → gluteus medius lurch.

Causes: Superior gluteal nerve damage, hip OA, coxa vara, CDH (congenital dislocation of hip), any condition weakening abductors.

Structures Deep to Gluteus Maximus ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2023

Reflection of the gluteus maximus exposes a multitude of anatomically vital structures in the gluteal region:

Muscles & Bones:

  • Gluteal Muscles: Gluteus medius and gluteus minimus.
  • Short Lateral Rotators: Piriformis, Gemellus superior, Obturator internus, Gemellus inferior, Quadratus femoris, Obturator externus.
  • Hamstring Origins: Long head of biceps femoris, semitendinosus, and semimembranosus (all from ischial tuberosity).
  • Adductor: Ischiocondylar portion of adductor magnus.
  • Bony Landmarks: Ilium, greater trochanter, and ischial tuberosity.

Nerves, Vessels & Ligaments:

  • Nerves: Sciatic nerve (prime clinical structure), Superior gluteal nerve, Inferior gluteal nerve, Pudendal nerve, Posterior cutaneous nerve of thigh, Nerve to obturator internus, Nerve to quadratus femoris.
  • Vessels: Superior gluteal vessels, Inferior gluteal vessels, and Internal pudendal vessels.
  • Ligaments: Sacrotuberous and Sacrospinous ligaments.
  • Bursae: Trochanteric, Ischial, and Gluteofemoral bursae.

Arches of the Foot & Foot Movements

The foot is not a flat block; it is a flexible arch structure designed to support body weight, act as a shock absorber, and act as a lever for propulsion during walking. The maintenance of these arches is a perennial exam favourite.
Skeleton of the foot — Medial aspect (medial longitudinal arch) Gray's Anatomy Plate 352 (Public Domain)
Arches of the Foot ⭐ PYQ 2013, 2017 supple, 2019 supple, 2022, 2024

The foot has three arches: two longitudinal (medial and lateral) and one transverse. They are maintained by three types of factors: bony factors (shapes of bones), passive factors (ligaments and aponeuroses), and dynamic factors (tendons and muscles).

Deformities — Flat Foot (Pes Planus) vs. Claw Foot (Pes Cavus)
  • Pes Planus (Flat Foot): Loss of the medial longitudinal arch, typically caused by stretching/rupture of the spring ligament or tibialis posterior tendon dysfunction. The talar head slips medially and downwards, placing the medial border of the foot flat on the ground.
  • Pes Cavus (Claw Foot): Exaggerated height of the medial longitudinal arch, often associated with neuromuscular conditions (e.g., Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease) or tight plantar fascia.
Inversion & Eversion of the Foot PYQ 2011 supple

These movements allow the foot to adapt to uneven terrain during walking.

Joints & Axis

  • Joints involved: Primarily the subtalar joint (between talus and calcaneus) and the talocalcaneonavicular joint (transverse tarsal joint).
  • Axis of movement: Runs obliquely forwards, upwards, and medially, passing from the lateral tubercle of the calcaneus to the neck of the talus.

Clubfoot (Talipes Equinovarus)

A congenital deformity where the foot is twisted downwards and inwards (plantarflexed, inverted, and adducted). It is managed using the Ponseti method (serial casting) to gradually stretch the tight soft tissues, especially the Achilles tendon and tibialis posterior tendon.

Femoral Sheath & Femoral Hernia PYQ 2017, 2022
  • Femoral sheath: Funnel-shaped fascial sleeve (continuation of transversalis + iliac fascia) enclosing femoral artery, vein, and canal (first 3–4 cm below inguinal ligament). Femoral nerve is OUTSIDE the sheath
  • Femoral canal: Most medial compartment of femoral sheath, ~1.25 cm long. Contains fat + Cloquet's node. Base = femoral ring (bounded by: inguinal ligament anteriorly, lacunar ligament medially, femoral vein laterally, pectineus posteriorly)
  • Femoral hernia: Bowel/omentum protrudes through femoral ring into femoral canal → emerges through saphenous opening → appears below and lateral to pubic tubercle
  • More common in females (wider femoral canal due to wider pelvis). Inguinal hernia still more common overall
  • Risk of strangulation HIGH — sharp lacunar ligament medially. Emergency surgery if incarcerated
Femoral vs Inguinal Hernia — Distinguish

Femoral hernia: Below and lateral to pubic tubercle; NOT reducible into scrotum; no cough impulse control; high strangulation risk.

Inguinal hernia: Above and medial to pubic tubercle; may enter scrotum (indirect); controlled by deep ring pressure.

Adductor Canal & Popliteal Fossa

Adductor Canal (Hunter's Canal / Subsartorial Canal) PYQ 2015, 2019

Boundaries:

  • Anterolateral wall: Vastus medialis
  • Posteromedial wall: Adductor longus (upper) → Adductor magnus (lower)
  • Roof (anterior): Sartorius + subsartorial fascia (vasoadductor membrane)
  • Extends: From apex of femoral triangle → adductor hiatus (opening in adductor magnus)

Contents:

  • Femoral artery (exits as popliteal artery at adductor hiatus)
  • Femoral vein (medial then posterior to artery)
  • Saphenous nerve (branch of femoral nerve — stays in thigh, exits canal through subsartorial fascia to supply medial leg and foot)
  • Nerve to vastus medialis (branch of femoral n.)
  • Note: femoral nerve itself does NOT enter adductor canal
The adductor canal is a surgical conduit — saphenous nerve block at this level produces anaesthesia of medial leg for surgery. Femoral artery becomes popliteal artery as it passes through the adductor hiatus.

Baker's Cyst (Popliteal Cyst)

Distension of the semimembranosus bursa (communicates with knee joint in adults) → swelling in medial part of popliteal fossa, best seen in extension. Associated with knee joint pathology (OA, RA, meniscal tear). Rupture → pain down calf, mimics DVT. Diagnose with ultrasound or MRI.

Nerves of the Lower Limb

Lumbar Plexus PYQ 2018

The lumbar plexus is a nervous network formed by the ventral rami of the first four lumbar spinal nerves (L1-L4) with a contribution from T12.

Formation & Location

  • Location: Formed within the substance of the psoas major muscle, anterior to the transverse processes of the lumbar vertebrae.
  • Roots: Ventral rami of L1, L2, L3, and L4, plus T12 (subcostal nerve) contribution.
  • Division of Rami: The rami of L2, L3, and L4 split into anterior and posterior divisions, which then unite to form the two main terminal nerves of the plexus.

Clinical Correlate: Psoas Abscess & Plexus Compression

Since the lumbar plexus is embedded inside the psoas major muscle, a psoas abscess (frequently due to tuberculosis of the spine / Pott's disease) can compress or irritate these nerves. This presents as radiating pain to the anterior/medial thigh and hip, with the patient holding the hip in flexion to relax the psoas muscle.

Dorsalis Pedis Artery PYQ 2019 supple

The chief artery of the dorsum of the foot, which is the direct continuation of the anterior tibial artery.

Course & Termination:

  • Origin: Begins in front of the ankle joint, midway between the two malleoli.
  • Course: Passes forward along the medial side of the dorsum of the foot, running lateral to the tendon of Extensor Hallucis Longus (EHL).
  • Termination: Ends at the proximal part of the first intermetatarsal space by dipping downwards (plantarward) between the two heads of the first dorsal interosseous muscle to complete the plantar arterial arch.

Dorsalis Pedis Pulse & Clinical:

  • Palpation: Easily palpated on the dorsum of the foot just lateral to the EHL tendon, over the tarsal bones (navicular/cuneiforms). The foot should be slightly dorsiflexed to relax the extensor tendons.
  • Clinical Significance: A weak or absent pulse indicates peripheral arterial disease (PAD) or acute arterial occlusion (embolism). Crucial clinical sign in diabetic patients (diabetic angiopathy).
Venous Drainage PYQ 2013, 2020

Superficial Veins:

  • Great (Long) saphenous vein: Starts at medial end of dorsal venous arch → medial malleolus → medial leg → medial knee → posteromedial thigh → drains into femoral vein at saphenous opening (4cm below and lateral to pubic tubercle). Accompanied by saphenous nerve in leg
  • Small (Short) saphenous vein: Lateral end of dorsal arch → behind lateral malleolus → posterior leg → drains into popliteal vein in popliteal fossa. Accompanied by sural nerve

Deep Veins + Clinical:

  • Deep veins accompany arteries (venae comitantes)
  • DVT: Posterior tibial and peroneal veins, soleal sinuses most commonly thrombosed. Risk: Virchow's triad (stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial damage). Homan's sign (calf pain on dorsiflexion) — not reliable
  • Varicose veins: Dilated tortuous superficial veins due to incompetent valves in perforating veins (or sapheno-femoral junction). Great saphenous territory most common
Inguinal Lymph Nodes

Superficial Inguinal Nodes:

  • Arranged in a T-shape below inguinal ligament.
  • Horizontal group: Drains lower anterior abdominal wall, perineum, external genitalia (except testes/ovary), lower anal canal.
  • Vertical group: Along terminal great saphenous vein. Drains the majority of the superficial lower limb.

Deep Inguinal Nodes:

  • Located medial to femoral vein (1-3 nodes).
  • Node of Cloquet: Highest node in the femoral canal.
  • Receive drainage from deep structures of leg, glans penis/clitoris, and efferents from superficial nodes.
  • Drain directly into External Iliac Nodes.

Viva & Exam Q&A

Joints & Bones
What is the blood supply to the head of the femur? Which vessel is most important and why?PYQ 2015, 2021

Blood supply to femoral head comes from three sources:

  • Medial circumflex femoral artery (MCFA) — branch of profunda femoris. Most important in adults. Retinacular vessels run along posterior neck of femur
  • Lateral circumflex femoral artery (LCFA) — minor contribution anteriorly
  • Artery of ligamentum teres (branch of obturator a.) — important only in children; negligible in adults

Why MCFA is most important: It provides the majority of blood to the femoral head in adults via retinacular vessels that pierce the capsule at the base of the neck. In an intracapsular femoral neck fracture (subcapital), these vessels are torn → AVN of femoral head. This is why intracapsular fractures are treated with hemiarthroplasty rather than fixation in the elderly.

What is the "unhappy triad" and why is the lateral meniscus spared?PYQ 2019

O'Donoghue's unhappy triad: ACL + MCL + Medial meniscus — injured by a valgus force with external rotation of tibia (lateral tackle in football).

Why lateral meniscus is spared: The MCL is firmly attached to the medial meniscus (they share a fibrous connection). When MCL is torn by the valgus force, it pulls and tears the medial meniscus with it. The LCL, by contrast, is NOT attached to the lateral meniscus — they are separated by the popliteus tendon. So lateral meniscus is free to move and escape injury.

Examiner tip: Always explain WHY medial (not lateral) meniscus — this is the discriminating point.

What is the screw-home mechanism of the knee?PYQ 2018

Locking mechanism (screw-home): In the last 10–15° of full knee extension, the tibia automatically rotates laterally (or femur rotates medially on fixed tibia) → all ligaments of knee tighten simultaneously → knee becomes rigid without muscular effort → stable standing position.

Anatomical basis: Medial femoral condyle is longer than lateral → when lateral condyle finishes rolling, medial condyle keeps moving → tibia spins laterally → locks.

Unlocking: Initiated solely by popliteus (lateral rotation of femur on fixed tibia) → releases all taut ligaments → flexion can begin.

Popliteus = "key" to unlocking the knee — classic viva line.

Spaces & Contents
Name the contents of the femoral triangle from lateral to medial.PYQ 2010, 2013, 2016, 2024

Lateral to medial: N–A–V–E–L

  • Nerve — femoral nerve (lateral to vessels, OUTSIDE femoral sheath)
  • Artery — femoral artery (inside femoral sheath)
  • Vein — femoral vein (inside femoral sheath)
  • Empty space — femoral canal (most medial compartment of sheath)
  • Lymphatics — within femoral canal; Cloquet's (Rosenmüller's) node

Critical: Femoral nerve is OUTSIDE the sheath. Femoral canal contains Cloquet's node. The femoral ring (mouth of canal) is the site of femoral hernia.

Which is the deepest structure in the popliteal fossa and why is this clinically important?PYQ 2014, 2023

The popliteal artery is the deepest structure (lies directly on the posterior capsule of the knee joint).

Clinical importance:

  • In supracondylar fracture of femur → posterior displacement of distal fragment → popliteal artery is trapped and may be torn → ischaemia of leg
  • Posteriorly dislocated knee → popliteal artery injury common
  • Popliteal aneurysm → thrombosis/embolism to leg
  • Because the artery is deepest, it is the last structure found when incising the fossa from behind (surgeon must be careful)

Order: From superficial to deep: Tibial nerve → Popliteal vein → Popliteal artery. "Nerve-Vein-Artery" (NVA) — deepest last.

Nerves & Clinical
Describe foot drop — cause, mechanism, clinical features, and gait.PYQ 2011, 2016, 2019, 2024

Cause: Injury to common peroneal nerve at the neck of the fibula (most vulnerable point — subcutaneous, winds around bone).

Mechanism: Common peroneal → deep peroneal (dorsiflexors: TA, EHL, EDL) + superficial peroneal (everters: peroneus longus, brevis). Both divisions lost → no dorsiflexion, no eversion.

Clinical features:

  • Foot hangs in plantarflexion + inversion (unopposed tibialis posterior)
  • Sensory loss: dorsum of foot, lateral leg (except narrow strip of lateral margin of foot = sural nerve = tibial)
  • Ankle jerk preserved (tibial nerve = S1,S2 — not affected)

Gait — High-stepping (Steppage) gait: Patient over-flexes hip and knee to prevent toe drag → leg lifted high → foot slaps ground on landing.

Distinguish from L4/L5 disc lesion: Root lesion = pain + dermatomal sensory loss; common peroneal = no pain, no hamstring weakness, no knee jerk change.

Why is referred pain felt in the knee in hip joint disease?PYQ 2016, 2021

This is an application of Hilton's Law: A nerve supplying a joint also supplies the muscles that move that joint AND the skin over the insertion of those muscles.

Obturator nerve (L2,3,4) supplies:

  • Hip joint (articular branch from anterior division)
  • Knee joint (articular branch — supplies medial aspect)
  • Adductor muscles of thigh

When hip joint is diseased (OA, septic arthritis, Perthes disease) → afferent pain impulses travel via obturator nerve → same nerve supplies knee → central nervous system interprets pain as originating from knee → referred pain to medial knee.

Clinical trap: Child presenting with knee pain → always examine the hip. Hip pathology is often missed because of referred knee pain.

What is Trendelenburg's sign? What are its causes?PYQ 2017, 2022

Trendelenburg sign: When a patient stands on the affected limb (single-leg stance), the pelvis on the OPPOSITE (normal) side drops instead of rising. Positive = pelvis falls on the side opposite the affected limb.

Mechanism: Normally, gluteus medius/minimus contract on the stance side to elevate (abduct) the pelvis → allows swing leg to clear the ground. If these muscles are weak → pelvis cannot be held up → drops to contralateral side.

Trendelenburg gait (gluteus medius lurch): Patient tilts trunk OVER the weak side to shift CoG over the stance limb → reduces abductor moment needed.

Causes:

  • Superior gluteal nerve damage (L4,5,S1)
  • Coxa vara (muscle mechanical disadvantage)
  • Hip OA / dislocation (abnormal fulcrum)
  • Poliomyelitis affecting gluteus medius
  • CDH (Congenital Dislocation of Hip)
Clinical "Explain Why" Questions
Explain why all muscles of the hamstring compartment are not "true" hamstrings.PYQ 2011 supple

A true hamstring muscle must satisfy four criteria: (1) originate from the ischial tuberosity, (2) insert into one of the bones of the leg (tibia or fibula), (3) cross both the hip and knee joints (biarticular), and (4) be supplied by the tibial component of the sciatic nerve.

The short head of the biceps femoris fails three of these criteria: it originates from the lateral lip of the linea aspera of the femur (not ischial tuberosity), crosses only the knee joint (uniarticular), and is supplied by the common peroneal component of the sciatic nerve. Thus, it is not a true hamstring.

Explain why the soleus muscle is referred to as the "peripheral heart."PYQ 2019 supple, 2020

The soleus muscle contains large, thin-walled venous sinuses (soleal sinuses) that hold a significant volume of blood. During walking, the contraction of the soleus compresses these sinuses, actively pumping venous blood upwards against gravity into the deep veins of the leg. The valves in the deep veins prevent retrograde flow. This muscle-pump action is crucial for venous return, acting as a "peripheral heart." Prolonged immobility inactivates this pump, leading to venous stasis and Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT).

Explain why ankle sprains occur more commonly during plantarflexion than dorsiflexion.PYQ 2023

This is due to the shape of the trochlea of the talus, which is wider anteriorly than posteriorly:

  • Dorsiflexion: The wider anterior part of the talus fits tightly into the tibiofibular mortise, making the joint highly stable.
  • Plantarflexion: The narrower posterior part of the talus lies loosely within the mortise, allowing side-to-side (wobbling) movements. In this unstable position, inversion forces easily exceed the strength of the lateral ligaments, leading to the common **inversion ankle sprain** (anterior talofibular ligament tear).
Explain why the great saphenous vein is preferred for Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (CABG).PYQ 2024

The great saphenous vein is highly preferred for CABG for several reasons:

  • Accessibility & Length: It is easily accessible subcutaneously, and its long, continuous length allows multiple graft segments to be harvested.
  • Wall Structure: It has a thick wall with plenty of elastic and muscular fibers, allowing it to adapt to high arterial pressures (arterialization) when grafted.
  • Low Morbidity: Harvesting it from the leg causes minimal long-term circulatory disturbance because deep veins handle the bulk of venous return.
Explain the medicolegal importance of the secondary ossification centers around the knee.PYQ 2013 supple

The secondary ossification centers at the distal end of the femur and proximal end of the tibia appear at specific developmental milestones:

  • Distal femoral center appears at **9 months of intrauterine life** (just before birth).
  • Proximal tibial center appears at **birth/full term**.

In forensic autopsies of newborn infants, radiological or histological demonstration of these centers is key medicolegal evidence to prove that the infant was **full-term** and viable at birth, helping rule out criminal abortion or prematurity as a cause of death.

Explain why the patella has a natural tendency to dislocate laterally, and how this is prevented.PYQ 2025

The lateral dislocation tendency is due to the **Q-angle** (quadriceps angle) — the line of pull of the quadriceps muscle is oblique, whereas the ligamentum patellae is vertical. When the quadriceps contracts, it pulls the patella laterally (valgus vector).

Prevention mechanisms:

  • Bony: The lateral condyle of the femur has a higher, more prominent anterior projection (lateral patellar lip) that acts as a physical barrier.
  • Muscular: The lowermost fibers of the Vastus Medialis Obliquus (VMO) insert directly into the medial border of the patella, actively pulling it medially during extension to counteract the lateral pull.

EQ — Describe the Femoral Triangle [PYQ 2010, 2013, 2016, 2018, 2024]
"Describe the boundaries, floor, roof, and contents of the femoral triangle. Add a note on the femoral sheath."
  1. Definition: The femoral triangle is a subfascial triangular space in the upper medial thigh, bounded by the inguinal ligament (superiorly/base), medial border of sartorius (laterally), and medial border of adductor longus (medially). Apex: where sartorius and adductor longus converge (~level of adductor canal).
  2. Boundaries — Details: Base = inguinal ligament (ASIS to pubic tubercle). Lateral = sartorius (extends from ASIS to pes anserinus). Medial = medial border of adductor longus. Floor (from lateral to medial) = iliopsoas + pectineus + adductor longus. Roof = fascia lata (with saphenous opening) + cribriform fascia over opening.
  3. Contents (lateral to medial — NAVEL): Femoral Nerve (outside femoral sheath, branches immediately into multiple motor/sensory twigs); Femoral Artery (enters at mid-inguinal point, gives profunda femoris ~3.5cm below); Femoral Vein (receives great saphenous vein at saphenous opening); Empty space / femoral canal (contains fat, lymphatics, Cloquet's node); Lymphatics (deep inguinal nodes).
  4. Femoral Sheath: Funnel-shaped fascial tube (derived from transversalis and iliac fascia) enclosing the femoral artery, vein, and canal for ~3–4cm below the inguinal ligament. Divided into 3 compartments by vertical septa: lateral (artery), middle (vein), medial (femoral canal). Femoral nerve is OUTSIDE the sheath.
  5. Femoral Canal: Most medial compartment of femoral sheath. ~1.25cm long. Contains fat and lymphatics (Cloquet's node). Its upper opening = femoral ring (bounded by inguinal ligament anteriorly, lacunar ligament medially, femoral vein laterally, pectineus/pectineal ligament posteriorly). Site of femoral hernia.
  6. Clinical Significance: (a) Femoral pulse at mid-inguinal point — used in cardiac catheterization, femoral nerve block. (b) Femoral hernia exits through femoral ring/canal — more common in females due to wider pelvis; high strangulation risk due to rigid lacunar ligament medially. (c) Great saphenous vein cutdown for IV access. (d) Cloquet's node enlargement in lower limb/perineal infections or melanoma.
EQ — Sciatic Nerve [PYQ 2012, 2015, 2017, 2020, 2023]
"Describe the sciatic nerve: origin, course, branches, and effects of injury."
  1. Origin: Largest peripheral nerve in the body. Arises from the sacral plexus — from L4, L5, S1, S2, S3 (both anterior and posterior divisions). Actually two nerves (tibial from anterior divisions + common peroneal from posterior divisions) enclosed in a common epineurial sheath.
  2. Course in Gluteal Region: Leaves pelvis through greater sciatic foramen, below piriformis. Lies between ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter (midpoint = surface marking). Deep to gluteus maximus. Crosses over obturator internus, gemelli, quadratus femoris (posterior to these muscles).
  3. Course in Posterior Thigh: Descends vertically in posterior thigh under gluteus maximus, medial to biceps femoris. Gives branches to all hamstrings (tibial component: long head BF, semimembranosus, semitendinosus; common peroneal component: short head BF) and adductor magnus (posterior part). Reaches upper angle of popliteal fossa.
  4. Termination: Divides (at popliteal fossa, or higher as variant) into: (a) Tibial nerve (medial popliteal nerve) — L4–S3 — descends vertically, supplies posterior compartment of leg, enters sole → medial + lateral plantar nerves. (b) Common peroneal nerve (lateral popliteal nerve) — L4–S2 — winds around neck of fibula → deep peroneal (anterior compartment + dorsum of foot) + superficial peroneal (lateral compartment).
  5. Effects of High Injury (gluteal region, e.g., misplaced injection): (a) Motor: loss of all knee flexion (hamstrings), all movements below knee (foot drop, loss of plantarflexion, toe movements). (b) Sensory: loss from below knee except medial leg and foot (saphenous nerve — from femoral n.). (c) Hip extension partially lost if proximal enough. (d) Total disability below the knee with wasted hamstrings.
  6. Safe Zone for Gluteal Injection: Upper outer quadrant of buttock. Alternative: ventrogluteal region (Hochstetter's technique). NEVER inject in lower medial quadrant — sciatic nerve at highest risk there.
EQ — Hip Joint [PYQ 2013, 2017, 2021]
"Describe the hip joint under the headings of: type, articular surfaces, ligaments, blood supply, nerve supply, and clinical anatomy."
  1. Type & Articular Surfaces: Ball-and-socket synovial joint; multiaxial. Femoral head (2/3 of sphere) fits into acetabulum (deepened by fibrocartilaginous acetabular labrum). Acetabulum faces anterolaterally and inferiorly. Articular cartilage on femoral head (horseshoe-shaped, absent at fovea) and acetabular lunate surface.
  2. Capsule: Thick, strong fibrous capsule. Attaches to acetabular rim + transverse acetabular ligament proximally → intertrochanteric line anteriorly + midway up femoral neck posteriorly (posterior neck partially extracapsular → significance in fractures). Intracapsular fat pad at acetabular fossa.
  3. Ligaments: (a) Iliofemoral (Y-ligament of Bigelow) — strongest in body, prevents hyperextension. (b) Pubofemoral — prevents excess abduction + extension. (c) Ischiofemoral — weakest, prevents medial rotation. (d) Ligamentum teres — intracapsular, carries artery to head of femur (significant in children). (e) Acetabular labrum — fibrocartilaginous, deepens socket. (f) Transverse acetabular ligament — bridges acetabular notch.
  4. Blood Supply to Femoral Head: Main supply = medial circumflex femoral artery (profunda femoris branch) via retinacular vessels along neck. Minor: lateral circumflex femoral a. Artery in ligamentum teres (obturator a.) significant only in children. Intracapsular fracture → MCFA torn → AVN of femoral head.
  5. Nerve Supply (Hilton's Law): Femoral nerve (L2,3,4), Obturator nerve (L2,3,4), Sciatic nerve (L4,5,S1,S2), Superior gluteal nerve (L4,5,S1). Obturator nerve also supplies medial knee → referred pain from hip disease to knee.
  6. Clinical: (a) Posterior dislocation (90%) — forced flexion + adduction; leg shortened, flexed, adducted, medially rotated; sciatic nerve risk. (b) Anterior dislocation — leg extended, abducted, laterally rotated; femoral nerve/vessels at risk. (c) Intracapsular fracture (subcapital) → AVN → treat elderly with hemiarthroplasty. (d) Extracapsular (intertrochanteric) — MCFA intact → fixation possible. (e) OA of hip → referred knee pain, groin pain, fixed flexion deformity.
