Bony Pelvis

The pelvis is formed by 2 hip bones + sacrum + coccyx. The pelvic brim (linea terminalis) divides the false pelvis (above, part of abdomen) from the true pelvis (below). Obstetric measurements of the true pelvis determine the possibility of normal vaginal delivery.

Pelvic Inlet, Cavity & Outlet — Obstetric Diameters ⭐ PYQ Every Year

Pelvic Inlet (Brim) — Bounded by:

  • Posteriorly: Sacral promontory (S1)
  • Laterally: Arcuate line of ilium + pectineal line of pubis
  • Anteriorly: Pubic crest + pubic symphysis upper border

Inlet Diameters:

  • Anteroposterior (True conjugate): Sacral promontory → upper border of pubic symphysis = 11 cm
  • Obstetric conjugate: Sacral promontory → posterior surface of pubic symphysis = 10.5 cm (smallest AP diameter — head must pass this)
  • Diagonal conjugate: Sacral promontory → lower border of pubic symphysis = 12 cm (only one measurable clinically by internal examination)
  • Transverse diameter: Widest transverse distance = 13 cm (widest at inlet)
  • Oblique diameter: SI joint → iliopubic eminence opposite = 12 cm

Pelvic Outlet — Bounded by:

  • Anteriorly: Pubic arch (subpubic angle)
  • Laterally: Ischial tuberosities + sacrotuberous ligaments
  • Posteriorly: Coccyx (tip)

Outlet Diameters:

  • AP diameter (outlet): Lower border of pubic symphysis → tip of coccyx = 13 cm (increases during delivery as coccyx moves back)
  • Transverse diameter: Between inner surfaces of ischial tuberosities = 11 cm

Subpubic Angle:

  • Female: 80–85° (wide — accommodates fetal head)
  • Male: 50–60° (narrow)
Obstetric Conjugate — Most Important
"10.5 = the gate the baby must pass" — Obstetric conjugate is the shortest AP diameter of pelvic inlet = critical for engagement of fetal head

Pelvic Floor & Diaphragm

The pelvic floor (pelvic diaphragm) is a musculofascial hammock that closes the pelvic outlet, supports pelvic viscera, and maintains continence. It is formed by levator ani + coccygeus muscles and their fascial coverings.

Pelvic Floor Dysfunction

Levator ani injury during childbirth → weakened puborectal sling → increased anorectal angle → stress urinary incontinence + faecal incontinence. Also causes uterine/vaginal prolapse (levator plate descends). Pelvic floor exercises (Kegel) strengthen levator ani.

Pelvic Fascia & Spaces PYQ 2016, 2021

Parietal Pelvic Fascia:

  • Lines walls of true pelvis
  • Obturator fascia covers obturator internus; gives origin to levator ani (ATLA)
  • Piriformis fascia covers piriformis
  • Thickened to form puboprostatic ligament (male) / pubovesical ligament (female) — anchor bladder neck

Visceral Pelvic Fascia:

  • Covers pelvic organs as condensations
  • Presacral space (Waldeyer's fascia): Between rectum and sacrum — contains presacral vessels (middle sacral artery) — dangerous in rectal surgery
  • Rectovesical septum (Denonvilliers' fascia): Between rectum and bladder/prostate/vagina — important in prostatectomy
03

Perineum

The perineum is the region below the pelvic floor (pelvic diaphragm), between the thighs. It is diamond-shaped — divided by a transverse line through the ischial tuberosities into two triangles: urogenital triangle (anterior) and anal triangle (posterior).
Boundaries & Triangles PYQ 2012, 2016, 2019

Perineal Diamond — Boundaries:

  • Anterior: Pubic symphysis lower border
  • Anterolateral: Ischiopubic rami
  • Posterolateral: Sacrotuberous ligaments
  • Posterior: Tip of coccyx
  • Lateral corners: Ischial tuberosities

Anal Triangle:

  • Posterior; contains anal canal + external anal sphincter
  • Ischioanal (ischiorectal) fossa on each side of anal canal — fat-filled, wedge-shaped space. Site of perianal abscess
  • Boundaries of ischioanal fossa: lateral = ischium + obturator internus; medial = levator ani + external sphincter; roof = pelvic diaphragm; base = skin
  • Contents: Ischioanal fat + inferior rectal nerve/vessels + pudendal canal (Alcock's canal) on lateral wall

Urogenital Triangle:

  • Anterior; closed by urogenital diaphragm (perineal membrane)
  • Contains external urethral sphincter, external genitalia roots

Perineal Body (Central Tendon of Perineum):

  • Fibromuscular node at centre of perineum, between anal canal and vagina/bulb of penis
  • 9 muscles meet here: External anal sphincter, Bulbospongiosus (×2), Superficial transverse perineal (×2), Deep transverse perineal (×2), Puborectalis (from levator ani), Pubococcygeus fibres
  • Critical support structure for pelvic viscera
  • Torn in childbirth → perineal tear / episiotomy repair must reconstruct perineal body

Extravasation of Urine — Colles' Fascia Rule

Rupture of spongy urethra (bulbar) → urine enters superficial perineal pouch and spreads under Colles' fascia. Colles' fascia is attached: laterally to ischiopubic rami (stops lateral spread), posteriorly at posterior border of UG triangle (stops posterior spread into anal triangle). Urine spreads: anteriorly into scrotum → under Dartos fascia → into penis → up anterior abdominal wall under Scarpa's fascia. Does NOT enter thigh (fascia lata attachment). "Butterfly bruise/swelling" pattern.

Ischiorectal (Ischioanal) Fossa & Painful Abscess ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2015
The ischiorectal fossa is a bilateral, fat-filled, wedge-shaped space located on either side of the anal canal below the pelvic diaphragm. It allows expansion of the anal canal during defecation.

Anatomical Boundaries:

  • Base (Inferior): Perineal skin of the anal region.
  • Apex (Superior): Meeting point of the obturator fascia and the inferior fascia of the pelvic diaphragm.
  • Lateral Wall: Ischial tuberosity and obturator internus muscle covered by obturator fascia.
  • Medial Wall: Levator ani muscle and external anal sphincter.
  • Anterior: Posterior border of the urogenital diaphragm (and its anterior recesses).
  • Posterior: Sacrotuberous ligament and gluteus maximus muscle.

Contents:

  • Ischiorectal Fat Pad: Large mass of lobulated fat tissue that acts as a flexible support. It has poor blood supply, making it highly susceptible to infection.
  • Pudendal (Alcock's) Canal: A fascial tunnel on the lateral wall (within the obturator internus fascia) containing the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels.
  • Inferior Rectal Vessels & Nerve: Arise from the pudendal nerve/vessels within the canal, crossing the fossa medially to supply the sphincter ani externus and lower anal canal.
  • Perineal branch of S4 nerve: Enters the posterior part of the fossa.

Ischiorectal Abscess & Severe Pain Clinical Basis

Pathogenesis: Infections usually originate from blocked and inflamed anal glands at the pectinate line (cryptitis), which track laterally through the external anal sphincter into the loose fat of the ischiorectal fossa, forming an abscess.

Clinical Features & Basis of Pain:

  • Severe, Throbbing Pain: The ischiorectal fossa and the perineal skin are supplied by the inferior rectal nerve (S2–S4), which carries somatic sensory fibers. Unlike the visceral sensation of the upper rectum, somatic nerves have a high density of pain receptors. The pressure of expanding pus against the rigid bony lateral walls and the pelvic floor causes excruciating pain, worsened by sitting or defecating.
  • Surgical Treatment (Incision & Drainage): The abscess must be drained surgically. A cruciate incision is made close to the anal margin.
    Surgical Warnings:
    • Lateral Wall: Do not go too deep laterally to avoid injuring the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels in Alcock's canal.
    • Medial Wall: Avoid injuring the inferior rectal nerve or the external anal sphincter, which would result in permanent fecal incontinence.

Anal Canal

The anal canal is 4 cm long, from the anorectal junction (puborectal sling level) to the anus. The pectinate (dentate) line at its middle is the single most important anatomical landmark — it is the embryological junction between hindgut endoderm and proctodeum ectoderm.

Above vs Below Pectinate Line
"Above: Columnar, Portal, Internal iliac nodes, Painless (autonomic). Below: Squamous, Systemic, Inguinal nodes, Painful (somatic)"

Why internal haemorrhoids are painless: above pectinate line → autonomic nerve supply → no pain sensation. Why they bleed bright red: rich arterial supply from superior rectal artery.

Hilton's White Line & Hemorrhoid Sites PYQ 2017, 2012-S

Hilton's White Line (Inter-sphincteric Groove): A landmark located at the lower third of the anal canal. It represents the junction between the involuntary internal anal sphincter and the voluntary subcutaneous external anal sphincter.
Significance: It represents a watershed line where the lining changes from non-keratinized stratified squamous (ectodermal) to true skin (keratinized). Visually, it is bluish-white due to the underlying venous plexus, and it is easily palpable during rectal examination.

Primary Sites of Internal Hemorrhoids (3, 7, 11 o'clock): Internal hemorrhoids (piles) are dilatations of the tributaries of the superior rectal vein. When viewed in the lithotomy position, they occur in three main positions:

  • 3 o'clock: Left lateral position.
  • 7 o'clock: Right posterolateral position.
  • 11 o'clock: Right anterolateral position.

Anatomical Explanation: The superior rectal artery divides into a left branch (runs on the left lateral wall → 3 o'clock) and a right branch (which bifurcates into right anterior → 11 o'clock, and right posterior → 7 o'clock branches). The venous radicles correspond directly to these branches. Secondary hemorrhoids can occur in between, but primary piles are always at 3, 7, and 11 o'clock.

Anal Sphincters PYQ 2014, 2019, 2023

Internal Anal Sphincter (IAS):

  • Thickening of circular smooth muscle of rectum
  • Involuntary — autonomic control
  • Normally tonically contracted (at rest = closed)
  • Sympathetic: contracts (maintains closure)
  • Parasympathetic (S2,3,4): relaxes IAS → defaecation
  • Contributes ~85% of resting anal tone
  • Extends from anorectal junction → just above pectinate line

External Anal Sphincter (EAS):

  • Skeletal (striated) muscle — voluntary control
  • 3 parts: Subcutaneous, Superficial, Deep
  • Nerve: Inferior rectal nerve (branch of pudendal — S2,3,4)
  • Deep part = continuous with puborectalis of levator ani
  • Surrounds IAS externally
Continence Mechanism

Anorectal angle (~90°) maintained by puborectal sling is the PRIMARY mechanism of faecal continence. IAS maintains resting tone. EAS provides voluntary squeeze. Distension of rectum → recto-anal inhibitory reflex (RAIR) → IAS relaxes → EAS voluntarily contracts → sampling of rectal contents without full defaecation.

Columns of Morgagni: 6–10 vertical folds of mucosa in upper half of anal canal. At their lower ends, adjacent columns are connected by anal valves — the small crescentic folds forming the pectinate line. Between valves = anal sinuses/crypts — infected crypts → perianal abscess → fistula-in-ano.

Male Pelvic Organs

Prostate Gland ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2014, 2017, 2019, 2022
The prostate is a fibromuscular glandular organ surrounding the proximal urethra in the male. Size of a chestnut, weighs ~20 g. Located in the true pelvis, between bladder neck (above) and urogenital diaphragm (below). Entirely inferior to the peritoneum — extraperitoneal.

Anatomy:

  • Base: Superior — applied to bladder neck
  • Apex: Inferior — rests on UG diaphragm
  • Posterior surface: Related to rectum (anterior rectal wall) — palpable on PR examination; median sulcus palpable between lateral lobes
  • Anterior surface: Related to pubic symphysis (separated by retropubic space of Retzius)
  • Urethra passes vertically through the gland (prostatic urethra)
  • Ejaculatory ducts enter posterosuperiorly, open on verumontanum

McNeal's Zones:

  • Peripheral zone (70%): Most of posterior + lateral — site of carcinoma prostate → palpable on PR
  • Central zone (25%): Around ejaculatory ducts — less affected
  • Transitional zone (5%): Around proximal urethra — site of BPH → urethral obstruction

Relations of Prostatic Urethra:

  • Verumontanum (colliculus seminalis) on posterior wall — ejaculatory ducts open here
  • Utricle prostataticus: Small blind pouch on verumontanum — Müllerian remnant
  • Prostatic sinuses on each side of verumontanum — openings of prostatic glands
BPH vs Carcinoma Prostate

BPH: Transitional zone hyperplasia → compresses urethra → hesitancy, poor stream, post-void dribble, nocturia. Treated with alpha-blockers (relax smooth muscle) or 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (shrink gland). Ca Prostate: Peripheral zone → not felt as urethral obstruction early; palpable hard nodule on PR; spreads to pelvic lymph nodes + bones (osteosclerotic mets). PSA elevated.

Capsule & Nerve Supply:

  • True capsule (fibromuscular) + false capsule (pelvic fascia)
  • Neurovascular bundle of Walsh runs posterolaterally — must be preserved in nerve-sparing prostatectomy to preserve erectile function
  • Sympathetic: contracts smooth muscle (ejaculation)
  • Parasympathetic: secretomotor (S2,3,4)

Prostate Microanatomy (Histology) PYQ 2024

  • Glandular Component (2/3): Consists of 30–50 branched tubuloalveolar glands arranged in mucosal, submucosal, and main layers. Secretions drain via 15–30 ducts into prostatic sinuses.
  • Epithelial Lining: Pseudostratified columnar or simple columnar epithelium, with basal cells. Secretory cells produce prostatic fluid (citric acid, acid phosphatase, zinc, and PSA).
  • Fibromuscular Stroma (1/3): Dense connective tissue rich in collagen and smooth muscle fibers surrounding the glands. Smooth muscle contracts under sympathetic control to empty secretions during ejaculation.
  • Corpora Amylacea (Amyloid Bodies): Characteristic concentric lamellated proteinaceous concretions (calcified amyloid-like bodies) in the glandular lumina. They increase progressively with age.
Prostatic Venous Plexus & Vertebral Metastasis (Batson's Plexus) PYQ 2010, 2024

Venous Drainage Pathway: The prostate is surrounded by a rich prostatic venous plexus (located between the true and false capsules). This plexus drains into the internal iliac veins.

Batson's Plexus Connection: The prostatic venous plexus communicates directly with the valveless internal vertebral venous plexus (Batson's plexus) via the pelvic veins (lateral sacral veins).

Pathological Basis of Bone Metastasis: Because these venous channels are valveless, any increase in intra-abdominal pressure (e.g., during defecation, coughing, or straining) causes retrograde blood flow from the pelvis to the spinal columns. Cancer cells from prostatic carcinoma can embolize via this pathway directly to the vertebral bodies (lumbar vertebrae, sacrum, and thoracic spine) and the skull, bypassing the systemic capillary beds (heart/lungs). This leads to characteristic osteoblastic (osteosclerotic) metastases in the vertebrae.

Vas Deferens — Anatomy, Histology & Development PYQ 2013-S
The vas (ductus) deferens is a thick-walled, cord-like muscular tube (45 cm long) that transports mature spermatozoa from the epididymis to the ejaculatory duct.

Anatomical Course:

  • Origin: Begins at the tail of the epididymis at the lower pole of the testis.
  • Spermatic Cord & Inguinal Canal: Ascends vertically in the posterior part of the spermatic cord, traverses the inguinal canal (external ring to internal ring).
  • Pelvic Course: Enters the pelvis by crossing over the external iliac vessels. Runs downwards and backwards on the lateral pelvic wall, crossing superior to the ureter ("water under the bridge", where the ureter is the water and vas is the bridge).
  • Termination: Expands to form the ampulla on the posterior bladder wall. It then narrows and joins the duct of the seminal vesicle to form the ejaculatory duct, which penetrates the prostate to open into the prostatic urethra.

Microanatomy (Histology):

  • Mucosa: Lined by pseudostratified columnar epithelium with stereocilia (non-motile microvilli that absorb residual fluids). The lumen has a star-shaped appearance due to longitudinal mucosal folds.
  • Muscularis (Three Layers): Extremely thick, making the duct feel cord-like on palpation. It consists of:
    1. Inner longitudinal layer
    2. Middle circular layer
    3. Outer longitudinal layer
  • Adventitia: Fibrous connective tissue enclosing the vessels and nerves.

Embryological Development:

  • Develops from the mesonephric (Wolffian) duct under the influence of testosterone.
  • Other mesonephric duct derivatives: epididymis, seminal vesicles, and ejaculatory duct.

Vasectomy

A surgical method of permanent male sterilization. The vas deferens is isolated within the scrotum by palpating its firm, cord-like structure. A small incision is made, and the vas is exposed, cut, and the ends are ligated. Sperm continue to be produced by the testes but are blocked from reaching the urethra and are safely resorbed. There is no change in semen volume (as 95% of semen is produced by the seminal vesicles and prostate).

Urinary Bladder PYQ 2013, 2018

Anatomy:

  • Muscular hollow organ — stores urine; capacity ~500 mL (desire at 150 mL)
  • Parts: Apex (superior, connected to median umbilical ligament = obliterated urachus), Body, Base/Fundus (posterior), Neck (inferoposterior → continues as urethra)
  • Trigone: Triangular smooth area at base — between ureteral orifices + internal urethral orifice. No rugae. Always smooth (even when empty)
  • Detrusor muscle (smooth muscle) — contracts during micturition (parasympathetic S2,3,4)

Relations — Male:

  • Superior: Peritoneum (covers top of bladder) → loops of sigmoid + ileum in rectovesical pouch
  • Posterior: Rectovesical pouch + seminal vesicles + vas deferens + rectum
  • Inferior/posterior: Prostate (bladder neck → prostate)
Urachal Anomalies

Urachus = remnant of allantois connecting bladder apex to umbilicus → normally obliterates to form median umbilical ligament. Patent urachus → urine discharge at umbilicus. Urachal cyst → midline swelling. Urachal sinus → discharges at umbilicus. Urachal carcinoma (adenocarcinoma at dome of bladder).

Male Urethra & Hypospadias PYQ 2018
The male urethra (18–20 cm) is a shared passage for urine and semen. It is divided into four main parts: preprostatic, prostatic, membranous, and spongy (penile).

Four Parts of Male Urethra:

  • Preprostatic (Intramural) (1–1.5 cm): Lies within the bladder neck, surrounded by the internal urethral sphincter.
  • Prostatic part (3 cm): Passes through the prostate gland. Widest and most dilatable part. Opens to the ejaculatory ducts, prostatic utricle, and prostatic sinuses on the verumontanum.
  • Membranous part (1.5 cm): Passes through the deep perineal pouch, surrounded by the voluntary external urethral sphincter. Narrowest and least dilatable part (at risk of rupture in pelvic fractures).
  • Spongy (Penile) part (15 cm): Longest part, runs through the corpus spongiosum of the penis. Dilates distally in the glans penis as the navicular fossa. Opens at the external urethral orifice.

Development & Congenital Hypospadias:

  • Embryological Origin: The prostatic (except upper part), membranous, and proximal spongy urethra develop from the endodermal urogenital sinus. The distal-most glandular urethra (in glans) develops from the ectodermal glandular plate.
  • Hypospadias: A congenital condition where the external urethral orifice opens on the ventral (inferior) aspect of the penis or scrotum/perineum.
    Embryological Basis: Failure of the bilateral urethral (cloacal) folds to fuse completely along the ventral aspect of the phallus.
    Surgical Note: Circumcision is strictly contraindicated in these infants, as the prepuce (foreskin) is required for reconstructive urethroplasty.
Urethral Rupture: Membranous vs Spongy (Bulbar)

Membranous Urethra Rupture: Usually caused by pelvic fractures (shear force at the urogenital diaphragm). Urine extravasates into the deep perineal pouch and pelvic cavity (extraperitoneal space of Retzius).

Spongy Urethra Rupture: Usually caused by a "straddle injury" (falling astride a beam, crushing the bulbous urethra). Urine leaks into the superficial perineal pouch and tracks under Colles' fascia into the scrotum and up the anterior abdominal wall. It does not enter the thigh due to fascia lata attachments.

Female Pelvic Organs

Ovary PYQ 2012, 2016, 2020

Anatomy:

  • Almond-shaped, ~4 × 2 × 1 cm, lies in ovarian fossa on lateral pelvic wall
  • Ovarian fossa boundaries: Anterior = obliterated umbilical artery; Posterior = ureter + internal iliac vessels; Superior = external iliac vein
  • Not covered by peritoneum (unlike all other pelvic organs) — covered by germinal epithelium (cuboidal)
  • Attached to posterior layer of broad ligament by mesovarium

Supports / Ligaments:

  • Mesovarium: Attaches ovary to broad ligament
  • Ovarian ligament (ligament of ovary): Connects medial pole of ovary to uterus (below uterine tube) — remnant of gubernaculum
  • Suspensory ligament of ovary (infundibulopelvic ligament): Connects lateral pole of ovary to pelvic wall — contains ovarian vessels + lymphatics + nerves

Blood Supply & Lymphatics:

  • Ovarian artery from abdominal aorta at L2 (like testicular)
  • Lymphatics drain to para-aortic lymph nodes at L2 (same as testis — embryological)
  • NOT to inguinal nodes

Development & Incomplete Descent PYQ 2019-S, 2024

  • Embryonic Origin: Develops from the gonadal (genital) ridge on the posterior abdominal wall (T10 level).
  • Descent: Descends into the pelvis guided by the gubernaculum (a fibromuscular band connecting the gonad to the labium majus).
  • Incomplete Descent: The gubernaculum attaches to the junction of the uterine tube and uterus. The portion of the gubernaculum between the ovary and uterus becomes the ovarian ligament, and the portion between the uterus and labium majus becomes the round ligament of the uterus. This attachment halts the descent, anchoring the ovary in the ovarian fossa of the pelvis.
  • Ectopic Ovary: Failure of descent can leave the ovary in the abdomen, or it may follow the gubernaculum too far into the inguinal canal or labium majus.

Graafian Follicle Histology PYQ 2019-S

  • Primary Oocyte: Located centrally or eccentrically, surrounded by the zona pellucida (glycoprotein layer) and the corona radiata (radial granulosa cells).
  • Antrum: A large central cavity filled with liquor folliculi (rich in hormones, proteoglycans).
  • Cumulus Oophorus: A mound of granulosa cells anchoring the oocyte to the wall. Stratum granulosum lines the follicle.
  • Theca Interna: Inner cellular, highly vascular layer. Converts cholesterol to androgens (under LH control).
  • Theca Externa: Outer fibrous layer, blending with the ovarian stroma.
Ovarian Referred Pain PYQ 2018-S, 2019-S

Afferent Pain Pathway: Pain fibers from the ovary travel via the ovarian plexus (sympathetic nerves) to enter the spinal cord at the T10 and T11 segments.

Clinical Presentation: Pathologies such as ovarian torsion, cyst rupture, or Mittelschmerz (ovulatory pain) cause referred pain to the dermatomal areas corresponding to T10–T11. This manifests as pain referred to the umbilical region, lower abdomen, and lower back.

Uterine (Fallopian) Tubes PYQ 2013, 2018
The uterine tube is ~10 cm long, transmits ovum from ovary to uterus. Site of fertilisation is the ampulla. Site of ectopic pregnancy is usually the ampulla (55%) or isthmus.

Ectopic Pregnancy

Fertilised ovum implants outside uterine cavity — 95% in uterine tube (ampulla most common). Risk: PID scarring, previous ectopic, IUD. Presents ~6–8 weeks with unilateral pelvic pain + missed period. Rupture → haemoperitoneum → collapse. Rectouterine Pouch (Pouch of Douglas) ⭐ PYQ 2010, 2025
The Pouch of Douglas (rectouterine pouch) is the lowest (most dependent) subdivision of the female peritoneal cavity in both the erect and supine positions. It lies between the uterus and rectum.

Anatomical Boundaries:

  • Anterior: Posterior surface of the uterus (cervix and body) and the posterior fornix of the vagina.
  • Posterior: Anterior surface of the middle third of the rectum.
  • Lateral Walls: Rectouterine folds of peritoneum (which contain the uterosacral ligaments).
  • Floor (Inferior): Rectouterine peritoneal reflection.
  • Contents: Coils of ileum and sigmoid colon, plus a small amount of physiological peritoneal fluid.

Applied Clinical Importance:

  • Fluid Accumulation: In cases of internal bleeding (e.g., ruptured ectopic pregnancy, splenic rupture) or pelvic infections (PID), blood, pus, or ascites pools preferentially in the Pouch of Douglas due to gravity.
  • Culdocentesis: A diagnostic procedure where a needle is passed through the posterior fornix of the vagina into the pouch of Douglas to aspirate fluid. Non-clotting blood indicates active hemoperitoneum.
  • Pelvic Abscess: Purulent fluid collecting here can lead to a localized abscess. It can be drained surgically by making an incision through the posterior vaginal wall (posterior colpotomy).

Uterus & Its Supports

Uterus — Anatomy ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2013, 2016, 2019, 2022

Parts:

  • Fundus: Domed upper part above uterine tube origins
  • Body (corpus): Main part; anteflexed on cervix in normal position
  • Isthmus: Constriction between body and cervix (~1 cm); becomes lower uterine segment in pregnancy — site of lower segment caesarean section (LSCS)
  • Cervix: 2.5 cm; projects into vagina; external os (parous = transverse slit, nulliparous = circular)

Normal Position:

  • Anteverted (long axis of uterus angled forward relative to vaginal axis — at cervicovaginal junction)
  • Anteflexed (body of uterus bent forward on cervix — at isthmus)

Relations:

  • Anterior: Vesicouterine pouch + bladder (body covered by peritoneum anterosuperiorly)
  • Posterior: Rectouterine pouch (Pouch of Douglas) + rectum. Entire posterior uterine wall covered by peritoneum
  • Lateral: Broad ligament, uterine artery (at level of isthmus), ureter (2 cm lateral to cervix — "water under the bridge")

Blood Supply:

  • Uterine artery (from internal iliac) = main supply
  • Ovarian artery (anastomoses with uterine at cornu)
  • Uterine artery crosses ABOVE ureter at isthmus — surgical danger
"Water under the bridge": In hysterectomy — the uterine artery crosses ABOVE the ureter at the level of the isthmus (2 cm lateral to cervix). Ligation of uterine artery risks ureteric injury. Mnemonic: "Artery is water, ureter is bridge — water flows over the bridge."
Supports of the Uterus ⭐ PYQ Every Year — Never Skip
Uterine supports are classified as primary (ligamentous/fascial) and secondary (muscular = pelvic floor/levator ani). Failure of supports → uterine prolapse.

Broad Ligament — Contents PYQ 2012, 2017, 2021
The broad ligament is a double layer of peritoneum draping over the uterus and uterine tubes, stretching laterally to the pelvic walls. It is NOT a true ligament and provides no structural support.

Parts of Broad Ligament:

  • Mesosalpinx: Upper free part enclosing uterine tube
  • Mesovarium: Posterior layer fold attaching ovary
  • Mesometrium: Largest part, below uterine vessels, attaches to uterine body

Contents of Broad Ligament:

  • Uterine tube (in mesosalpinx)
  • Round ligament of uterus (anterior layer)
  • Ovarian ligament (posterior layer)
  • Uterine artery + veins
  • Ovarian artery (in suspensory ligament at lateral end)
  • Lymphatics, nerves
  • Epoophoron + paroophoron (remnants of mesonephric/Wolffian duct)

Internal Iliac Artery

Internal Iliac — Branches PYQ 2013, 2017, 2021
The internal iliac artery (hypogastric artery) is the artery of the pelvis. It divides into anterior and posterior divisions ~4 cm after origin at the bifurcation of common iliac at L5/S1.

Anterior Division — "Visceral" branches:

  • Umbilical artery (obliterates distally → medial umbilical ligament; patency → superior vesical artery)
  • Superior vesical artery (from proximal umbilical → upper bladder + artery to vas deferens)
  • Uterine artery (female → main supply of uterus; crosses above ureter at isthmus)
  • Vaginal artery (female)
  • Inferior vesical artery (male → lower bladder + prostate + seminal vesicles)
  • Middle rectal artery (middle rectum)
  • Internal pudendal artery (perineum + external genitalia; exits via greater sciatic foramen → loops around ischial spine → enters Alcock's canal)
  • Obturator artery (obturator foramen → medial thigh muscles)
  • Inferior gluteal artery (exits greater sciatic foramen below piriformis)

Posterior Division — "Parietal" branches:

  • Iliolumbar artery (to iliacus + psoas)
  • Lateral sacral artery (×2) (enters sacral foramina)
  • Superior gluteal artery (largest branch — exits above piriformis through greater sciatic foramen → gluteal muscles)
Anterior Division Branches
"U-SUV-MOPI-G" = Umbilical · Superior vesical · Uterine · Vaginal (female) · Middle rectal · Obturator · Pudendal (internal) · Inferior vesical · Inferior Gluteal
Internal Iliac Ligation

Bilateral internal iliac artery ligation is performed to control PPH (postpartum haemorrhage) during obstetric emergencies. By converting pulsatile flow to non-pulsatile venous-type flow, it allows clot formation without completely devascularising the uterus (collateral supply from ovarian arteries is preserved).

Pudendal Nerve — Complete ⭐ PYQ 2012, 2016, 2019, 2023
The pudendal nerve is the principal nerve of the perineum. It is the somatic nerve supply of the entire perineum. Root value: S2, S3, S4 — "S2, 3, 4 keep you off the floor."

Viva Q&A

Pelvic Floor & Perineum
Describe levator ani — its parts, attachments, and function.PYQ 2014, 2020, 2024

Levator ani is a broad muscular sheet forming the pelvic diaphragm. It has 3 parts:

  • Pubococcygeus: Pubic body → anococcygeal raphe/coccyx. Contains pubovaginalis/puboprostaticus fibres
  • Puborectalis: Pubic body → loops behind anorectal junction forming a U-shaped sling. Most functionally important — maintains anorectal angle (~90°) for faecal continence
  • Iliococcygeus: ATLA on obturator fascia → anococcygeal raphe + coccyx

Nerve supply: Perineal branch of S4 (pelvic surface) + pudendal nerve branches (perineal surface)

Functions: Supports pelvic viscera; maintains urinary/faecal continence; assists in parturition; maintains intra-abdominal pressure

The levator hiatus (U-shaped gap) transmits urethra, vagina/prostate, and anal canal — the weakest point where prolapse occurs.

What is the significance of the pectinate (dentate) line?PYQ Every Year

The pectinate (dentate) line is the most important landmark of the anal canal — located at its midpoint, formed by the free edges of anal valves connecting the lower ends of columns of Morgagni.

Embryological significance: Junction between hindgut endoderm (above) and proctodeum ectoderm (below).

Above pectinate line: Columnar epithelium, portal venous drainage (superior rectal → portal), internal iliac lymphatics, autonomic innervation (painless). → Internal haemorrhoids (painless, bleed bright red), adenocarcinoma

Below pectinate line: Squamous epithelium, systemic venous drainage (inferior rectal → IVC), inguinal lymph node drainage, somatic innervation (pudendal — painful). → External haemorrhoids (painful), SCC anal canal

The anorectal junction (above anal canal, where puborectal sling compresses) is separate from pectinate line — located ~2 cm above it at the top of the anal canal.

What are the supports of the uterus? Which is most important?PYQ Every Year

Uterine supports (most to least important):

  • 1. Transverse cervical (cardinal / Mackenrodt's) ligament: Most important primary support. Condensation of pelvic fascia from cervix + upper vagina → lateral pelvic wall. Runs horizontally at base of broad ligament. Contains uterine artery. Prevents lateral displacement and uterine descent.
  • 2. Uterosacral ligaments: Cervix → sacrum posteriorly. Maintain anteversion. Bilateral.
  • 3. Pubocervical ligament: Cervix → pubis anteriorly.
  • 4. Levator ani (pelvic floor): Secondary muscular support; acts as shelf. Damaged in multiparity → prolapse.
  • 5. Peritoneal folds: Broad ligament, round ligament, ovarian ligament — maintain position but NOT true supports. Cannot prevent prolapse.

Round ligament maintains anteversion but is NOT a structural support — the most common surgical misconception.

Pelvis & Ureter
Where is the ureter at risk in pelvic surgery?PYQ 2015, 2018, 2022

The ureter is the most commonly injured structure in pelvic surgery. Three danger zones in female pelvic surgery:

  • 1. Pelvic brim: During ligation of infundibulopelvic (suspensory) ligament in oophorectomy — ureter runs beneath this ligament on medial surface of internal iliac artery
  • 2. Lateral to cervix (isthmus level): During ligation of uterine artery in hysterectomy. Uterine artery crosses ABOVE the ureter at ~2 cm lateral to cervix — "Water (artery) runs over the bridge (ureter)"
  • 3. Near vaginal fornix: During clamping of cardinal ligament — ureter runs close to lateral vaginal fornix

Prevention: Always identify ureter before clipping any major pelvic vessel. Intraoperative ureteric catheters/stents (ureteric stenting) help identification. If injured: uretero-ureterostomy, ureteroneocystostomy, or Boari flap repair depending on site.

Describe the pudendal nerve — its course and distribution.PYQ 2016, 2019, 2023

Pudendal nerve — root value: S2, S3, S4 (anterior rami of sacral plexus).

Course:

  • Exits pelvis through greater sciatic foramen below piriformis
  • Winds around the ischial spine (medial to internal pudendal vessels)
  • Re-enters perineum through lesser sciatic foramen
  • Runs forward in Alcock's canal (pudendal canal) in lateral wall of ischioanal fossa on medial surface of obturator internus

3 Terminal branches:

  • Inferior rectal nerve: EAS + anal canal below pectinate line + perianal skin
  • Perineal nerve: Muscles of UG triangle; scrotal/labial skin
  • Dorsal nerve of penis/clitoris: Sensory

Pudendal nerve block: at ischial spine (transvaginally or transpubically). Provides anaesthesia for perineum, labia, and anal canal for forceps delivery or perineal repair.

Compare male and female pelvis — how do differences aid obstetrics?PYQ 2013, 2018, 2022

Female pelvis adaptations for parturition:

  • Wider subpubic angle (80–85°): Allows fetal head to pass under pubic arch
  • Oval/round pelvic inlet: Larger transverse diameter (13 cm) vs AP (11 cm) → fetal head enters in transverse position
  • Shorter, wider sacrum with less curvature: More posterior pelvic space
  • Everted, wider ischial tuberosities: Wider transverse outlet diameter (11 cm)
  • Less prominent ischial spines: Wider mid-cavity diameter
  • Larger greater sciatic notch (>90°)

Obstetric conjugate (10.5 cm) is the critical diameter — the shortest AP at inlet. Diagonal conjugate (12 cm) is measured clinically by internal examination. Cardinal movements of labour (engagement, descent, flexion, internal rotation, extension, external rotation) are designed around these pelvic dimensions.

Essay Question Templates

EQ — Pectinate Line [PYQ Every Year]
"Describe the pectinate line of the anal canal and its clinical significance."
  1. Definition & Location: The pectinate (dentate) line is a serrated line running circumferentially at the midpoint of the anal canal (2 cm from anus). Formed by the free crescentic edges of anal valves connecting the lower ends of the columns of Morgagni. Represents the embryological junction between hindgut and proctodeum.
  2. Above the Pectinate Line: Columnar epithelium (hindgut origin), venous drainage via superior rectal vein to IMV → portal system, lymphatic drainage to internal iliac nodes, autonomic nerve supply (insensitive to pain, temperature, and touch — only sensitive to stretch/distension).
  3. Below the Pectinate Line: Squamous epithelium (proctodeum origin), venous drainage via inferior rectal vein to internal pudendal → internal iliac → IVC (systemic), lymphatic drainage to superficial inguinal nodes, somatic nerve supply via inferior rectal nerve (pudendal) — sensitive to all sensations including pain.
  4. Clinical Significance — Haemorrhoids: Internal haemorrhoids arise above pectinate line from superior rectal venous plexus → painless (autonomic), bleed bright red (arterial supply from superior rectal artery). External haemorrhoids form below pectinate line → intensely painful (somatic innervation). Thrombosed external haemorrhoid = acute blue-black swelling.
  5. Clinical Significance — Carcinoma: Above pectinate line → adenocarcinoma (columnar epithelium), spreads to internal iliac and perirectal nodes. Below pectinate line → squamous cell carcinoma, spreads to superficial inguinal nodes. This dictates different surgical and radiotherapy field planning.
  6. Clinical Significance — Abscess/Fistula: Anal crypts (between anal valves) can become infected → perianal abscess. Parks' classification of fistula-in-ano depends on relationship of fistula tract to external sphincter — the pectinate line serves as a reference landmark for planning surgery.
EQ — Supports of Uterus [PYQ Every Year]
"Describe the supports of the uterus. How does failure of these supports lead to uterine prolapse?"
  1. Introduction: The uterus is maintained in its anteflexed anteverted position by a combination of ligamentous, fascial, and muscular supports. These are classified as primary (fascial condensations) and secondary (muscular).
  2. Primary Support — Cardinal (Mackenrodt's) Ligament: Most important. Condensation of pelvic fascia running from the cervix and upper vagina to the lateral pelvic wall. Runs horizontally at the base of the broad ligament. Contains the uterine artery. Prevents lateral displacement and downward descent.
  3. Primary Support — Uterosacral Ligaments: Cervix to sacrum, passing on either side of the rectum. Maintain the cervix posteriorly, contributing to anteversion. Palpable on PR examination when infiltrated by cervical carcinoma.
  4. Primary Support — Pubocervical Ligament: Cervix to pubis anteriorly. Less important than cardinal and uterosacral.
  5. Secondary Support — Pelvic Floor (Levator Ani): The puborectal sling and levator plate act as a muscular hammock. Damage during childbirth (prolonged labour, large baby, forceps) weakens this support, allowing the pelvic floor to descend.
  6. Prolapse Mechanism & Degrees: Weakening of cardinal ligaments (primary) + pelvic floor damage (secondary) → uterine descent. 1st degree: cervix descends into vagina; 2nd degree: cervix at introitus; 3rd degree (procidentia): entire uterus outside vagina. Treated with pelvic floor exercises (mild), pessaries, or surgical repair (Manchester repair, vaginal hysterectomy + pelvic floor repair).
EQ — Prostate Gland [PYQ 2014, 2019, 2022]
"Describe the anatomy of the prostate gland. What are its surgical relations?"
  1. Position & Size: The prostate gland is a fibromuscular glandular organ, size of a chestnut (~4 × 3 × 2 cm), weight ~20 g. Located in true pelvis between bladder neck (superior) and urogenital diaphragm (inferior). Entirely extraperitoneal (below peritoneum).
  2. Relations: Anterior: Pubic symphysis (separated by retropubic space/cave of Retzius — used in retropubic prostatectomy). Posterior: Rectum — separated by Denonvilliers' fascia (rectovesical septum — surgical plane in prostatectomy). Superior: Bladder neck. Inferior: Urogenital diaphragm (external urethral sphincter). Lateral: Levator ani + pelvic fascia containing neurovascular bundles of Walsh (posterolateral — preserve in nerve-sparing prostatectomy).
  3. Zones (McNeal): Peripheral zone (70%) = site of carcinoma prostate, palpable as hard nodule on PR. Central zone (25%) = around ejaculatory ducts. Transitional zone (5%) = around proximal urethra = site of BPH → compresses urethra → lower urinary tract symptoms.
  4. Surgical Importance — PR Examination: Normal prostate: smooth, firm, rubbery, median sulcus palpable. BPH: enlarged, smooth, rubbery. Carcinoma: hard nodule, irregular, loss of median sulcus, fixation to surrounding structures.
  5. PSA: Prostate-specific antigen — a serine protease produced by prostatic epithelium. Normal < 4 ng/mL. Used for screening, staging, monitoring treatment response. Not organ-specific — elevated in BPH, prostatitis, also carcinoma. PSA density, PSA velocity, and free/total PSA ratio improve specificity.
