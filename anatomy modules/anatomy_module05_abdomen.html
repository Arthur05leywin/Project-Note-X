Anterior Abdominal Wall

The anterior abdominal wall has 9 layers (skin → peritoneum). The three flat muscles form a musculoaponeurotic sheet — their aponeuroses interdigitate at the linea alba and form the rectus sheath around rectus abdominis.

Rectus Sheath — The Arcuate Line ⭐ PYQ Every Year
The arcuate line (linea semicircularis) lies midway between umbilicus and pubic symphysis. It is the lower limit of the posterior wall of the rectus sheath.
RegionAnterior WallPosterior Wall
Above arcuate lineExternal oblique aponeurosis + anterior lamina of internal oblique aponeurosisPosterior lamina of internal oblique + transversus abdominis aponeurosis + transversalis fascia
Below arcuate lineAll three aponeuroses (EO + IO + TA) pass anterior to rectusAbsent — only transversalis fascia. Hence no posterior wall below arcuate line

Contents of Rectus Sheath:

  • Rectus abdominis muscle
  • Pyramidalis muscle (anterior to lower rectus, absent in 20%)
  • Superior epigastric artery (branch of internal thoracic)
  • Inferior epigastric artery (branch of external iliac)
  • Lower 6 intercostal nerves (T7–T12) + iliohypogastric nerve
  • Lymphatics

Rectus Abdominis:

  • Origin: Pubic crest + pubic symphysis
  • Insertion: Costal cartilages of 5th, 6th, 7th ribs + xiphoid process
  • 3 tendinous intersections — adherent to anterior wall only (not posterior)
  • Action: Flex trunk, compress abdomen, depress thorax

Rectus Sheath Haematoma

Tear of inferior epigastric artery (after trauma/cough/anticoagulants) → blood collects within rectus sheath. Below arcuate line it spreads freely (no posterior wall) but is confined by anterior wall. Fothergill's sign: mass does NOT cross midline, does NOT disappear on tensing abdominal muscles (unlike intraperitoneal mass).

Midline Incision & Incisional Hernia PYQ 2011

Linea Alba: A midline fibrous band running from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis, formed by the interlacing fibers of the aponeuroses of the external oblique, internal oblique, and transversus abdominis. It contains very few blood vessels and nerves.

Median/Midline Incision: Made along the linea alba. It is the most common incision for emergency laparotomy because it is simple, bloodless, avoids major nerves/vessels, and provides rapid access to any part of the peritoneal cavity.

Risk of Incisional Hernia: Because the linea alba is relatively avascular, it heals very slowly. In the presence of post-operative risk factors (coughing, wound infection, obesity, or abdominal distension), the wound edges can fail to unite, resulting in a weak scar through which abdominal contents herniate (incisional hernia).

Inguinal Region

The inguinal canal is the single most important topic in abdominal anatomy for MBBS exams. Appears in almost every annual paper. Master it completely.

  • Round ligament of uterus
  • Ilioinguinal nerve
  • Lymphatics from fundus of uterus

Peritoneum

Peritoneum is a serous membrane lining the abdominal cavity (parietal) and covering abdominal viscera (visceral). Total surface area ~2 m² — similar to skin. The cavity contains only a thin film of fluid (~50 mL) normally.

Peritoneal Pouches & Recesses ⭐ PYQ 2013, 2018, 2021

Subphrenic Spaces (Above Transverse Colon):

  • Right subphrenic space: Between right lobe of liver + diaphragm. Site of right subphrenic abscess after perforated appendix/duodenal ulcer
  • Left subphrenic space: Between left lobe of liver/stomach/spleen + diaphragm
  • Hepatorenal pouch (Morrison's pouch): Between right lobe of liver and right kidney. Lowest space in supine position → fluid collects here first. Classic site of subhepatic abscess
  • Lesser sac (omental bursa): Behind stomach/lesser omentum. Communicates with greater sac via epiploic foramen of Winslow

Pelvic Pouches (Below Transverse Colon):

  • Males: Rectovesical pouch — between rectum and bladder. Lowest point in male peritoneal cavity in erect position
  • Females: Rectouterine pouch (Pouch of Douglas) — between rectum and uterus. Lowest point in female peritoneal cavity. Accessible via posterior vaginal fornix → culdocentesis
  • Vesicouterine pouch: Between uterus and bladder (not as deep)
Epiploic Foramen of Winslow

Gateway to lesser sac. Boundaries: Anterior = hepatoduodenal ligament (portal triad); Posterior = IVC; Superior = caudate lobe of liver; Inferior = 1st part of duodenum. Pringle's manoeuvre: compress hepatoduodenal ligament to stop liver bleeding.

04

Stomach

Stomach — Anatomy PYQ 2011, 2014, 2019

Parts (Cardia → Pylorus):

  • Cardia: Junction with oesophagus at T10 level behind 7th costal cartilage
  • Fundus: Above cardia level (dome), contains swallowed air (gastric bubble on X-ray)
  • Body: Main part between fundus and pyloric antrum
  • Pyloric antrum: Funnel-shaped, leads to pyloric canal
  • Pylorus: Thick muscular sphincter at L1 level (transpyloric plane)

Curvatures:

  • Lesser curvature: Right border; notch = angularis/incisura angularis; lesser omentum attached
  • Greater curvature: Left border, longer; greater omentum + gastrosplenic ligament attached

Blood Supply:

  • Lesser curvature: Left gastric artery (from celiac trunk — largest supply) + Right gastric artery (from hepatic proper)
  • Greater curvature: Left gastroepiploic (from splenic) + Right gastroepiploic (from gastroduodenal)
  • Fundus: Short gastric arteries (from splenic artery)
  • All arteries anastomose freely → stomach rarely infarcted

Nerve Supply:

  • Vagus (CN X): Left vagus → anterior surface; Right vagus → posterior surface. "LARP" = Left Anterior, Right Posterior
  • Sympathetic: T6–T10 via greater splanchnic nerve → celiac plexus
Pyloric Stenosis

Hypertrophy of pyloric smooth muscle (congenital — presents at 4–6 weeks as projectile vomiting). No bile (obstruction above Ampulla of Vater). Olive-shaped mass palpable in right epigastrium. Causes hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis (loss of HCl in vomit). Treatment: Ramstedt's pyloromyotomy.

Duodenum

The duodenum is the most important part of the small intestine anatomically. C-shaped, 25 cm long, wraps around the head of pancreas. All parts except the 1st 2.5 cm are retroperitoneal.

Duodenum — Second (Descending) Part ⭐ PYQ 2010, 2015
The 2nd part of the duodenum (7.5 cm) is vertical, descending from L1 to L3. It is retroperitoneal and is the site where the biliary and pancreatic secretions enter the digestive tract. It forms the boundary between the foregut and midgut.

Anatomical Relations:

  • Anterior: Transverse colon, transverse mesocolon, and coils of jejunum. Crossed by the gall bladder/liver.
  • Posterior: Anterior surface of right kidney (medial part), renal vessels, IVC, and right psoas major.
  • Medial: Head of the pancreas and common bile duct.
  • Lateral: Right colic (hepatic) flexure, ascending colon, and right lobe of liver.

Luminal (Internal) Features:

  • Plicae Circulares: Circular mucosal folds that are well developed here.
  • Major Duodenal Papilla (Ampulla of Vater): Located on the posteromedial wall, 8–10 cm distal to the pylorus. It is the common opening of the **Common Bile Duct (CBD)** and the **Main Pancreatic Duct (Wirsung)**. Its opening is controlled by the **Sphincter of Oddi**.
  • Minor Duodenal Papilla: Located 2 cm anterosuperior (proximal) to the major papilla. It is the opening of the **Accessory Pancreatic Duct (Santorini)**.
  • Plica Longitudinalis: A longitudinal mucosal fold extending inferiorly from the major papilla, caused by the underlying CBD.

Dual Blood Supply (Foregut-Midgut Transition):

The major duodenal papilla represents the embryological transition point between the **foregut** and **midgut**. Consequently, the duodenum has a dual arterial supply:
  • Proximal to Papilla (Foregut): Supplied by the Superior Pancreaticoduodenal Artery (branch of the Gastroduodenal Artery, which arises from the Common Hepatic Artery of the Celiac Trunk).
  • Distal to Papilla (Midgut): Supplied by the Inferior Pancreaticoduodenal Artery (branch of the Superior Mesenteric Artery [SMA]).
  • These two arteries form anterior and posterior anastomotic arches (pancreaticoduodenal arcades) in the groove between the head of the pancreas and the duodenum, providing a rich collateral circulation.

Large Intestine

Appendicitis & McBurney's Point

McBurney's point: Junction of lateral 1/3 and medial 2/3 of a line from right ASIS to umbilicus. Base of appendix always here (even if tip varies). Rovsing's sign: Pressure on LIF causes pain in RIF (peritoneal irritation). Retrocaecal appendix — psoas sign positive (hip extension causes pain). Pelvic appendix — rectal tenderness.

Watershed area of colon: Splenic flexure = junction of SMA and IMA territories. Griffiths' critical point. Most vulnerable in low-flow states → ischaemic colitis typically here. The marginal artery of Drummond runs along the inner margin of colon connecting SMA + IMA territories.

Liver & Biliary System

Liver — Lobes & Surfaces ⭐ PYQ 2010, 2013, 2016, 2019, 2023

Anatomical (Morphological) Lobes — 4:

  • Right lobe: Large, separated from left by falciform ligament (anteriorly) and fissure for ligamentum teres + ligamentum venosum (inferiorly)
  • Left lobe: Smaller, more anterior
  • Caudate lobe: On posterior surface, between IVC and ligamentum venosum. Gets blood from BOTH left and right portal branches — drains directly into IVC (not hepatic veins). Not involved in Budd-Chiari
  • Quadrate lobe: On visceral surface, between GB fossa and ligamentum teres. Functionally belongs to LEFT lobe

Porta Hepatis (Hilum of Liver):

  • On visceral (inferior) surface, between caudate and quadrate lobes
  • Contents (anterior → posterior): Hepatic ducts (R+L) → Hepatic arteries (R+L) → Portal vein
  • Mnemonic: "Duct in front, Vein at back, Artery between" = DVA or HAD (Hepatic duct-Artery-portal vein in D A V order superficial to deep)

Blood Supply (Dual):

  • Portal vein: 75% of total blood, 50% of oxygen
  • Hepatic artery proper: 25% of blood, 50% of oxygen
  • Hepatic veins (3): Drain into IVC directly

Portal-Systemic Anastomoses

Portal hypertension (normal portal pressure 5–10 mmHg, hypertension = >12 mmHg) causes blood to bypass the liver via collateral channels at 4 sites where portal and systemic veins communicate. These become varicosities when engorged. One of the highest-yield topics in abdominal anatomy.

Pancreas & Spleen

Pancreas ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2017, 2019, 2021
Pancreas is a retroperitoneal gland (except the tail which is intraperitoneal in splenorenal ligament). It lies at L1–L2 behind the stomach. Head is cradled in the C-curve of duodenum.

Parts & Key Relations:

  • Head: In concavity of duodenum (C-loop). Uncinate process projects from lower left of head, hooks behind SMA + SMV. CBD grooves its posterior surface
  • Neck: Over portal vein formation (SMV + SV junction). At L1 (transpyloric plane)
  • Body: Anterior surface covered by peritoneum forming posterior wall of lesser sac. Splenic vein runs in groove on posterior surface
  • Tail: Only intraperitoneal part, reaches hilum of spleen in splenorenal ligament. Relationship to spleen important in splenectomy

Duct System:

  • Main duct of Wirsung: Runs through whole gland from tail → head. Joins CBD at ampulla of Vater
  • Accessory duct of Santorini: Drains upper head/neck → opens at minor duodenal papilla (2 cm above major papilla)
  • Pancreas divisum: failure of duct fusion → most accessory duct carries most drainage → minor papilla too small → pancreatitis
Acute Pancreatitis — Anatomy Basis

Grey-Turner's sign: Flank bruising (retroperitoneal blood tracking to flank). Cullen's sign: Periumbilical bruising (blood tracking via falciform ligament to umbilicus). Both indicate severe haemorrhagic pancreatitis. Pseudocyst forms in lesser sac.

Development & Anomalies:

  • Buds: Develops from ventral and dorsal pancreatic buds of the foregut endoderm.
    • Ventral Bud: Forms lower head & uncinate process. Relates to hepatic diverticulum.
    • Dorsal Bud: Forms upper head, neck, body, and tail.
  • Fusion: Duodenal rotation brings ventral bud posterior to dorsal bud; they fuse. Main duct (Wirsung) = ventral duct + distal dorsal duct. Accessory duct (Santorini) = proximal dorsal duct.
  • Annular Pancreas: Bilobed ventral bud rotates in opposite directions, encircling and compressing the 2nd part of duodenum → duodenal obstruction (vomiting, double-bubble sign).
  • Pancreas Divisum: Failure of dorsal & ventral duct fusion. Most pancreas drains via minor papilla → stenosis risk → chronic pancreatitis.

Microanatomy & Blood Supply:

  • Exocrine (99%): Serous acini. Simple pyramidal cells with basal basophilic cytoplasm (RER) and apical eosinophilic zymogen granules. Drained by intercalated ducts starting with centroacinar cells (distinctive for pancreas; no striated ducts).
  • Endocrine (1%): Islets of Langerhans. Pale-staining spherical nests. α-cells (glucagon, 20%), β-cells (insulin, 70%), δ-cells (somatostatin, 5-10%), PP cells (1%). Rich fenestrated capillary network.
  • Blood Supply: Dual arterial supply:
    • Head/Neck: Anastomosing superior pancreaticoduodenal (from Gastroduodenal, branch of Celiac) & inferior pancreaticoduodenal (from SMA).
    • Body/Tail: Multiple splenic artery branches (e.g., a. pancreatica magna).
Obstructive Jaundice in Carcinoma of Pancreatic Head PYQ 2011, 2014, 2016, 2019

Pathological Basis: The common bile duct (CBD) passes behind the head of the pancreas and is often embedded in a groove on its posterior surface. Carcinoma arising from the head of the pancreas easily compresses or invades the CBD, completely blocking bile flow into the duodenum.

Clinical Signs & Courvoisier's Law:

  • Painless, Progressive Jaundice: Skin yellowing, scleral icterus, and severe pruritus (deposition of bile salts under the skin).
  • Stool & Urine: Clay-colored (acholic) stools (lack of stercobilin) and dark tea-colored urine (excess conjugated bilirubin excreted by kidneys).
  • Courvoisier's Law: In a patient with jaundice, if the gallbladder is palpably enlarged, it is typically NOT due to gallstones (as prior gallstone inflammation causes a scarred, non-distensible gallbladder). Instead, it is due to neoplastic obstruction of the distal CBD (e.g., Ca head of pancreas or cholangiocarcinoma), which distends the normal gallbladder.
Spleen PYQ 2011, 2015, 2020

Key Facts:

  • Size rule: 1 × 3 × 5 inches, weighs 7 oz (~200 g), lies under 9th, 10th, 11th ribs in left hypochondrium
  • Intraperitoneal — covered by peritoneum except at hilum
  • Long axis along 10th rib; posterior end reaches T10, anterior end reaches mid-axillary line
  • Notched anterior border (2–3 notches) — distinguishes from other masses
  • Hilum on medial surface — tail of pancreas reaches here; gastrosplenic + splenorenal ligaments attach here

Relations:

  • Anterior: Stomach (gastrosplenic ligament)
  • Posterior: Left kidney, left suprarenal
  • Medial: Tail of pancreas (via splenorenal ligament)
  • Superior/Lateral: Diaphragm
  • Inferior: Left colic (splenic) flexure of colon
Spleen Enlargement Direction

Spleen enlarges toward right iliac fossa (diagonally). Notched anterior border palpable. Dullness extends below 9th rib in left flank. Cannot get above it (splenic dullness continuous with chest). Cannot ballot it (retroperitoneal = kidney; intraperitoneal = spleen).

Renal Angle & Nephrectomy

Renal angle (costovertebral angle): Between 12th rib and lateral border of erector spinae — site of palpation and punch tenderness in kidney disease. Ureter — Anatomy, Histology & Development PYQ 2010, 2012, 2013, 2017
The ureter is a muscular retroperitoneal tube (25 cm long) that propels urine from the renal pelvis to the urinary bladder. It consists of three parts (abdominal, pelvic, and intravesical) and is lined by transitional epithelium.

Anatomical Course & Constrictions:

  • Course: Begins at the renal pelvis (L1/L2), runs vertically down on psoas major, crosses the pelvic brim (common iliac bifurcation), runs along the lateral pelvic wall, and enters the posterolateral bladder wall obliquely.
  • Three Anatomical Constrictions (Clinically vital for stone impaction):
    1. Pelviureteric Junction (PUJ): Where the renal pelvis becomes the ureter.
    2. Pelvic Brim crossing: Where the ureter crosses the bifurcation of the common iliac artery.
    3. Ureterovesical Junction (UVJ): Where the ureter obliquely traverses the bladder wall (acts as a physiological valve).
  • Blood Supply: Segmental (Renal artery → Gonadal artery → Abdominal aorta → Common iliac → Internal iliac → Superior/Inferior vesical arteries). Anastomoses run in the adventitia.

Microanatomy (Histology):

  • Mucosa: Lined by urothelium (transitional epithelium) (4–6 cell layers thick). Contains superficial umbrella cells with rigid plaques (uroplakin) that form a barrier to urine toxicity. No muscularis mucosae or submucosa.
  • Muscularis: Outer circular and inner longitudinal smooth muscle layers (opposite to the gut!). In the lower 1/3 (pelvic/vesical part), a third outer longitudinal layer is added to assist in sphincter-like closure.
  • Adventitia: Fibroelastic connective tissue carrying blood vessels, nerves, and lymphatics.

Embryological Development:

  • Arises from the ureteric bud (an outgrowth of the distal mesonephric duct/Wolffian duct).
  • The ureteric bud grows dorsocranially into the metanephric blastema, inducing kidney development.
  • Derivative structures: Ureter, renal pelvis, major & minor calyces, and collecting ducts.

Course of the ureter and its relations in the pelvis — Gray's Anatomy
Anatomical Basis of Loin-to-Groin Pain (Ureteric Colic)

When a ureteric calculus (stone) gets impacted (usually at one of the three constrictions), it causes hyperperistalsis and spasm of the smooth muscle, leading to excruciating, spasmodic ureteric colic.

Referred Pain Pathway: The ureter is supplied by autonomic fibers from the renal, aortic, and hypogastric plexuses (sensory afferents enter spinal cord segments T11 to L2).

Pain is referred to the skin dermatomes of the same spinal segments (T11–L2):

  • Loin (T11–T12): Posterior flank and subcostal region.
  • Groin (L1–L2): Inguinal region, scrotum (in males) or labium majus (in females), and the anterior-medial aspect of the thigh (via the genitofemoral nerve).

This explains the classic clinical radiation: pain starting in the loin and shooting down to the groin.

Suprarenal (Adrenal) Glands PYQ 2013, 2019

Anatomy:

  • Right: Pyramidal shape, behind IVC and right lobe of liver. Vein drains directly into IVC
  • Left: Semilunar (crescent) shape, related to stomach + pancreatic tail. Vein drains into left renal vein
  • Both enclosed in Gerota's fascia (with kidneys)
  • Blood supply: Superior suprarenal (inferior phrenic), Middle suprarenal (aorta), Inferior suprarenal (renal artery)

Histology & Function:

  • Cortex (mesodermal): GFR = Glomerulosa (Aldosterone), Fasciculata (Cortisol), Reticularis (Androgens)
  • Medulla (neuroectodermal): Chromaffin cells → Adrenaline (80%) + Noradrenaline (20%)
  • Pheochromocytoma: Catecholamine-secreting tumour → hypertensive crises, headache, palpitations, sweating. 10% rule: 10% bilateral, 10% malignant, 10% extra-adrenal

Inguinal Region & Abdominal Wall
What are the walls of the inguinal canal?PYQ Every Year

The inguinal canal has 4 walls, 2 openings:

  • Anterior: External oblique aponeurosis (full length) + Internal oblique (lateral 1/3 only)
  • Posterior: Transversalis fascia (full length) + Conjoint tendon/inguinal falx (medial 1/3)
  • Roof: Arching fibres of internal oblique + transversus abdominis
  • Floor: Inguinal ligament + lacunar ligament medially
  • Deep ring: In transversalis fascia, lateral to inferior epigastric artery
  • Superficial ring: In EO aponeurosis, above pubic tubercle

Examiner tip: The medial wall has the conjoint tendon — important in direct hernia repair (Bassini repair sutures conjoint tendon to inguinal ligament).

What is the arcuate line? What is its significance?PYQ 2014, 2019

The arcuate line (linea semicircularis of Douglas) is the curved lower border of the posterior lamina of rectus sheath, located midway between umbilicus and pubic symphysis.

Significance:

  • Above it: posterior rectus sheath is present (formed by posterior lamina of IO + TA aponeurosis)
  • Below it: all 3 aponeuroses pass anterior → no posterior rectus sheath; only transversalis fascia protects posteriorly
  • Inferior epigastric vessels enter rectus sheath above this line
  • Clinical: in rectus sheath haematoma below arcuate line, blood spreads in the space of Retzius — can track down to thigh
Differentiate direct from indirect inguinal hernia.PYQ 2013, 2017, 2022

Indirect (Oblique):

  • Enters deep ring → traverses full length of canal → exits superficial ring
  • Lateral to inferior epigastric artery
  • Young patients, congenital (patent processus vaginalis)
  • Narrow neck → high strangulation risk
  • 3 fascial coverings (from all layers of spermatic cord)
  • Deep ring compression controls the hernia

Direct:

  • Pushes directly through posterior wall (Hesselbach's triangle)
  • Medial to inferior epigastric artery
  • Elderly, acquired (muscle weakness)
  • Wide neck → rarely strangulates
  • Only transversalis fascia + peritoneum
  • Deep ring compression does not control it
Portal Circulation & Liver
Enumerate the sites of porto-systemic anastomosis. What is their clinical significance?PYQ Every Year — Never Skip

4 Sites of Porto-Systemic Anastomosis:

  • 1. Lower oesophagus: Left gastric vein (portal) ↔ oesophageal tributaries of azygos (systemic). → Oesophageal varices — most dangerous, rupture causes massive haematemesis
  • 2. Umbilicus: Paraumbilical veins in falciform ligament (portal) ↔ superficial epigastric veins (systemic). → Caput Medusae
  • 3. Anal canal: Superior rectal vein (portal) ↔ middle + inferior rectal veins (systemic). → Portal hypertensive haemorrhoids
  • 4. Retroperitoneum: Veins of abdominal viscera ↔ renal/lumbar/phrenic veins. → Retroperitoneal varices (Retzius veins)

In portal hypertension, blood bypasses liver → these collaterals engorge → varices. Most lethal = oesophageal varices (30% mortality per bleed). Treated with endoscopic banding, propranolol (↓ portal pressure), TIPS procedure.

What are Couinaud's segments? Why are they surgically important?PYQ 2015, 2020, 2024

Couinaud divided the liver into 8 independent functional segments (I–VIII), each with its own:

  • Portal vein branch
  • Hepatic artery branch
  • Bile duct
  • Hepatic vein drainage

Surgical importance:

  • Each segment can be resected independently without devascularising adjacent segments
  • Segmentectomy / anatomical hepatectomy preserves maximum functional parenchyma
  • Metastases, HCC localised to specific segments can be excised
  • Segment I (caudate): Unique — drains directly to IVC, preserved in Budd-Chiari (hepatic vein obstruction affects all other segments)
  • Main lobar fissure (Cantlie's line) separates right (V–VIII) from left (I–IV) functional lobes — does NOT correspond to anatomical right/left lobe boundary
What is the epiploic foramen of Winslow? What are its boundaries?PYQ 2012, 2018

The epiploic foramen (foramen of Winslow) is the only normal communication between the greater sac and the lesser sac (omental bursa).

Boundaries:

  • Anterior: Free edge of lesser omentum (hepatoduodenal ligament) containing portal vein, hepatic artery, CBD
  • Posterior: IVC (covered by peritoneum)
  • Superior: Caudate lobe of liver
  • Inferior: 1st part of duodenum

Pringle's manoeuvre: Compress hepatoduodenal ligament (free edge of lesser omentum) between thumb and forefinger → controls hepatic blood flow → used to reduce bleeding during liver surgery. Fingers placed in epiploic foramen for this manoeuvre.

Kidneys & Retroperitoneum
What are the anterior relations of the right kidney? How does it differ from the left?PYQ 2014, 2021

Right kidney — anterior relations (top to bottom):

  • Right suprarenal gland (superiormedial)
  • Right lobe of liver (upper 2/3)
  • 2nd part of duodenum (medial border)
  • Right colic (hepatic) flexure (lower pole)
  • Small intestine / jejunum (lower pole lateral)

Left kidney — anterior relations:

  • Left suprarenal gland (superiormedial)
  • Stomach (upper pole)
  • Spleen (upper lateral)
  • Tail of pancreas + splenic vessels (hilum level)
  • Left colic (splenic) flexure + descending colon (lower pole)
  • Small intestine (lower medial)

Key difference: Right kidney is related to liver and duodenum; left kidney is related to spleen, stomach, and pancreatic tail. The 2nd part of duodenum is medial to right kidney — important in right nephrectomy.

Why does pancreatitis present with Grey-Turner's sign and Cullen's sign?PYQ 2016, 2021

In severe haemorrhagic pancreatitis, blood and pancreatic enzymes leak into surrounding retroperitoneal and peritoneal spaces:

  • Grey-Turner's sign: Bruising in flanks — haemorrhage tracks laterally in the retroperitoneal plane along the posterior pararenal space → discolours the skin of the flanks bilaterally
  • Cullen's sign: Bruising around the umbilicus — blood tracks along the falciform ligament from the retroperitoneum to the periumbilical region, or through extraperitoneal fat around the umbilical ring

Both are signs of retroperitoneal haemorrhage. They typically appear 24–48 hours after the onset of acute pancreatitis. Indicate severe disease — associated with high mortality. Ecchymosis can also appear in the scrotum (Cullen extension) or groin.

EQ — Inguinal Canal [PYQ Almost Every Year]
"Describe the inguinal canal. Add a note on its clinical importance."
  1. Definition & Location: The inguinal canal is a 4 cm oblique tunnel in the anterior abdominal wall, running from the deep inguinal ring (in transversalis fascia, lateral to inferior epigastric artery) to the superficial inguinal ring (in EO aponeurosis, above pubic tubercle). Runs parallel to and 1 cm above the medial half of the inguinal ligament.
  2. Walls — APRF: Anterior = EO aponeurosis (full) + IO muscle (lateral 1/3). Posterior = Transversalis fascia (full) + Conjoint tendon (medial 1/3). Roof = Arching IO + TA fibres. Floor = Inguinal ligament + lacunar ligament (medial).
  3. Contents: Male: Spermatic cord (3 fasciae: external spermatic, cremasteric, internal spermatic; 3 arteries: testicular, cremasteric, artery to vas; vas deferens; pampiniform plexus) + Ilioinguinal nerve (not in cord). Female: Round ligament of uterus + ilioinguinal nerve + lymphatics from fundus.
  4. Mechanism of Patency/Protection: The oblique direction (deep ring is not directly behind superficial ring) — increased intra-abdominal pressure causes anterior + posterior walls to be compressed together, closing canal. "Shutter mechanism" of arching IO fibres over deep ring prevents bowel herniation.
  5. Clinical Importance — Hernia: Indirect inguinal hernia enters deep ring (lateral to IEA), traverses canal, exits superficial ring. Direct inguinal hernia pushes through posterior wall of Hesselbach's triangle (medial to IEA). Indirect more common, young, narrow neck. Direct: elderly, wide neck, rarely strangulates.
  6. Examiner High-Yield: Inferior epigastric artery is the key landmark separating direct (medial) from indirect (lateral) hernias. Femoral hernia lies below inguinal ligament — do not confuse.
EQ — Porto-Systemic Anastomosis [PYQ Every Year — Group B/C]
"Describe the sites of porto-systemic anastomosis. What happens when portal pressure rises?"
  1. Introduction: Portal hypertension (portal pressure >12 mmHg, normal 5–10) causes reversal of flow in portal tributaries. Blood bypasses the liver via pre-existing anastomoses between portal and systemic veins at 4 sites.
  2. Site 1 — Lower Oesophagus: Left gastric vein (portal) ↔ oesophageal tributaries of azygos (systemic). → Oesophageal varices — most dangerous. Rupture → massive haematemesis with 30–40% mortality per episode. Endoscopic banding/sclerotherapy; propranolol prophylaxis.
  3. Site 2 — Umbilicus: Paraumbilical veins in falciform ligament (portal) ↔ superficial epigastric + thoracoepigastric veins (systemic). → Caput Medusae — dilated tortuous veins radiating from umbilicus. Flow direction: AWAY from umbilicus in all directions (distinguish from IVC obstruction where flow is upward).
  4. Site 3 — Anal Canal: Superior rectal vein (portal territory via IMV) ↔ middle + inferior rectal veins (systemic via internal iliac). → Internal haemorrhoids at anorectal junction (above dentate line = painless portal territory).
  5. Site 4 — Retroperitoneum: Veins of bare area of liver, retroperitoneal colon, and pancreas ↔ renal, lumbar, and phrenic veins. → Retzius veins; least clinically significant.
  6. Summary Concept: All sites represent embryological communications that are normally vestigial. Portal hypertension (usually from hepatic cirrhosis) reactivates them. Treat underlying cause + manage varices acutely with terlipressin + banding; electively with propranolol or TIPS.
EQ — Kidney Relations [PYQ 2014, 2021]
"Describe the anterior and posterior relations of the kidneys."
  1. Position: Retroperitoneal, on posterior abdominal wall in perirenal fat within Gerota's fascia. Right kidney: T12–L3 (lower by half a vertebra). Left kidney: T11–L2. Hilum at L1 (transpyloric plane), faces anteromedially. Hilum contents: Renal Vein (most anterior) → Renal Artery → Pelvis of ureter (most posterior) — "VAP".
  2. Anterior Relations — Right Kidney: Suprarenal gland (superomedial), right lobe of liver (upper 2/3), 2nd part of duodenum (along medial border), right colic flexure (inferolateral), jejunum (inferolateral).
  3. Anterior Relations — Left Kidney: Left suprarenal (superomedial), stomach (upper pole), spleen (upper lateral), tail of pancreas + splenic vessels (at hilum), left colic flexure + descending colon (lower lateral), loops of small intestine (lower medial).
  4. Posterior Relations (Both Kidneys): Upper 1/3: Diaphragm (separates kidney from 12th rib and costodiaphragmatic recess of pleura). Middle 1/3: Psoas major (medial) + quadratus lumborum (lateral) + transversus aponeurosis. Lower 1/3: Iliacus. Nerves behind: subcostal (T12), iliohypogastric (L1), ilioinguinal (L1) — can be damaged in lumbar approach to kidney.
  5. Clinical Application: Right kidney's relation to 2nd part of duodenum is relevant in right nephrectomy. Left kidney's relation to tail of pancreas means pancreatic pseudocysts or tumours can displace/invade the left kidney. The pleural reflection risk during nephrectomy via 11th rib approach — always check for pneumothorax post-op.
