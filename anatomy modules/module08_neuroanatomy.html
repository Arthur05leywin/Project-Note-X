Meninges & Spaces

Three meningeal layers surround the CNS from outside in: Dura mater → Arachnoid mater → Pia mater. Between them lie three spaces — only the subarachnoid space is real in life; the rest are potential spaces that open pathologically.

Extradural vs Subdural vs Subarachnoid Haematoma — Exam Differentiator

Epidural: Arterial bleed (MMA), biconvex lens shape on CT (cannot cross sutures), lucid interval (initial LOC → recovery → secondary deterioration). Temporal bone fracture = key history.

Subdural: Venous bleed (bridging veins), crescent-shaped (follows brain contour, can cross sutures), elderly or alcoholic (brain atrophied → longer bridging veins). Chronic SDH — waxing/waning confusion.

Subarachnoid: Ruptured saccular aneurysm (Circle of Willis, anterior communicating a. most common), thunderclap headache, neck stiffness, bloody + xanthochromic CSF on LP. Vasospasm 3–10 days later causes delayed ischemia.

Dural Folds (Reflections) PYQ 2014, 2019
  • Falx cerebri: Vertical sickle-shaped fold in the longitudinal fissure — separates cerebral hemispheres. Attached anteriorly to crista galli, posteriorly to tentorium.
  • Tentorium cerebelli: Horizontal shelf separating occipital lobes from cerebellum. Has a notch (incisura tentorii) for brainstem. Critical landmark — supratentorial vs infratentorial.
  • Falx cerebelli: Vertical fold between cerebellar hemispheres.
  • Diaphragma sellae: Horizontal roof of sella turcica with central aperture for pituitary stalk.
Dural Sinuses — Key ones
"Superior Sagittal → Transverse → Sigmoid → IJV"

Venous drainage route: SSS + inferior sagittal sinus → straight sinus → confluence of sinuses (torcular Herophili) → transverse → sigmoid → internal jugular vein.

Transtentorial (Uncal) Herniation

Raised ICP → uncus of temporal lobe herniates through incisura tentorii → compresses CN III (ipsilateral dilated pupil — "blown pupil") + contralateral corticospinal tract → ipsilateral eye down and out + contralateral hemiplegia. False localising sign if compression shifts to ipsilateral tract.

Pia Mater — Spinal Cord & Special Regions PYQ 2018-S

The Pia mater is the innermost layer of the meninges, a delicate, highly vascular connective tissue membrane that closely invests the brain and spinal cord, dipping into all sulci and fissures.

Key Spinal Cord Modifications:

  • Ligamentum Denticulatum: 21 pairs of tooth-like lateral processes extending from the pia between the anterior and posterior nerve roots. They pierce the arachnoid to attach to the inner surface of the dura mater, anchoring the spinal cord centrally within the vertebral canal.
  • Filum Terminale: A thread-like extension from the conus medullaris (L1-L2).
    • Filum terminale internum: Upper 15 cm within the dural sac (ends at S2).
    • Filum terminale externum: Lower 5 cm that pierces the dural sac and passes through the sacral hiatus to attach to the coccygeal periosteum.
  • Linea Splendens: A thickened longitudinal band of pia mater in the anterior median fissure of the spinal cord that supports the anterior spinal artery.
Regions Where Pia Mater is Absent

The pia mater forms a continuous barrier but is absent or modified at:

  • Apertures of the Fourth Ventricle: Foramina of Luschka (lateral) and Magendie (median) where CSF passes into the subarachnoid space.
  • Exit/Entrance Points of Nerves: Fuses with the epineurium at the transition of cranial and spinal nerves.
  • Optic Nerve Head (Optic Disc): Where the nerve fibres exit the eyeball.

Ventricular System & CSF

CSF is produced by the choroid plexus in all four ventricles (mainly lateral). Total volume ~150 mL. Production rate ~500 mL/day. Turnover ~3–4×/day. Absorbed via arachnoid granulations into superior sagittal sinus.
CSF Circulation Pathway
Lateral Ventricles (1 & 2) — largest Choroid plexus produces CSF. Parts: anterior horn (frontal lobe), body, posterior horn (occipital), inferior horn (temporal). Lined by ependyma.
↓ Interventricular Foramen of Monro
3rd Ventricle Diencephalic cavity between two thalami. Connected to 4th by aqueduct of Sylvius (cerebral aqueduct) — narrowest point = most common site of obstruction in non-communicating hydrocephalus.
↓ Cerebral Aqueduct of Sylvius (narrowest)
4th Ventricle Rhomboid shape. Floor = rhomboid fossa (brainstem nuclei). Roof: superior and inferior medullary velum. Three apertures exit CSF to subarachnoid space.
↓ Foramen of Magendie (median) + Foramina of Luschka (2 lateral)
Subarachnoid Space → Cerebellomedullary Cistern (Cisterna Magna) CSF circulates in subarachnoid space around brain and spinal cord. Cisternal spaces at base of brain: cisterna magna, pontine cistern, interpeduncular cistern, chiasmatic cistern.
↓ Arachnoid Granulations (Villi) project into Superior Sagittal Sinus
Absorbed into Superior Sagittal Sinus → Venous Blood Passive pressure-dependent absorption. ICP > venous pressure = absorption occurs.

Hydrocephalus — Communicating vs Non-communicating

Non-communicating (obstructive): Block within ventricular system. Most common at aqueduct of Sylvius (stenosis, tumour). Only ventricles upstream dilate. Treat: VP shunt.

Communicating: CSF reaches subarachnoid space but absorption fails (post-meningitis adhesions, blocked arachnoid granulations). All ventricles dilate. NPH = communicating hydrocephalus in elderly — triad: wet, wobbly, wacky (incontinence, gait apraxia, dementia).

Lumbar Puncture — Anatomy

Performed at L3–L4 or L4–L5 interspace (below conus medullaris at L1–L2). Patient in lateral decubitus, knees to chest. Layers pierced: skin → superficial fascia → supraspinous lig → interspinous lig → ligamentum flavum → epidural space → dura → arachnoid → subarachnoid space. Opening pressure measured before any fluid withdrawn.

Floor of the Fourth Ventricle (Rhomboid Fossa) ⭐ PYQ 2017, 2024

The floor of the 4th ventricle is a diamond-shaped (rhomboid) depression formed by the posterior surfaces of the pons (upper part) and the open medulla (lower part).

Key Anatomical Features:

  • Median Sulcus: Divides the floor into symmetrical left and right halves.
  • Sulcus Limitans: Longitudinal groove lateral to the median sulcus; represents the embryonic boundary between motor (alar) and sensory (basal) plates.
  • Facial Colliculus: A prominent swelling in the pontine part, formed by fibers of the facial nerve (CN VII) looping around the nucleus of the abducens nerve (CN VI). Lesion here causes concurrent facial palsy and medial squint (CN VI palsy).
  • Vestibular Area: A lateral region overlying the vestibular nuclei.
  • Striae Medullares: Transverse white fibers crossing from the median sulcus to the lateral recesses; separates the pontine and medullary parts of the floor.
  • Hypoglossal & Vagal Triangles: Small triangular depressions in the medullary part:
    • Hypoglossal triangle: Medial, overlying the hypoglossal (CN XII) nucleus.
    • Vagal triangle (ala cinerea): Lateral/inferior, overlying the dorsal motor nucleus of vagus (CN X).

Spinal Cord

Spinal cord extends from foramen magnum (continuous with medulla) to L1–L2 in adults (conus medullaris). Below L2: cauda equina (motor + sensory roots). Filum terminale anchors conus to coccyx.
Spinal Cord — Key Structural Facts ⭐ PYQ 2011, 2014, 2018, 2022

Extent & Relationships:

  • Begins at foramen magnum (C1 level), continuous with medulla oblongata
  • Ends at conus medullaris — L1/L2 in adults; L3 at birth (cord ascends relative to spine as child grows)
  • Two enlargements: Cervical (C5–T1) — for upper limb; Lumbosacral (L1–S3) — for lower limb
  • 31 pairs of spinal nerves (8C, 12T, 5L, 5S, 1Co)
  • C1–C7 nerves exit ABOVE their vertebra; C8 exits below C7; T1 downward exit BELOW their vertebra

Cross-sectional Anatomy (H-shaped grey matter):

  • Anterior horn (ventral): Motor neurons (alpha, gamma). Source of LMN to muscles
  • Posterior horn (dorsal): Receives sensory input from dorsal root ganglion neurons
  • Lateral horn: Present only at T1–L2 (sympathetic preganglionic) and S2–S4 (parasympathetic preganglionic)
  • White matter: Posterior funiculus (dorsal columns), lateral funiculus, anterior funiculus

Tract Crossing Rule
"Pain crosses FAST (at cord) — Touch crosses LATE (at medulla)"

Spinothalamic (pain/temp) crosses within 1–2 segments in cord. Dorsal columns (touch/proprioception) travel all the way up to medulla before crossing. This explains Brown-Séquard: ipsilateral motor loss + proprioception loss, but CONTRALATERAL pain/temp loss.

Brainstem

Brainstem = Midbrain + Pons + Medulla oblongata. Contains cranial nerve nuclei III–XII, all ascending/descending tracts, and the reticular formation (consciousness). All are vital — brainstem death = death.

Transverse Section of Midbrain at Superior Colliculus ⭐ PYQ 2022, 2025

A transverse section of the midbrain at the level of the Superior Colliculus (responsible for visual reflexes) reveals four major divisions from anterior to posterior: Crus Cerebri, Substantia Nigra, Tegmentum, and Tectum.

Key Internal Structures:

  • Oculomotor Nucleus (CN III) & EW Nucleus: Located in the ventral part of the periaqueductal gray (PAG). Fibers pass anteriorly through the tegmentum, red nucleus, and medial margin of the crus cerebri to exit into the interpeduncular fossa.
  • Red Nucleus: Large, highly vascularized cell mass in the central tegmentum; part of the extrapyramidal system (rubrospinal tract).
  • Substantia Nigra: A crescent-shaped band of dark, pigmented neuromelanin-containing dopaminergic neurons. Divides the peduncle into crus cerebri and tegmentum.
  • Crus Cerebri: Flat band of descending motor fibers:
    • Middle 3/5: Corticospinal and corticobulbar fibers.
    • Medial 1/5: Frontopontine fibers.
    • Lateral 1/5: Temporopontine, parietopontine, and occipitopontine fibers.
  • Tectum: Formed by the bilateral superior colliculi, connected by the posterior commissure.
Weber's Syndrome — Crossed Hemiplegia

Occurs due to occlusion of paramedian branches of the PCA, damaging the ventral midbrain (crus cerebri + CN III fibers). Clinically results in:

  • Ipsilateral CN III palsy: Ptosis, dilated pupil, "down and out" eyeball.
  • Contralateral hemiplegia: Paralysis of contralateral limbs and lower face (corticospinal/corticobulbar tract damage).

Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) Occlusion & Aphasia

A stroke in the main stem of the dominant MCA causes Global Aphasia (loss of all language components — non-fluent, impaired comprehension, impaired repetition) combined with severe contralateral hemiplegia and hemianesthesia of the face and upper limb.

Speech Areas — Dominant Hemisphere (L in 95% R-handers)
"Broca = Broken speech (can't produce). Wernicke = Weird speech (can't understand)."

Arcuate fasciculus connects Wernicke's ↔ Broca's. Damage to this → conduction aphasia: fluent speech, normal comprehension, unable to REPEAT.

Homunculus (Motor & Sensory)

Medial surface of precentral/postcentral gyri = leg, perineum. Lateral surface = trunk → arm → hand → face (face most inferior). Hand area is disproportionately large (fine movements). UCA (upper limb) blood supply from ACA (medial) → ACA stroke causes leg weakness. MCA territory damage → face + arm weakness, aphasia.

Key Limbic Structures & Functions
  • Hippocampus: Declarative (explicit) memory — converting STM to LTM. Bilateral damage → profound anterograde amnesia (patient HM). Also spatial navigation (grid + place cells).
  • Amygdala: Fear conditioning, emotional responses (fear, aggression), recognition of emotional facial expressions. Bilateral lesion (Klüver-Bucy syndrome): hypersexuality, hyperphagia, visual agnosia, docility.
  • Cingulate gyrus: Emotion regulation, pain processing, cognitive function (anterior), motor control (posterior). Anterior cingulate lesion → akinetic mutism.
  • Fornix: Main output tract of hippocampus. Bilateral lesion (fornix transection) → severe memory impairment.
Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome

Thiamine (B1) deficiency (classically alcohol) → damage to mammillary bodies + medial thalamus → Wernicke's encephalopathy: confusion + ataxia + ophthalmoplegia (triad). If untreated → Korsakoff psychosis: anterograde amnesia + confabulation (fills gaps in memory with false stories). Treat immediately with IV thiamine BEFORE glucose.

Fornix — Structure, Parts & Connections PYQ 2017-S

The Fornix is a prominent, C-shaped bundle of white matter projection fibers that acts as the primary efferent pathway of the hippocampus, connecting it to the hypothalamus (mammillary bodies) and other limbic structures.

Structure and Divisions (From Posterior to Anterior):

  • Alveus & Fimbria: The alveus is a thin layer of myelinated axons on the ventricular surface of the hippocampus. These consolidate medially to form the fimbria.
  • Crura (Left & Right): The fimbriae continue backwards under the splenium of the corpus callosum as the two crura, which are connected by the hippocampal commissure (transverse fibers crossing to share memory traces).
  • Body: The crura unite under the corpus callosum to form the body of the fornix. It is attached to the under surface of the septum pellucidum.
  • Columns (Anterior Columns): The body splits anteriorly into two columns. They curve downward in the anterior wall of the 3rd ventricle (bounding the interventricular foramen of Monro anteriorly).
Fiber Pathways of Columns

Upon reaching the anterior commissure, the columns split into:

  • Precommissural fibers: Pass in front of the anterior commissure to supply the septal nuclei, preoptic area, and frontal cortex.
  • Postcommissural fibers: Pass behind the anterior commissure to terminate in the mammillary bodies (core of the Papez circuit) and the anterior thalamic nucleus.

Clinical: Bilateral transection of the fornix (e.g., during surgical excision of colloid cysts of the third ventricle) leads to severe, permanent anterograde amnesia (inability to form new memories).

Meninges, CSF & Ventricular System
Differentiate epidural, subdural, and subarachnoid haemorrhage.PYQ 2016, 2021

Epidural (extradural): Arterial — usually middle meningeal artery after temporal bone fracture. Between skull and periosteal dura. Biconvex/lenticular on CT (cannot cross sutures). Lucid interval (initial LOC → recovery → rapid deterioration from haematoma expanding). Treat: emergency burr holes/craniotomy.

Subdural: Venous — bridging veins (superior cerebral veins crossing to SSS). Between dura and arachnoid. Crescent-shaped on CT (follows brain contour, CAN cross sutures). Seen in elderly (atrophy stretches bridging veins), alcoholics, anticoagulated patients. No lucid interval — slow onset with waxing/waning confusion (chronic SDH).

Subarachnoid: Arterial aneurysm rupture (85% saccular at Circle of Willis — ACoA most common) or AVM. Into subarachnoid space. CT: blood in cisterns/sulci. LP: uniformly bloodstained → xanthochromic after 2–4 hours (oxyhaemoglobin → bilirubin). Thunderclap headache. Vasospasm: 3–10 days. Treat: nimodipine (CCB) to prevent vasospasm + surgical clipping/coiling.

Key memory: Epidural = Arterial = biconvex = lucid interval. Subdural = Venous = crescent = elderly. SAH = Aneurysm = thunderclap = xanthochromic CSF.

Name the foramina of the 4th ventricle. What is their clinical significance?PYQ 2019

The 4th ventricle has three apertures through which CSF exits into the subarachnoid space:

  • Foramen of Magendie (median aperture): Single, in the midline of the inferior part of the roof (tela choroidea). CSF flows into the cisterna magna (cerebellomedullary cistern).
  • Foramina of Luschka (lateral apertures): Two, one on each side of the lateral recess of the 4th ventricle. Open into the cerebellopontine angle cisterns.

Clinical significance: Blockage of all three (e.g., by a posterior fossa tumour — medulloblastoma, ependymoma; or post-meningitis adhesions) → obstructive hydrocephalus. Dandy-Walker malformation: atresia of foramina of Magendie and Luschka + cystic dilation of 4th ventricle → non-communicating hydrocephalus + absent/hypoplastic cerebellar vermis.

Distinction: Magendie = median = one; Luschka = lateral = two. Remember "ML" — median then lateral, Magendie then Luschka.

Spinal Cord & Tracts
Describe Brown-Séquard syndrome. What lesion produces it?PYQ 2017, 2022

Cause: Hemisection of the spinal cord. Classically: penetrating trauma (knife wound), demyelinating plaque (MS), cord tumour pressing on one side, radiation myelopathy.

Ipsilateral to lesion (SAME SIDE):

  • At the level of lesion: LMN signs (flaccid paralysis, areflexia, fasciculations)
  • Below the level: UMN signs (spastic paralysis, hyperreflexia, Babinski) — lateral corticospinal tract
  • Below the level: Loss of vibration, proprioception, fine touch — posterior columns (crosses in medulla)

Contralateral to lesion (OPPOSITE SIDE):

  • Below the level (2 segments below): Loss of pain and temperature — spinothalamic tract (crosses in cord at entry level via anterior commissure)
  • Crude touch partially preserved (bilateral representation in anterior spinothalamic + DCML)

Mnemonic: "SAME side = lost position/power. OPPOSITE side = lost pain."

What is syringomyelia? What are its characteristic features?PYQ 2020

Syringomyelia: Fluid-filled cavity (syrinx) within the spinal cord, most common in the cervical region. Associated with Chiari malformation type I (most common), cord tumours, trauma, arachnoiditis.

Pathology: Syrinx expands → first destroys the decussating spinothalamic fibres in the anterior commissure (central) → then as syrinx enlarges → anterior horn cells → lateral corticospinal tracts.

Classic clinical features (stepwise):

  • Earliest: "Cape" or "shawl" distribution of dissociated sensory loss — bilateral loss of pain + temperature over shoulders, arms, upper chest — but light touch/proprioception PRESERVED (spinothalamic affected first, DCML spared)
  • Later: LMN signs in upper limbs (anterior horn involvement — wasting, weakness, areflexia)
  • Later: UMN signs in lower limbs (corticospinal tract compression)
  • Horner's syndrome (ciliospinal centre at C8–T1 affected)

Key: dissociated sensory loss in a cape distribution = syringomyelia until proven otherwise.

Wallenberg's (Lateral Medullary) Syndrome: Infarction of lateral medulla, usually from PICA (posterior inferior cerebellar artery) occlusion or vertebral artery occlusion.

What is the significance of CN VI palsy as a false localising sign?PYQ 2021

CN VI (abducens) has the longest intracranial course of any cranial nerve — it runs from its nucleus in the pons tegmentum, passes upward and forward, pierces the dura at the apex of petrous bone, runs through the cavernous sinus, and exits via the superior orbital fissure. This long course makes it vulnerable to traction and stretching from any cause of raised intracranial pressure — even if the primary pathology is remote from the pons.

False localising sign: CN VI palsy appears to localise the lesion to the pons/posterior fossa, but the actual cause (tumour, haemorrhage, hydrocephalus) may be in the supratentorial compartment. The palsy results from downward displacement of the brainstem stretching the nerve.

Clinically: Medial squint (LR muscle paralysed → medial rectus unopposed), inability to abduct the eye on the affected side, horizontal diplopia (maximum on gaze towards lesion).

Other false localising signs: contralateral hemiplegia from Kernohan's notch (compression of opposite cerebral peduncle against tentorium), ipsilateral CN III palsy from uncal herniation.

Basal Ganglia, Thalamus & Blood Supply
Compare Parkinson's disease and Huntington's disease — basal ganglia basis.PYQ 2019, 2022

Parkinson's disease:

  • Loss of dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra pars compacta
  • ↓ DA → direct pathway underactive (less movement promotion) + indirect pathway overactive (more movement suppression) → net: GPi overactive → thalamus over-inhibited → ↓ cortical activation
  • Hypokinesia (akinesia, bradykinesia), resting tremor (3–5 Hz, pill-rolling), cogwheel/lead-pipe rigidity, postural instability, festinant gait
  • Treat: L-DOPA + carbidopa (peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor), dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors

Huntington's disease:

  • Degeneration of striatum (caudate nucleus first) — GABAergic neurons lost
  • Loss of striatal inhibition of GPe (indirect pathway) → GPe overactive → STN underactive → GPi underactive → thalamus disinhibited → excessive cortical activation
  • Hyperkinesia (chorea), dementia, psychiatric disturbance (depression, psychosis), dysphagia
  • CAG repeat expansion on chromosome 4 (huntingtin gene), autosomal dominant, anticipation (worsens each generation)
  • No cure; symptomatic — tetrabenazine for chorea (depletes DA/monoamines)

Rule: PD = hypokinesia = ↓DA. HD = hyperkinesia = ↓GABA. Opposite movements, opposite neurotransmitters.

"Explain Why" Clinical Reasoning Q&A
Why does a patient with a pituitary tumor suffer from bitemporal hemianopia?PYQ 2016, 2018

1. The pituitary gland lies in the sella turcica, directly inferior to the optic chiasm.

2. A pituitary tumor (macroadenoma) expanding upwards compresses the central part of the optic chiasm.

3. The central chiasm contains the crossing fibers from the nasal halves of both retinas (which carry light signals from the temporal visual fields of both eyes).

4. Therefore, selective compression of these decussating fibers leads to loss of the temporal visual fields in both eyes, resulting in bitemporal hemianopia (tunnel vision).

Why does the obliquity and length of the spinal nerve roots increase progressively from above downwards?PYQ 2016

1. In early embryonic life, the spinal cord and the vertebral column are equal in length, and spinal nerve roots exit horizontally through their corresponding intervertebral foramina.

2. During development, the vertebral column grows much faster than the spinal cord (differential growth). In adults, the spinal cord terminates at the L1–L2 vertebra level, whereas the vertebral column extends much lower.

3. To reach their respective intervertebral foramina (which are located much lower than their origin segments on the spinal cord), the lumbar and sacral nerve roots must travel downwards for a significant distance within the vertebral canal.

4. As a result, the nerve roots become increasingly longer and more oblique from above downwards, eventually forming a bundle resembling a horse's tail below the conus medullaris, known as the cauda equina.

Why can ischemic damage to the anterior limb of the internal capsule affect recent memory?PYQ 2019

1. The anterior limb of the internal capsule contains projection fibers (thalamocortical tracts) that connect the anterior group of thalamic nuclei to the cingulate gyrus.

2. These structures and their connection form a major part of the Papez circuit, which is critical for emotional expression and the consolidation of short-term memory into long-term memory (recent memory tracing).

3. Ischemic injury to the anterior limb (e.g. occlusion of Heubner's artery or anteromedial branches of ACA) interrupts this tract, preventing information flow in the Papez circuit and leading to loss of recent memory consolidation.

Why does a lesion in the hypothalamus lead to Diabetes Insipidus?PYQ 2023

1. The supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus synthesize antidiuretic hormone (ADH / vasopressin).

2. ADH is transported axonally along the hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract to the posterior pituitary, where it is stored and released into the blood.

3. A lesion in the hypothalamus (due to trauma, tumor, or surgery) destroys these nuclei or the tract, leading to a severe deficiency of ADH.

4. In the absence of ADH, the renal collecting ducts become impermeable to water, preventing water reabsorption. This results in the excretion of massive volumes of dilute urine (polyuria) and extreme compensatory thirst (polydipsia) — the hallmarks of Central Diabetes Insipidus.

Explain the anatomical basis and mechanism of the Argyll-Robertson Pupil.PYQ 2012-S

1. The Argyll-Robertson pupil is characterized by a pupil that accommodates to near vision but does not react to light ("accommodation-light dissociation"). It is classically associated with neurosyphilis.

2. The lesion is located in the pretectal area of the midbrain tectum.

3. The light reflex pathway requires synapsing in the pretectal nucleus and crossing in the posterior commissure to reach the Edinger-Westphal nuclei bilaterally. A lesion in the pretectum blocks this reflex.

4. The accommodation reflex pathway, however, bypasses the pretectum and descends directly from the visual association cortex via a more ventral route to the Edinger-Westphal nuclei. This ventral pathway is spared, allowing pupillary constriction during accommodation.

Why is abducens nerve (CN VI) palsy a common manifestation of increased intracranial pressure?PYQ 2019, 2019-S

1. The abducens nerve exits the brainstem at the pontomedullary junction and must travel a long distance upwards along the skull base to enter the cavernous sinus.

2. During this course, it makes a sharp 90-degree bend over the sharp petrous ridge of the temporal bone.

3. When intracranial pressure rises, the brainstem is displaced downwards toward the foramen magnum. This downward movement stretches the nerve and compresses it against the petrous ridge.

4. Due to this mechanical vulnerability, CN VI is frequently damaged in raised ICP, resulting in paralysis of the lateral rectus muscle, horizontal diplopia, and medial strabismus (convergent squint). This is a classic false localizing sign because the lesion is not in the pons itself.

EQ — Describe the Ventricular System and Circulation of CSF [PYQ 2015, 2020, 2024]
"Describe the ventricular system of the brain. Add a note on the formation, circulation, and absorption of CSF."
  1. Introduction: The ventricular system consists of four connected cavities within the brain — two lateral ventricles, the 3rd ventricle, and the 4th ventricle — continuous with the central canal of the spinal cord. It produces and circulates cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which cushions the CNS, provides buoyancy, and maintains chemical homeostasis.
  2. Ventricular Anatomy: Lateral ventricles (1 and 2) occupy each cerebral hemisphere and have four parts: anterior horn (frontal), body, posterior horn (occipital), and inferior horn (temporal). They communicate with the 3rd ventricle via the paired interventricular foramina of Monro. The 3rd ventricle lies between the diencephalon (thalami). The cerebral aqueduct of Sylvius (narrowest point — site of obstruction in non-communicating hydrocephalus) connects the 3rd to the 4th ventricle. The 4th ventricle overlies the pons and medulla, with a rhomboid fossa as its floor.
  3. Formation of CSF: Produced by the choroid plexus in all ventricles (predominantly in lateral ventricles). Rate: ~500 mL/day. Total volume at any time: ~150 mL (70 mL in ventricles, 80 mL in subarachnoid space). Formation involves active secretion of Na⁺ by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase in choroid epithelium, drawing water osmotically. Carbonic anhydrase also contributes; inhibited by acetazolamide (reduces CSF production).
  4. Circulation: CSF flows from lateral → 3rd → aqueduct → 4th ventricle → subarachnoid space via median foramen of Magendie and lateral foramina of Luschka. CSF then circulates up over cerebral convexities and down into spinal subarachnoid space. Pulsatile, driven by choroid plexus secretion pressure and arterial pulsation.
  5. Absorption: Arachnoid granulations (Pacchionian bodies) project into the superior sagittal sinus and other venous sinuses. CSF absorbed by bulk flow when CSF pressure exceeds venous pressure. Also by spinal arachnoid granulations along nerve roots and lymphatic vessels near cribriform plate. Absorbed at same rate as produced to maintain constant pressure.
  6. Clinical correlates: Hydrocephalus = excess CSF accumulation — non-communicating (aqueduct block) or communicating (impaired absorption post-meningitis). Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH): Hakim's triad — urinary incontinence + gait apraxia + dementia. Pseudotumour cerebri (idiopathic intracranial hypertension): raised ICP without mass — headache, papilloedema, visual loss — treat with acetazolamide + LP.
EQ — Internal Capsule: Anatomy and Clinical Significance [PYQ 2013, 2017, 2021]
"Describe the internal capsule. What are the effects of lesion of the internal capsule?"
  1. Definition & Position: The internal capsule is a compact band of white matter fibres that lies between the lentiform nucleus (lateral) and the head of caudate nucleus and thalamus (medial). On horizontal section, it appears as a V or boomerang shape opening laterally. It connects the cerebral cortex to subcortical structures in both directions (corticofugal = down; thalamocortical = up).
  2. Subdivisions and contents: The internal capsule has five parts: (a) Anterior limb: between head of caudate and anterior lentiform nucleus. Contains frontopontine fibres and anterior thalamic radiation. (b) Genu: at the angle of the V. Contains corticobulbar (corticonuclear) fibres to cranial nerve motor nuclei (face, tongue, jaw). (c) Posterior limb: between thalamus and posterior lentiform nucleus. Contains — from anterior to posterior — corticospinal fibres, superior thalamic radiation (VPL/VPM → somatosensory cortex), corticorubral fibres. (d) Retrolenticular part: behind lentiform nucleus — optic radiation (LGN → visual cortex). (e) Sublenticular part: below lentiform nucleus — auditory radiation (MGN → Heschl's), temporopontine fibres.
  3. Blood supply: Primarily by lenticulostriate arteries — branches of MCA (medial and lateral groups) and ACA. These are end arteries with no collateral supply. Charcot called them "arteries of cerebral haemorrhage" — most common site of hypertensive haemorrhage (usually posterior limb).
  4. Effects of complete internal capsule lesion: Contralateral spastic hemiplegia (posterior limb, corticospinal) with UMN signs below — spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski positive. Contralateral hemisensory loss (posterior limb, thalamocortical fibres). Contralateral lower facial palsy (genu, corticobulbar — upper face spared by bilateral supply). Contralateral homonymous hemianopia (optic radiation, retrolenticular part). Aphasia if dominant hemisphere. Consciousness preserved (no damage to ARAS or cerebral cortex).
  5. Pure motor hemiplegia: Small capsular lacunar infarct (posterior limb) from hypertensive small vessel disease — contralateral face, arm, and leg weakness without sensory loss, aphasia, or visual field defect. Most common lacunar syndrome.
EQ — Circle of Willis: Anatomy & Clinical Significance [PYQ 2011, 2014, 2017, 2022]
"Describe the Circle of Willis. What is its clinical significance?"
  1. Definition: The Circle of Willis (circulus arteriosus cerebri) is an anastomotic ring of arteries at the base of the brain, lying in the interpeduncular cistern. It was described by Thomas Willis in 1664. It forms the primary collateral circulation for the brain.
  2. Components (anterior from posterior): Formed by — Anterior communicating artery (ACoA, single) connecting right and left anterior cerebral arteries (ACA) → two ACAs → two internal carotid arteries (ICA) → two posterior communicating arteries (PCoA) connecting ICA to posterior cerebral arteries → two PCAs → basilar artery (formed by union of two vertebral arteries, not part of circle itself). Complete circle provides communication between right/left, and anterior/posterior circulation.
  3. Normal variation: Complete classical circle present in only ~20–25% of individuals. Common variants: absent/hypoplastic ACoA; fetal origin of PCA (PCA arises from ICA, not basilar); absent PCoA. These variations reduce collateral protection.
  4. Clinical significance: (a) Collateral circulation: Occlusion of one ICA may be compensated by cross-flow through ACoA or retrograde through PCoA. (b) Intracranial aneurysms: Saccular (berry) aneurysms arise predominantly at arterial junctions in the circle (bifurcation points, high haemodynamic stress). ACoA (35%) = most common, ACoA-ACA junction. PCoA (25%) — CN III palsy (pupillomotor fibres peripheral → compressed first). ICA bifurcation (20%). Basilar tip. Rupture → subarachnoid haemorrhage. (c) Embolism: Thrombi from heart/ICA lodge at bifurcation points of the circle. (d) Moyamoya disease: Progressive stenosis of ICA and proximal MCA/ACA → ischaemia + characteristic "puff of smoke" angiographic collaterals at base.
  5. Functional anatomy insight: MCA territory (lateral hemisphere) is the most common site of territorial infarction — face + arm weakness + aphasia (dominant). ACA territory (medial) → leg weakness predominant (parasagittal cortex). PCA → hemianopia. The circle redistributes pressure if one vessel is occluded, provided the communicating arteries are patent.
