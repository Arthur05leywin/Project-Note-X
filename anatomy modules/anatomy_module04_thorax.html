Sternal Angle (Angle of Louis) — All Landmarks at T4/T5 ⭐ PYQ EVERY YEAR
Junction of manubrium + body of sternum. Palpable ridge. Level: T4/T5 intervertebral disc. The most clinically important thoracic landmark.

WHAT LIES AT T4/T5 LEVEL:

  • 2nd costal cartilage articulates here → count ribs from sternal angle
  • Trachea bifurcates → carina → left & right main bronchi
  • Arch of aorta begins (ascending → arch) and ends (arch → descending)
  • Thoracic duct crosses midline (right to left)
  • Azygos vein arches over right lung root → joins SVC
  • Upper border of SVC
  • Ligamentum arteriosum (remnant of ductus arteriosus)
  • Left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under aortic arch

CLINICAL USES:

  • Reference to count intercostal spaces (2nd ICS found lateral to sternal angle)
  • Landmark for auscultation areas
  • In CT reporting: "at the level of the carina" = T4/T5
  • Central venous catheter tip should be ABOVE this level (to avoid entering right atrium)

Intercostal Muscles (3 layers):

  • External intercostal: Fibres run downward + forward ("hands in jacket pockets"). Active in inspiration. Most lateral; ends at costochondral junction → replaced by anterior intercostal membrane
  • Internal intercostal: Fibres run downward + backward (opposite to external). Active in forced expiration
  • Innermost intercostal: Incomplete, parallel to internal; VAN bundle runs between internal and innermost

Intercostal Nerves:

  • Anterior rami of T1–T11 thoracic spinal nerves (T12 = subcostal nerve).
  • Typical Nerves (3rd-6th): Confined to their own intercostal spaces.
  • Atypical Nerves:
    • 1st: Joins brachial plexus; small branch to 1st space.
    • 2nd: Gives off intercostobrachial nerve (sensation to medial arm/axilla), responsible for cardiac referred pain.
    • 7th-11th (Thoracoabdominal): Supply both thoracic and abdominal walls.
  • Intercostal nerve block: Inject local anaesthetic posterior to angle of rib, above UPPER border of lower rib to avoid VAN.
Thoracocentesis (Pleural Tap)

Needle inserted in posterior axillary line, upper border of lower rib (usually 7th ICS), to access costodiaphragmatic recess. Mid-axillary line used to avoid serratus anterior (medially) and latissimus dorsi (posteriorly). Always over UPPER BORDER to avoid VAN.

Azygos Vein System & SVC Collaterals PYQ 2011 supple, 2018 supple, 2019

The azygos venous system drains the back and thoracic/abdominal walls, serving as an important collateral pathway between the SVC and IVC.

The Azygos Vein:

  • Origin: Formed at the level of the L1/L2 vertebrae by the union of the right ascending lumbar vein and right subcostal vein.
  • Course: Enters the thorax through the aortic opening of the diaphragm (or pierces the right crus) and ascends in the posterior mediastinum to the right of the thoracic duct and aorta.
  • Termination: At the level of the T4 vertebrae (sternal angle), it arches forward over the root of the right lung to empty into the **Superior Vena Cava (SVC)**.
  • Tributaries: Hemiazygos vein (at T9), Accessory hemiazygos vein (at T8), right 5th–11th posterior intercostal veins, right superior intercostal vein, and bronchial/esophageal veins.

Left-sided Counterparts & SVC Obstruction:

  • Hemiazygos Vein (Inferior): Formed on the left side (ascending lumbar + subcostal); ascends to T9 level and crosses the vertebral column behind the aorta and thoracic duct to drain into the azygos vein.
  • Accessory Hemiazygos Vein (Superior): Descends on the left side of the spine from T4 to T8; crosses the spinal column at T8 to drain into the azygos vein.
  • SVC Obstruction Collateral Pathways:
    - **Above Azygos Opening:** Blood from the upper body bypasses the obstruction via collateral routes (e.g., internal thoracic, posterior intercostal) into the azygos vein, which still drains into the SVC.
    - **Below Azygos Opening:** Azygos flow is reversed; blood travels down the azygos vein into the ascending lumbar and iliac veins to enter the **Inferior Vena Cava (IVC)** (cavocaval shunt). Dilated superficial abdominal veins (thoracoepigastric veins) appear on the skin.

Pleura

Pleural Cavities
Pleural reflection and recesses — Gray's Anatomy
Pleural Layers & Recesses PYQ 2012, 2016-S, 2021

Parietal Pleura — 4 Parts:

  • Costal: Lines inner surface of ribs + intercostal spaces. Supplied by intercostal nerves (somatic → sharp, well-localised pain)
  • Mediastinal: Lines lateral surface of mediastinum. Supplied by phrenic nerve
  • Diaphragmatic: Lines upper surface of diaphragm. Central part → phrenic; peripheral → intercostal
  • Cervical (Cupola): Projects 2.5 cm above medial 1/3 of clavicle into root of neck. At risk during subclavian line insertion → pneumothorax
Visceral pleura has NO pain fibres — only stretch receptors. Pain in pleurisy = parietal pleura involved.

Pleural Recesses:

  • Costodiaphragmatic recess: Between costal + diaphragmatic parietal pleura. Most dependent part → fluid collects here first. Depth: 5–7 cm (right), 5 cm (left). Dull to percussion when >200–300 mL
  • Costomediastinal recess: Between costal + mediastinal pleura, anterior. Small; obliterated on deep inspiration by lingula (left) or middle lobe (right)

Surface Markings of Pleura:

  • Both pleura meet midline at 4th rib → cardiac notch causes left to deviate laterally
  • Reflect down: 6th CC (midclavicular) → 8th rib (midaxillary) → 10th/12th rib (paravertebral)
  • Lung surface marking: 2 rib spaces ABOVE pleura at each line (i.e., lung doesn't fill entire pleural cavity)
Pleural Effusion vs Pneumothorax

Pleural effusion: Fluid in pleural cavity → dull percussion, decreased breath sounds, trachea deviation AWAY (large effusion). Aspirate from costodiaphragmatic recess.

Pneumothorax: Air in pleural cavity → hyper-resonance, decreased breath sounds, trachea deviation TOWARD (tension = away). Tension pneumothorax → needle decompression → 2nd ICS, midclavicular line → insert over upper border of 3rd rib.

Bronchopulmonary Segments

DIAGRAM 04 & 05 — Bronchopulmonary Segments
Bronchopulmonary segments of the right and left lungs
Definition: The smallest functionally + surgically independent unit of the lung. Each segment has its own: segmental bronchus, segmental artery, lymphatics, autonomic nerves. Veins are INTERSEGMENTAL (run in planes between segments — used as surgical planes for segmentectomy).
Clinical Importance of Bronchopulmonary Segments

Segmentectomy: Surgical resection of one segment while preserving rest of lobe. Possible because segments are independent units with intersegmental veins as planes.

Postural drainage: Position patient to gravity-drain specific segment (e.g., posterior segments drain with patient prone tilted).

Inhaled foreign body: Right > left; goes to posterior basal segment (lower lobe) in erect, or posterior segment (upper lobe) in supine patient.

Foreign Body Aspiration — Which Segment?

Erect patient: Right > lower lobe > posterior basal segment (S10) — gravity pulls fluid/object downward + backward.

Supine patient: Right > lower lobe > superior segment (S6) — most dependent posterior segment in supine position.

Right-sided preference: Short, wide, vertical right main bronchus is almost direct continuation of trachea.

Cardiac Septa Development

Development of the cardiac septa is a complex embryological process involving partition of both atria and ventricles. Failures in this process lead to common congenital cardiac anomalies like ASD, VSD, and TOF.
Interatrial Septum Development ⭐ PYQ 2018, 2023, 2024

The partitioning of the primitive atrium is accomplished by the growth of two overlapping septa:

Septum / ComponentAnatomical Description & Growth DirectionOpenings & ShuntsFate / Adult Remnant
Septum PrimumA thin, sickle-shaped membrane that grows downwards from the roof of the atrium toward the endocardial cushions.1. Ostium (Foramen) Primum: Gap at the lower border before it fuses with cushions.
2. Ostium (Foramen) Secundum: Formed by apoptosis in its upper part before the ostium primum closes.		Fuses with the endocardial cushions. Forms the floor of the **fossa ovalis** (valve of the foramen ovale).
Septum SecundumA thick, muscular crescentic fold that grows from the roof of the right atrium, immediately to the right of the septum primum.Foramen Ovale: An oblique opening between its lower edge and the septum primum, allowing right-to-left blood shunt in the fetus (bypassing lungs).Its free margin forms the **limbus fossae ovalis** (anulus ovalis).
Physiological Closure at Birth

At birth, the expansion of the lungs increases left atrial blood pressure, and cessation of umbilical flow decreases right atrial pressure. This pressure difference pushes the flexible septum primum against the rigid septum secundum, functionally closing the foramen ovale. Structural fusion occurs in the first year of life.

Interventricular Septum Development ⭐ PYQ 2018, 2023, 2024

The interventricular septum (IVS) partitions the primitive ventricle into right and left ventricles, and is formed by the fusion of three distinct embryological components:

  • Muscular Part: Grows upwards from the apex of the primitive ventricle toward the endocardial cushions. It forms the thick, muscular lower 5/6 of the adult IVS.
  • Membranous Part: Closes the remaining interventricular foramen. It is formed by the downward growth and fusion of the right and left bulbar (conotruncal) ridges with the posterior edge of the endocardial cushions. It forms the thin, fibrous upper 1/6 of the adult IVS.
  • Bulbar (Spiral) Septum: Derived from neural crest cells, partitions the bulbus cordis and truncus arteriosus into the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk.
Congenital Septal & Valvular Anomalies PYQ 2018, 2023, 2024

Atrial Septal Defects (ASD):

  • Ostium Secundum Defect: Most common type. Caused by excessive resorption of the septum primum or inadequate growth of the septum secundum, leaving a large open hole in the center of the septum.
  • Ostium Primum Defect: Caused by failure of the septum primum to fuse with the endocardial cushions. Often associated with mitral valve clefts.
  • Patent Foramen Ovale (PFO): Failure of the septum primum and secundum to fuse after birth (probe-patent PFO exists in ~25% of normal population, usually asymptomatic but can cause paradoxical emboli).

Ventricular & Aorticopulmonary Defects:

  • Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD): The most common congenital cardiac malformation. Most commonly occurs in the **membranous part** of the IVS, due to failure of the bulbar ridges and endocardial cushions to fuse. Causes a left-to-right shunt (pansystolic murmur).
  • Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF): Caused by the **anterosuperior displacement of the aorticopulmonary septum** (conotruncal division defect). Consists of: (1) VSD, (2) Pulmonary stenosis, (3) Overriding aorta, (4) Right ventricular hypertrophy.
  • Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA): Ductus arteriosus fails to close. Leads to a continuous "machinery" murmur.

Coronary Arteries

DIAGRAM 08 — Coronary Arteries
Coronary arteries and their branches — Gray's Anatomy
Both coronary arteries arise from the ascending aorta within the aortic sinuses (sinuses of Valsalva). They fill during diastole (when aortic valve is closed and sinuses are not obliterated). This is why HR matters — tachycardia shortens diastole → reduced coronary filling.
Coronary Artery Territories ⭐ PYQ 2012-S, 2015, 2018, 2023

Left Coronary Artery (LCA):

Arises from LEFT aortic sinus → short left main → divides into:

LAD (Left Anterior Descending) = "Artery of Sudden Death"

  • Runs in anterior interventricular groove
  • Supplies: anterior wall LV, anterior 2/3 of interventricular septum, apex, right bundle branch, anterior fascicle of LBB
  • Diagonal branches → lateral wall LV
  • Septal perforators → septum
  • Blocked in anterior MI → widest territory → most dangerous

LCx (Left Circumflex)

  • Runs in left atrioventricular groove
  • Supplies: lateral wall LV, LA, posterior wall LV (if left-dominant)
  • SA node artery in 35–45% of cases (when from LCx)
  • Obtuse marginal branches → lateral LV

Right Coronary Artery (RCA):

Arises from RIGHT aortic sinus → right atrioventricular groove:

  • SA nodal artery: In 55–65% — from RCA (proximal right) → SA node
  • Right marginal artery: Along inferior border of right ventricle
  • Acute marginal branch → anterior RV
  • Posterior Descending Artery (PDA): In RIGHT DOMINANT (85%) pattern → posterior IVS (posterior 1/3 of septum)
  • AV nodal artery: 90% from RCA (in right-dominant)
  • Supplies: RV free wall, posterior LV (right-dominant), posterior 1/3 septum

Coronary Dominance:

  • Right dominant (85%): RCA gives PDA
  • Left dominant (8%): LCx gives PDA
  • Co-dominant (7%): Both contribute to PDA
  • Dominance = whichever vessel supplies the AV node + PDA

Mitral Valve Complex & Chordae Tendineae PYQ 2024

The mitral (bicuspid) valve is more than just flaps of tissue — it is a complex of structures that function as a unit to ensure unidirectional blood flow during left ventricular systole.

The Mitral Valve Complex Components:

  • Fibrous Annulus: A fibrous ring surrounding the left AV orifice, anchoring the valve leaflets.
  • Valve Leaflets (Cusps): Two main cusps:
    • Anterior (Aortic) Leaflet: Larger, semicircular, smooth; situated between the inflow and outflow tracts of the left ventricle.
    • Posterior (Mural) Leaflet: Smaller, rectangular, has three scallops (P1, P2, P3).
  • Chordae Tendineae: Fibrous cords that attach the papillary muscles to the ventricular surfaces and free margins of the leaflets.
  • Papillary Muscles: Two large muscles (Anterolateral and Posteromedial) arising from the left ventricular wall.

Chordae Tiers & Clinical Significance:

  • Chordae Tendineae Tiers:
    • **Primary (Marginal) Chordae:** Attach to the free edge of the leaflets; prevent leaflet eversion.
    • **Secondary (Intermediate) Chordae:** Attach to the ventricular surface of the leaflets; distribute tension.
    • **Tertiary Chordae:** Found only at the base of the posterior leaflet, attaching to the ventricular wall.
  • Mechanism of Action: During ventricular systole, the papillary muscles contract first, pulling the chordae tendineae taut. This prevents the leaflets from everting/prolapsing back into the left atrium under the high ventricular pressure.
  • Papillary Rupture: The **posteromedial papillary muscle** has a single blood supply (from the PDA), whereas the anterolateral has dual supply (LAD + LCx). Hence, posteromedial papillary rupture is a common, life-threatening complication of myocardial infarction, causing acute mitral regurgitation.

DIAGRAM 09 — Pericardial Sinuses
Posterior wall of the pericardial sac showing sinuses — Gray's Anatomy
Pericardium — Layers & Sinuses PYQ 2014, 2019-S, 2022

Pericardial Layers:

  • Fibrous pericardium (outer): Strong, inelastic. Attached to: central tendon of diaphragm (below), great vessels (above). Prevents acute cardiac dilation. Supplied by phrenic nerve → referral to shoulder
  • Serous pericardium — parietal: Lines inner surface of fibrous pericardium
  • Serous pericardium — visceral (epicardium): Covers outer surface of heart. Reflected at great vessels
  • Pericardial cavity: Between parietal + visceral serous layers. Contains 20–50 mL of clear fluid (lubricant)

Pericardial Sinuses:

Transverse Sinus:

Between: Ascending aorta + Pulmonary trunk (ANTERIOR) vs SVC + Pulmonary veins (POSTERIOR). A finger passed through transverse sinus can compress BOTH aorta and pulmonary trunk simultaneously → cardiac surgery manoeuvre (Pringle-type for heart)

Oblique Sinus:

Blind J-shaped recess on posterior surface of LA. Bounded by: 4 pulmonary veins + IVC. Most dependent recess of pericardial cavity → fluid accumulates here first

Development of Transverse Pericardial Sinus: PYQ 2014, 2019 supple

Formed during embryological folding of the heart tube. Initially, the arterial and venous ends of the heart tube are anchored to the dorsal body wall by the dorsal mesocardium. As development proceeds, the central part of the dorsal mesocardium ruptures (degenerates), leaving a passage connecting the right and left sides of the pericardial cavity. This passage is the transverse pericardial sinus, which separates the outflow tract (arteries) from the inflow tract (veins).

Pericardial Effusion & Tamponade

Beck's triad of cardiac tamponade: (1) Hypotension, (2) Muffled/distant heart sounds, (3) Raised JVP (distended neck veins).

Pericardiocentesis: Needle inserted subxiphoid (subxiphisternal approach) at 45° angle toward LEFT shoulder. Passes through: skin → fat → linea alba → diaphragm → fibrous pericardium → parietal serous pericardium → pericardial cavity. ECG monitoring during procedure (ST elevation = needle touching myocardium).

Development of the Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) PYQ 2014, 2019 supple

The IVC is a complex venous channel formed during the 6th to 8th weeks of embryonic life. Rather than developing from a single vessel, it is built from the fusion of segments from four embryonic venous systems: the vitelline system and three pairs of cardinal veins (subcardinal, supracardinal, and sacrocardinal).

Four Embryological Segments of IVC

  • Hepatic Segment: Derived from the right vitelline vein (proximal part) and the hepatic sinusoids.
  • Prerenal Segment: Formed by the right subcardinal vein.
  • Renal Segment: Formed by the anastomosis between the subcardinal and supracardinal veins.
  • Postrenal (Infrarenal) Segment: Derived from the right supracardinal vein (lower portion). The left supracardinal vein degenerates.

Congenital Anomalies & Development Details

  • Double IVC: Caused by failure of the left supracardinal vein to regress below the kidneys, resulting in two infrarenal IVCs.
  • Absence of Hepatic Segment: Occurs when the right vitelline vein fails to connect with the subcardinal system. Blood from the lower body is diverted into the azygos/hemiazygos vein system to reach the SVC.
  • Left IVC: Occurs when the right supracardinal vein regresses and the left persists, leading to an IVC on the left side that crosses the midline at the renal level.

"Very Smart Sister Sues" → Vitelline (Hepatic) · Subcardinal (Prerenal) · Sub-Supra anastomosis (Renal) · Supracardinal (Postrenal).

Congenital Anomalies

Congenital Malformations PYQ Frequent

Cardiovascular Anomalies:

  • Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF): Most common cyanotic heart defect. Four features (PROVe): Pulmonary stenosis, Right ventricular hypertrophy, Overriding aorta, Ventricular septal defect. Due to anterior displacement of the aorticopulmonary septum.
  • Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA): Failure of ductus arteriosus (connecting pulmonary artery to aorta) to close after birth. Causes a continuous "machinery-like" murmur. Treated with indomethacin (prostaglandin inhibitor) to close it.

Thoracic & GI Anomalies:

  • Tracheo-oesophageal Fistula (TOF): Abnormal connection between trachea and oesophagus. Often associated with oesophageal atresia. Presents with choking, cyanosis, and regurgitation immediately upon first feed.
  • Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia: Failure of the pleuroperitoneal membrane to close (most commonly on the left — Foramen of Bochdalek). Abdominal organs herniate into the thorax, causing pulmonary hypoplasia.

Viva Q&A

Thoracic Wall & Pleura
What structures pass through the sternal angle? What is its vertebral level?PYQ Every Year

The sternal angle (angle of Louis) is the junction of manubrium and body of sternum, at the level of the T4/T5 intervertebral disc.

Structures at this level:

  • Trachea bifurcates → carina, right + left main bronchi
  • Arch of aorta begins (from ascending) and ends (becoming descending thoracic aorta)
  • Thoracic duct crosses from right to left
  • Azygos vein arches over right lung root to join SVC
  • Upper border of SVC
  • Ligamentum arteriosum (ductus arteriosus remnant)
  • Clinical use: 2nd costal cartilage articulates here — count rib spaces from sternal angle
Where is the safe site for thoracocentesis? Why over the upper border of the rib?PYQ 2019, 2023

Site: Posterior axillary line, 7th–8th ICS, over the upper border of the lower rib.

Why upper border: Intercostal neurovascular bundle (Vein-Artery-Nerve, from above) runs in the costal groove on the INFERIOR surface of the upper rib. Going over the upper border of the lower rib avoids this VAN bundle entirely.

Why posterior axillary line: Avoids serratus anterior (medially) and latissimus dorsi (posteriorly). The costodiaphragmatic recess is deepest here.

Clinical: In supine patient, effusion accumulates in paravertebral gutter posteriorly. In sitting, it goes to costodiaphragmatic recess.

Bronchopulmonary Segments & Lungs
Define a bronchopulmonary segment. Why is it surgically important?PYQ 2012, 2017, 2021

A bronchopulmonary segment is the smallest functionally independent unit of the lung supplied by its own: (1) segmental bronchus, (2) segmental artery, (3) lymphatics, (4) autonomic nerves.

Surgical importance:

  • Veins are INTERSEGMENTAL — they run in the connective tissue planes between adjacent segments, serving as surgical landmarks for segmentectomy
  • Segmentectomy: A diseased segment (localised tuberculosis, carcinoid tumour, sequestration) can be removed while preserving healthy lung tissue — this is only possible because each segment is an independent unit
  • Postural drainage: knowing which segment is affected allows correct patient positioning for gravity-assisted drainage
  • Foreign body location: knowing segment anatomy allows bronchoscopic retrieval
Why do inhaled foreign bodies lodge more commonly in the right lung? And in which segment?PYQ 2013-S, 2019

Why right more common: The right main bronchus is: shorter (2.5 cm vs 5 cm), wider, more vertical (25° vs 45° from tracheal axis) — essentially a near-continuation of the trachea. A foreign body in free fall follows the straighter path.

Which segment:

  • Erect patient: Posterior basal segment (S10) of right lower lobe — gravity pulls downward + posteriorly
  • Supine patient: Superior segment (S6) of right lower lobe — most dependent posterior segment when lying flat
  • Right-side-down lateral position: Lateral basal segment (S9) of right lower lobe
What is an eparterial bronchus? Where is it found?PYQ 2016-S

An eparterial bronchus is one that arises ABOVE (epi = above) the pulmonary artery.

Found only in the RIGHT lung — the right upper lobe bronchus arises from the right main bronchus before (above) the right pulmonary artery. The azygos vein arches over this segment.

In the LEFT lung, all lobar bronchi arise BELOW the left pulmonary artery (hyparterial bronchi) — the left pulmonary artery arches over the left main bronchus.

Clinical: The eparterial bronchus is why the right hilum arrangement differs — bronchus tops the right hilum, while artery tops the left hilum.

Heart, Coronary Arteries & Conducting System
Where exactly is the SA node? What is its blood supply?PYQ 2014, 2018, 2023

Location: The SA node is a crescent-shaped structure in the upper right atrium, at the junction of the SVC and RA, just to the right of the crista terminalis. Depth: subepicardial.

Blood supply:

  • SA nodal artery from RCA: 55–65% of population (proximal RCA gives the SA nodal artery)
  • SA nodal artery from LCx: 35–45% of population
  • The SA nodal artery passes through the centre of the node — compresses with increased atrial pressure (reflexly slows HR)

Clinical significance: In RCA occlusion causing inferior MI, SA node ischaemia → sick sinus syndrome → bradycardia/tachycardia syndrome.

Why does inferior myocardial infarction (MI) cause heart block?PYQ 2017, 2022

Inferior MI is caused by occlusion of the Right Coronary Artery (RCA).

RCA supplies in right-dominant patients (85%):

  • SA node (55–65% from RCA) → SA nodal dysfunction → sinus bradycardia, sinus pause
  • AV node (90% from RCA via AV nodal artery) → AV block (1°, 2° Mobitz I, or 3° AV block)

Inferior MI ECG: ST elevation in leads II, III, aVF (inferior leads face inferior surface of LV = diaphragmatic surface).

Management: Atropine (blocks vagal slowing), temporary pacemaker if haemodynamically unstable. Usually reversible with revascularisation as AV nodal ischaemia is transient.

Key point: This is the most common cause of complete AV block (3°) seen acutely in the CCU.

What is the Triangle of Koch? What structure lies at its apex?PYQ 2019-S, 2024

The Triangle of Koch is a triangular region on the floor of the right atrium, bounded by:

  • Anteriorly: Tricuspid valve ring (septal leaflet)
  • Posterosuperiorly: Tendon of Todaro (a fibrous ridge running from Eustachian valve to membranous septum)
  • Inferiorly/Base: Coronary sinus orifice (os)

The AV node lies at the apex of this triangle (where tendon of Todaro meets tricuspid valve ring).

Clinical importance:

  • Radiofrequency catheter ablation targets this region for AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT)
  • Surgical risk: incisions near this triangle during tricuspid valve repair risk AV node damage → complete heart block
  • Anatomical guide for electrophysiologists during EP studies
What is the 'artery of sudden death' and why is it called so?PYQ 2015, 2020

The Left Anterior Descending (LAD) artery is called the "artery of sudden death."

Reason: LAD supplies the LARGEST territory of any coronary artery:

  • Anterior wall of the LEFT ventricle (the main pumping chamber)
  • Anterior 2/3 of the interventricular septum
  • Apex of the heart
  • Right bundle branch and anterior fascicle of LBB → occlusion causes RBBB + LAFB

Acute LAD occlusion causes massive anterior MI — loss of the main pumping muscle → cardiogenic shock → ventricular fibrillation → sudden death.

On ECG: ST elevation in V1–V4 (anterior leads). Mortality rate without reperfusion is much higher than inferior MI.

Pericardium, Mediastinum & Diaphragm
Name the structures passing through the diaphragm at T8, T10, and T12.PYQ 2010, 2013, 2017, 2020, 2024

T8 (IVC hiatus) — in central tendon: IVC + right phrenic nerve

T10 (Oesophageal hiatus) — in right crus of diaphragm: Oesophagus + Left vagus (anterior) + Right vagus (posterior) + Oesophageal branches of left gastric artery and veins

T12 (Aortic hiatus) — behind diaphragm: Descending aorta + Thoracic duct + Azygos vein (sometimes hemiazygos)

Mnemonic: "I 8 (ate) 10 EGGs At 12" → T8=IVC, T10=Esophagus+Gastric+vagi, T12=Aorta

Why aorta is BEHIND diaphragm: The aortic "hiatus" is actually a space between the diaphragm and vertebral column — the aorta doesn't pierce the diaphragm, so diaphragm contractions do NOT compress aortic flow during inspiration.

What is the significance of the pericardial transverse sinus in cardiac surgery?PYQ 2021

Transverse sinus of the pericardium lies between:

  • Anterior: Ascending aorta + Pulmonary trunk
  • Posterior: SVC + Pulmonary veins

Surgical significance: By passing a finger through the transverse sinus, the surgeon can simultaneously compress BOTH the ascending aorta AND the pulmonary trunk — allowing rapid control of the outflow of both ventricles. This is used in cardiac surgery (analogous to Pringle's manoeuvre for heart) to arrest blood flow before placing clamps on cardiopulmonary bypass.

The oblique sinus (J-shaped, behind LA) is the most dependent part of the pericardial cavity → accumulates haemopericardium after trauma or surgery.

Clinical "Explain Why" Questions
Explain why the central tendon of the diaphragm is fused with the fibrous pericardium.PYQ 2014, 2018, 2020, 2025

This fusion has an **embryological basis**: both the central tendon of the diaphragm and the fibrous pericardium develop from the same embryological structure, the **septum transversum**. As the heart descends and the diaphragm forms, the fibrous pericardium remains anchored to the central tendon. Clinically, this means that during deep inspiration, the descent of the diaphragm pulls the pericardium and heart downwards, elongating the vena cava and facilitating venous return to the right atrium.

Explain why respiration in newborn infants is purely abdominal, not thoracic.PYQ 2012, 2023

In newborn infants, the **ribs lie horizontally** (rather than obliquely downwards as in adults). When the intercostal muscles contract, they cannot elevate the horizontal ribs further to increase the transverse or anteroposterior diameters of the chest. Therefore, respiration is almost entirely dependent on the descent of the diaphragm (abdominal respiration). As the child begins to walk, gravity pulls the ribs into an oblique position, establishing the thoracic component of respiration by ~2 years of age.

Explain why bronchopulmonary segments 2 (apical segment of lower lobe) and 6 (superior segment of lower lobe) are the most common sites for lung abscess.PYQ 2011, 2019 supple

This is due to **postural gravity drainage** in the supine position:

  • When a patient is lying flat on their back (supine) — e.g., during anesthesia, coma, or deep sleep — the bronchi of **segment 2 (posterior segment of upper lobe)** and **segment 6 (superior segment of lower lobe)** point directly downwards (posteriorly).
  • Aspirated material (oropharyngeal secretions, vomit, blood) naturally flows under gravity straight into these dependent bronchi, making them the primary sites for aspiration pneumonia and lung abscess.
Explain why foreign bodies are more commonly inhaled into the right bronchus than the left.PYQ Frequent

The right main bronchus is anatomically predisposed to receive aspirated foreign bodies because it is **wider, shorter, and runs more vertically** (at ~25° from the vertical trachea) compared to the left main bronchus, which is narrower, longer, and runs more obliquely (at ~45°) to clear the heart. Thus, the right main bronchus acts as a direct continuation of the trachea.

Explain why complete diaphragmatic paralysis is not achieved by simple phrenic nerve division in the neck.PYQ 2011 supple

This is because of the presence of an **accessory phrenic nerve** (present in ~20–30% of individuals). The accessory phrenic nerve arises from the 5th cervical nerve root (or nerve to subclavius), runs a different course in the neck lateral to the main phrenic nerve, and joins the main phrenic nerve in the thoracic inlet. Simple division of the main phrenic nerve leaves the accessory branch intact, allowing partial diaphragm function. To achieve complete hemidiaphragmatic paralysis, the accessory phrenic nerve must also be identified and divided.

Explain why a coronary artery occlusion has a better prognosis in an elderly patient than in a young adult.PYQ 2022, 2023

In elderly patients, gradual, long-term narrowing of the coronary arteries (atherosclerosis) allows time for **collateral anastomosis (collateral circulation)** to develop between adjacent coronary artery branches. If a major branch suddenly occludes, these pre-existing collaterals can immediately supply the ischemic myocardium, minimizing infarct size. In young adults, there are no pre-formed collaterals. A sudden coronary occlusion leads to immediate, massive myocardial infarction or fatal arrhythmia due to lack of alternative pathways.

Explain how a cervical rib can cause Thoracic Outlet Syndrome (TOS).PYQ 2024

A **cervical rib** is a supernumerary rib arising from the C7 vertebra. It extends forward to attach to the first rib or sternum. This osseous structure compresses the **lower trunk of the brachial plexus (C8, T1)** and the **subclavian artery** as they cross the first rib. This leads to Thoracic Outlet Syndrome, presenting with: (1) wasting and weakness of intrinsic hand muscles (T1 fibers, claw hand), (2) paresthesia along the medial forearm/hand, and (3) ischemic symptoms in the upper limb (coldness, weakened radial pulse) due to subclavian compression.

Explain why a stab wound at the root of the neck can cause a tension pneumothorax.PYQ 2024

The **cervical pleura (cupula)** and the **apex of the lung** project upwards into the root of the neck, extending about **2.5 cm above the medial 1/3 of the clavicle** (behind the sternocleidomastoid). A stab wound in this region easily pierces the suprapleural membrane (Sibson's fascia) and the cervical pleura, entering the pleural cavity. Air enters the pleural space during inspiration but cannot escape during expiration (valve-like mechanism), leading to a rapid accumulation of positive intrapleural pressure — a life-threatening **tension pneumothorax** and surgical emphysema.

EQ Templates & Quickfire

EQ — Describe the Bronchopulmonary Segments [PYQ 2012, 2017, 2021-S, 2024]
"Describe the bronchopulmonary segments with clinical importance."
  1. Definition: A bronchopulmonary segment is the smallest independently functioning unit of the lung, supplied by its own tertiary (segmental) bronchus, segmental artery, lymphatics, and autonomic nerves. Veins are intersegmental (run between segments — surgical importance).
  2. Number: Right lung = 10 segments. Left lung = 8 (classical) or 10 (some authorities) due to fusion of apical + posterior (S1+S2) and anteromedial basal (S7+S8).
  3. Right Lung Segments: Upper lobe (3): Apical, Posterior, Anterior. Middle lobe (2): Lateral, Medial. Lower lobe (5): Superior, Medial Basal, Anterior Basal, Lateral Basal, Posterior Basal.
  4. Left Lung Segments: Upper lobe (4+2 lingular): Apicoposterior, Anterior, Superior Lingular, Inferior Lingular. Lower lobe (4): Superior, Anteromedial Basal, Lateral Basal, Posterior Basal. No S7 alone; No middle lobe (lingula is functional equivalent).
  5. Clinical Importance: (a) Segmentectomy — each segment independently resectable (TB, carcinoid, sequestration). (b) Postural drainage — specific positioning drains specific segment. (c) Foreign body localisation — predicts which segment to bronchoscope. (d) CT reporting — radiological pulmonary anatomy uses segmental nomenclature.
  6. Key Anatomical Points: Segments are separated by intersegmental veins (surgical planes). Each segment is a complete, self-sustaining bronchopulmonary unit — disease in one does NOT necessarily spread to adjacent segment.
EQ — Coronary Arteries and Conducting System [PYQ 2015, 2018, 2023]
"Describe the coronary arteries and their relation to the conducting system."
  1. Origin: Both coronary arteries arise from the ascending aorta within the aortic sinuses (sinuses of Valsalva). LCA from left aortic sinus; RCA from right aortic sinus. They fill during DIASTOLE.
  2. Left Coronary Artery (LCA): Short left main → LAD (anterior IVS, anterior LV wall, apex, septal perforators) + LCx (lateral wall LV, LA, SA node in 35–45%). LAD = "artery of sudden death" — supplies largest territory. Occlusion → massive anterior MI.
  3. Right Coronary Artery (RCA): Right AV groove → SA nodal artery (55–65%), right marginal, → PDA in right-dominant (85%). Supplies: RV free wall, inferior LV, posterior IVS (PDA), SA node (usually), AV node (90%).
  4. Dominance: The vessel supplying the PDA (posterior descending artery) determines dominance. Right dominant = 85%, Left dominant = 8%, Codominant = 7%. Dominance determines AV nodal supply.
  5. Conducting System Supply: SA node — RCA (55–65%) or LCx (35–45%). AV node — RCA (90%) in right-dominant. Bundle of His — dual supply (septal branches LAD + AV nodal artery). Bundle branches — LAD mainly.
  6. Clinical Correlation: Inferior MI (RCA occlusion) → SA + AV nodal ischaemia → bradycardia + AV block. Anterior MI (LAD) → RBBB (LAD supplies RBB). Complete AV block needs temporary pacing. Knowing coronary anatomy predicts arrhythmia type post-MI.
EQ — Conducting System of Heart [PYQ 2014-S, 2019, 2022]
"Describe the conducting system of the heart with clinical significance."
  1. Components (in order of impulse): SA node → Internodal tracts → AV node → Bundle of His → Right + Left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres.
  2. SA Node: Pacemaker, upper right RA at SVC junction (crista terminalis). Rate 60–100 bpm. Blood supply: RCA (55–65%) or LCx (35–45%). Automaticity due to funny current (If). Only structure that initiates impulse normally.
  3. AV Node: Floor of RA in Triangle of Koch (bounded by tendon of Todaro, tricuspid valve ring, coronary sinus os). Delay of 0.1 sec allows atrial contraction to complete before ventricular systole. Rate 40–60 bpm. Blood supply: RCA (90% right-dominant). Target for catheter ablation in AVNRT.
  4. Bundle of His to Purkinje: Bundle of His pierces central fibrous body — ONLY electrical connection between atria and ventricles (fibrous ring otherwise insulates). RBB is thin and passes through MODERATOR BAND (RV) to anterior papillary muscle. LBB fans into anterior + posterior fascicles. Purkinje fibres fastest (4 m/s) and final delivery of impulse.
  5. Clinical Significance: SSS (sick sinus syndrome) → pacemaker. AV block (1°: prolonged PR; 2° Mobitz I: Wenckebach; 2° Mobitz II: sudden drop; 3° complete: no relationship P to QRS) → pacemaker if symptomatic. RBBB: S1Q3T3 pattern in PE, RBB vulnerable in anterior MI. WPW: accessory pathway (bundle of Kent) bypasses AV delay → pre-excitation, delta wave.
  6. Embryological note: Conducting tissue originates from working myocardium that becomes specialised. SA node forms from the sinus venosus. Congenital complete heart block can occur if anti-Ro/La antibodies (maternal SLE) cross placenta and damage AV node.
