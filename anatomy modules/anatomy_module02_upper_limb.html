Bones of the Upper Limb

Upper limb has 32 bones per side: clavicle (1) + scapula (1) + humerus (1) + radius + ulna (2) + carpals (8) + metacarpals (5) + phalanges (14).
Clavicle ⭐ PYQ
  • Only long bone that lies horizontally
  • Only bone in upper limb that ossifies by intramembranous ossification (exception: medial end ossifies by endochondral)
  • Medial 2/3: convex forward; Lateral 1/3: concave forward
  • No medullary canal (peculiarity)
  • First bone to ossify in utero (5th–6th week)
  • Last bone to complete ossification (medial end, 22–25 years)

Important Attachments:

  • Superior surface: Sternocleidomastoid (medial), Trapezius (lateral)
  • Inferior surface: Subclavius (groove) — protects subclavian vessels in fracture
  • Posterior border (lateral): Deltoid (anterior), Trapezius (posterior)
  • Coracoclavicular ligament attaches at conoid tubercle + trapezoid line
⚡ Fracture site: Junction of medial 2/3 and lateral 1/3 — narrowest point, weakest area. Medial fragment pulled up by SCM, lateral pulled down by weight of limb.
Scapula — Notable Features
  • Suprascapular notch: Suprascapular nerve passes through; artery passes above ligament ("Generals go through the gate; Generals' Lady goes over")
  • Spine of scapula: continuous with acromion; divides posterior surface into supra- and infraspinous fossae
  • Coracoid process: "Crow's beak" — attachment for Coracobrachialis, Short head of Biceps, Pectoralis minor
  • Glenoid cavity: faces anterolaterally; deepened by glenoid labrum
  • Lies between ribs 2–7 posteriorly

Radius & Ulna — Side Determination & Key Points

Radius:

  • Head at proximal end — small (for radius), disc-shaped
  • Radial tuberosity: biceps brachii insertion
  • Styloid process is 1 cm lower than ulnar styloid — reversed in Colles' fracture
  • Anterior surface: Pronator quadratus (distal quarter)
  • Interosseous membrane connects radius to ulna — fibres run obliquely downward medially (forces transmitted from hand to elbow via radius → ulna → humerus)

Ulna:

  • Head at distal end (remember: opposite to radius)
  • Olecranon: triceps insertion; forms point of elbow
  • Trochlear notch + coronoid process: articulates with humerus
  • Radial notch: proximal radioulnar joint
  • Ulnar styloid: less prominent, higher than radial styloid
Side determination trick: Hold bone vertically, articular head (disc) at top = radius; hold ulna — coronoid process + olecranon at top, concavity faces laterally.
Carpal Bones ⭐ PYQ
Mnemonic — 8 Carpals (Proximal then Distal row, lateral to medial)
"Some Lovers Try Positions That They Can't Handle"

Proximal row: Scaphoid · Lunate · Triquetrum · Pisiform
Distal row: Trapezium · Trapezoid · Capitate · Hamate

  • Scaphoid: Most commonly fractured carpal; waist fracture → avascular necrosis (proximal part has no direct blood supply). Tenderness in anatomical snuffbox.
  • Lunate: Most commonly dislocated carpal — dislocates anteriorly → median nerve compression
  • Pisiform: Sesamoid bone (within flexor carpi ulnaris tendon); only carpal that articulates with one bone only
  • Hamate: Hook of hamate in palm — Guyon's canal (ulnar nerve/artery)
  • Trapezium: Articulates with 1st metacarpal (saddle joint — only one in wrist area, allows thumb opposition)
  • Capitate: Largest carpal
  • Floor of anatomical snuffbox: scaphoid + trapezium (radial artery crosses)
  • Carpal tunnel: bounded by flexor retinaculum — contains 4 FDS, 4 FDP, 1 FPL tendons + median nerve (10 structures total)
Anatomical Snuff Box PYQ 2014, 2017 supple

A triangular depression on the posterolateral aspect of the wrist and metacarpal I, visible during extension of the thumb.

Boundaries & Floor

  • Lateral (Anterior) Boundary: Tendons of Abductor Pollicis Longus (APL) and Extensor Pollicis Brevis (EPB).
  • Medial (Posterior) Boundary: Tendon of Extensor Pollicis Longus (EPL).
  • Floor: Formed by the scaphoid and trapezium bones.
  • Roof: Skin, superficial fascia containing the cephalic vein and superficial branch of the radial nerve.

Contents & Clinical Significance

  • Radial Artery: Runs along the floor of the snuff box, passing deep to the tendons. Radial pulse can be felt here.
  • Cephalic Vein: Originates in the roof, crossing the roof from medial to lateral.
  • Superficial Radial Nerve: Crosses the roof to supply sensory innervation to the lateral 3½ digits on the dorsum of the hand.
  • Scaphoid Fracture: Tenderness elicited by deep pressure in the snuff box is a key diagnostic indicator of a scaphoid fracture.
Snuff Box Boundaries Mnemonic
"A Paul McCartney (APL) playing EPB (EPB) on the lateral side, and EPL (EPL) on the medial side" — lateral is APL+EPB (sandwich), medial is EPL.

Joints of the Upper Limb

Glenohumeral (Shoulder) Joint ⭐ PYQ

Type & Stability:

  • Ball-and-socket synovial joint; most mobile joint in body
  • Glenoid cavity = 1/3 head of humerus → inherently unstable
  • Stability maintained by: rotator cuff muscles (dynamic), glenohumeral ligaments + labrum (static), long head of biceps (dynamic)
  • Capsule: lax inferiorly → common dislocation direction = inferior/anterior
  • Subscapularis bursa communicates with joint → acts as weak spot anteriorly

Rotator Cuff — SITS:

Rotator Cuff Tears

Supraspinatus most commonly torn (impingement against coracoacromial arch). Painful arc sign: pain 60–120° abduction (arc of impingement). MRI gold standard. Empty can test for supraspinatus. Lift-off test for subscapularis.

Shoulder Dislocation

Anterior dislocation (95%) — arm in abduction + external rotation. Axillary nerve commonly injured → loss of sensation over "regimental badge" area (lateral arm) + deltoid weakness. Confirm with AP + axillary X-ray.

Elevation of the Arm & Scapulohumeral Rhythm ⭐ PYQ 2016, 2022, 2023

Active abduction and elevation of the arm from 0° to 180° is a coordinated process involving multiple muscle groups and joints, structured in three distinct phases:

PhaseRange of MotionPrime Movers (Muscles)Mechanism & Nerve Supply
Phase 10° to 15°SupraspinatusInitiates abduction. Suprascapular nerve (C5, C6). Deltoid cannot initiate because its line of pull is parallel to the humerus.
Phase 215° to 90°Deltoid (aided by Supraspinatus)Abduction up to horizontal. Axillary nerve (C5, C6). Requires stabilization of the humeral head by rotator cuff.
Phase 390° to 180°Serratus Anterior & TrapeziusScapular rotation. The scapula is rotated so that the glenoid cavity faces upwards. Serratus anterior (Long thoracic nerve, C5-C7) and Trapezius (Spinal accessory nerve, CN XI) act as a force couple.
Scapulohumeral Rhythm (2:1 Ratio)

During the full range of arm elevation, movement occurs at both the glenohumeral (shoulder) joint and the scapulothoracic joint in a 2:1 ratio. For every 3° of total arm elevation, 2° occurs at the glenohumeral joint and 1° occurs via scapular rotation (e.g., out of 180° total, 120° is glenohumeral and 60° is scapular rotation).

Elbow Joint PYQ

Structure:

  • Compound joint with one synovial cavity containing three articulations:
    1. Humero-ulnar (hinge — flexion/extension)
    2. Humero-radial (capitulo-radial — flexion/extension + rotation)
    3. Proximal radio-ulnar (pivot — supination/pronation)
  • Carrying angle: ~170° (valgus) in males, greater in females → needed to clear hips during arm swing
  • Cubitus valgus → tardy ulnar nerve palsy

Ligaments:

  • Medial (ulnar) collateral ligament: 3 bands (anterior strongest) — resists valgus stress. Attached to medial epicondyle → coronoid process + olecranon. Tested in throwing athletes.
  • Lateral (radial) collateral ligament: medial epicondyle → annular ligament
  • Annular ligament: holds radial head; Pulled elbow (nursemaid's elbow) in children — radial head subluxes through annular ligament when arm pulled
Wrist (Radiocarpal) Joint
  • Ellipsoid (condyloid) synovial joint
  • Between: distal radius + articular disc below ulna (proximal side) and scaphoid + lunate + triquetrum (distal side)
  • Ulna does NOT directly articulate with carpals — articular disc separates them
  • Movements: flexion, extension, abduction (radial deviation), adduction (ulnar deviation), circumduction
  • Colles' fracture: distal radius fracture — "dinner fork" deformity, radial styloid displaced proximally
1st Carpometacarpal Joint (Thumb)
  • Saddle (sellar) joint — only true saddle joint in upper limb
  • Between trapezium and base of 1st metacarpal
  • Bennett's fracture: intra-articular fracture-dislocation of 1st metacarpal base
Pronation & Supination Mechanism PYQ 2018

Pronation and supination are rotatory movements of the forearm where the radius rotates around the relatively fixed ulna.

Joints & Axis of Movement:

  • Joints involved: Superior (proximal) and inferior (distal) radioulnar joints (pivot joints), along with middle radioulnar joint (interosseous membrane).
  • Axis of rotation: An oblique axis passing from the center of the head of the radius proximally to the center of the head of the ulna distally.
  • Movement: The head of the radius rotates within the osseofibrous ring formed by the radial notch of the ulna and the annular ligament. Distally, the radius swings around the ulnar head, crossing it anteriorly in pronation.

Muscles Involved:

Brachial plexus = C5, C6, C7, C8, T1 ventral rami. Mnemonic for levels: RTTCB = "Randy Travis Drinks Cold Beer" (Roots → Trunks → Divisions → Cords → Branches).

Brachial Plexus Injuries — Erb's vs Klumpke's ⭐ PYQ Multiple
Erb's Palsy — Upper Trunk (C5, C6)
  • Cause: Excessive widening of angle between neck and shoulder — difficult delivery, fall on shoulder
  • Deformity: "Waiter's tip" — arm adducted, medially rotated, elbow extended, forearm pronated, wrist flexed
  • Muscles paralysed: Deltoid, Biceps, Brachialis, Brachioradialis, Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus
  • Loss: Abduction + lateral rotation of shoulder, flexion of elbow, supination
  • Sensation lost: C5/C6 dermatome (lateral arm, lateral forearm, thumb/index)
Klumpke's Palsy — Lower Trunk (C8, T1)
  • Cause: Excessive abduction — grabbing overhead to break a fall, birth (arm over head position)
  • Deformity: "Claw hand" — intrinsic hand muscles paralysed, hyperextension at MCPJs, flexion at IPJs
  • Muscles paralysed: All intrinsic hand muscles (interossei, lumbricals, thenar, hypothenar), finger flexors
  • Sensation lost: Medial forearm + hand (C8, T1)
  • Horner's syndrome if T1 ramus involved (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) — sympathetic fibres at T1

Nerve Injuries — Clinical Correlations

Nerve injuries are guaranteed PYQ territory. Know each nerve: level of injury → muscles paralysed → deformity → sensory loss → clinical tests.
Radial Nerve (C5–C8, T1) — Posterior Cord

High injury — in axilla (Saturday night palsy, crutch palsy):

  • All radial nerve muscles paralysed including Triceps
  • Wrist drop + finger drop + elbow extension weakness
  • Sensory: loss over posterior arm, forearm, hand (dorsum lateral 3½ fingers)

Mid-shaft humerus fracture (spiral groove):

  • Triceps spared (branch given above groove)
  • Wrist drop + finger drop
  • Sensory loss: dorsal lateral hand + thumb web space

Posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) at elbow:

  • Motor branch only (deep branch of radial) — no sensory loss
  • Finger + thumb drop; wrist extension partially preserved (ECRL still works — it's off before PIN)

Superficial radial nerve at wrist:

  • Sensory loss only (Wartenberg syndrome)
  • No motor deficit
Radial Nerve Mnemonic — "I Must Get Drunk By Friday Evening"
I=brachIalis(partial), M=Medial head triceps(1st branch), G=heads(Gravity), D=Deltoid(none), B=Brachioradialis, F=ECRL, E=ECRB — proximal to distal branches

Simpler: Triceps → Brachioradialis → Extensors carpi → Extensors digitorum/pollicis → Intrinsics (none)

Median Nerve (C5–C8, T1) — Lateral + Medial Cord

High injury — at elbow (supracondylar fracture, children):

  • Pronation weak (pronators both lost), wrist flexion weak (FCR lost)
  • FDS all lost, FDP (index + middle) lost, FPL lost
  • "Papal blessing" hand on attempted grip (index + middle can't flex)
  • Thenar wasting (LOAF — Lateral 2 Lumbricals, Opponens pollicis, Abductor pollicis brevis, Flexor pollicis brevis)
  • Sensory: lateral 3½ fingers (palmar surface) + terminal dorsum

Low injury — at wrist (Carpal Tunnel Syndrome):

  • LOAF muscles affected → Ape hand deformity (thumb in plane of palm — opposition lost)
  • Sensory: lateral 3½ fingers + palm (but thenar eminence sensation SPARED — palmar cutaneous branch exits before carpal tunnel)
  • Tinel's sign: tingling on percussion over carpal tunnel
  • Phalen's test: wrist flexion 60 sec → tingling
  • Common causes: pregnancy, hypothyroidism, RA, acromegaly, occupation
Anterior Interosseous Nerve (AIN) Syndrome

Branch of median at elbow (motor only). Paralyses FPL + FDP (index, middle) + Pronator quadratus. Patient cannot make "OK sign" — instead makes "pinch sign" with straight thumb and index finger joints. No sensory loss.

Ulnar Nerve (C7, C8, T1) — Medial Cord PYQ 2013
Anatomical Course & Relations

Origin: Continuation of the medial cord (C7, C8, T1).
Axilla & Arm: Descends medial to the axillary and brachial arteries. At mid-arm, it pierces the medial intermuscular septum to enter the posterior compartment.
Elbow: Passes behind the medial epicondyle (resting in the cubital tunnel) — this is the most common site of injury/entrapment.
Forearm: Enters the anterior compartment by passing between the two heads of Flexor Carpi Ulnaris (FCU). Descends deep to FCU, running medial to the ulnar artery.
Wrist & Hand: Enters the hand superficial to the flexor retinaculum by passing through Guyon's canal (between pisiform and hook of hamate). Terminates by dividing into superficial (sensory) and deep (motor) branches.

High injury — at elbow (medial epicondyle / cubital tunnel):

  • FCU paralysed (wrist deviates radially on flexion)
  • FDP (ring + little) lost
  • Claw hand (ring + little) — less pronounced than low injury
  • All interossei, medial 2 lumbricals, hypothenar muscles lost
  • Sensory: medial 1½ fingers + medial palm

Low injury — at wrist (Guyon's canal / hook of hamate):

  • FCU and FDP spared (they receive branches above wrist)
  • More pronounced claw hand (ring + little) — "ulnar paradox": low lesion = worse deformity
  • Froment's sign: compensatory thumb IP flexion (FPL/median) during paper grip — adductor pollicis lost
  • Card test: cannot abduct little finger
  • Egawa test: can't spread/close fingers (interossei lost)
  • Guttering between metacarpals (interosseous wasting)
Ulnar Paradox: A low ulnar nerve lesion produces MORE claw deformity than a high lesion because in a high lesion, FDP (ring + little) is also paralysed — so the fingers cannot flex into the clawed position. Low lesion = FDP intact = more flexion = more claw.
Axillary Nerve (C5, C6) — Posterior Cord PYQ 2012 supple
Anatomical Course & Relations

Origin: Branch of the posterior cord (C5, C6).
Axilla: Lies posterior to the axillary artery and anterior to the subscapularis muscle.
Quadrangular Space: Passes backwards through the quadrangular space along with the posterior circumflex humeral artery.
Surgical Neck: Winds around the surgical neck of the humerus, lying deep to the deltoid muscle.
Termination: Divides into an anterior branch (supplies deltoid) and a posterior branch (supplies teres minor and becomes the superior lateral cutaneous nerve of arm supplying the "regimental badge" area).

  • Injury: Surgical neck of humerus fracture, anterior shoulder dislocation
  • Muscles: Deltoid (abduction lost) + Teres minor (lateral rotation)
  • Deformity: Loss of shoulder contour (deltoid wasted), flat shoulder
  • Sensory: "Regimental badge" area — lateral arm (superior cutaneous nerve of arm)
  • Test: Ask patient to abduct arm against resistance. If weak with deltoid wasting + loss of "badge" sensation = axillary nerve palsy
Musculocutaneous Nerve (C5, C6, C7) — Lateral Cord
  • Injury: Rare — penetrating wounds, shoulder dislocations
  • Muscles: Coracobrachialis, Biceps brachii, Brachialis
  • Loss: Flexion of elbow (weakened — not completely lost; brachioradialis via radial still works), supination (weakened)
  • Sensory: Lateral cutaneous nerve of forearm (lateral forearm)

Lumbrical Muscles of the Hand PYQ 2024

The lumbricals are four small, worm-like (intrinsic) muscles of the hand that play a critical role in finger coordination.

Type & Attachments:

  • Type: 1st and 2nd are unipennate (lateral two); 3rd and 4th are bipennate (medial two).
  • Origin: Tendons of the Flexor Digitorum Profundus (FDP).
  • Insertion: Extensor expansions of digits 2 to 5 (on the radial/lateral side of each digit).

Nerve Supply & Action:

  • Dual Nerve Supply:
    - Lumbricals 1 & 2: **Median nerve** (C8, T1).
    - Lumbricals 3 & 4: Deep branch of **Ulnar nerve** (C8, T1).
  • Actions: **Flexion at the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints** and **extension at the interphalangeal (IP) joints** of the fingers. This is the **"writing position"** or "intrinsic plus" position.
  • Clinical: Paralysis of these muscles leads to "claw hand" (unopposed long extensors hyperextend MCPJ, unopposed long flexors flex IPJ).

Pectoral Region & Axilla

Mammary Gland (Breast) PYQ Frequent

Anatomy & Blood Supply:

  • Situation: Superficial fascia of pectoral region. Base extends from 2nd to 6th rib.
  • Structure: 15-20 lactiferous lobes drained by lactiferous ducts, suspended by Suspensory ligaments of Cooper.
  • Arterial Supply: Internal thoracic (mammary) artery (perforating branches), Lateral thoracic artery, and Thoracoacromial artery.
  • Venous Drainage: Axillary vein and internal thoracic vein. Internal thoracic veins communicate with vertebral venous plexus (route for bony metastases).

Lymphatic Drainage & Clinical:

  • Lymphatics (Crucial): 75% drains laterally into Axillary nodes (mostly pectoral group). 20% drains medially into parasternal (internal mammary) nodes.
  • Peau d'orange: Blockage of superficial lymphatics by cancer cells causes edema, while tethering by hair follicles creates an orange-peel dimpled appearance.
  • Retraction of nipple: Infiltration and fibrosis of the lactiferous ducts.
  • Malignancy: Upper outer quadrant (including axillary tail of Spence) is the most common and dangerous site for breast cancer.
Axilla = pyramidal space between arm and chest wall. Apex (narrow) = between clavicle, 1st rib, scapula. Base = skin + fascia of armpit.
Clavipectoral Fascia PYQ 2010, 2013 supple

A strong fascial sheet in the pectoral region, situated deep to the clavicular head of the pectoralis major, filling the gap between the clavicle and the pectoralis minor.

Attachments:

  • Superiorly: Splits to enclose the Subclavius muscle; attached to the clavicle.

Viva & Exam Q&A

Bones & Joints
What are the peculiarities of the clavicle?PYQ Perennial
  • Only long bone that lies horizontally
  • Ossifies by intramembranous ossification (first bone to ossify, 5th–6th week in utero)
  • No medullary cavity
  • No nutrient foramen (or rudimentary)
  • First bone to ossify but last epiphysis to fuse (medial end, 22–25 years) → used in forensic age estimation
  • Transmits forces from upper limb to axial skeleton
  • Subclavius attachment on inferior surface protects subclavian vessels in fracture

Examiner tip: If they say "no medullary canal" OR "first bone to ossify" + "last epiphysis to fuse" → definitely clavicle.

Describe the scaphoid bone and its clinical importance.PYQ

Position: Proximal row of carpals, most lateral (radial side). Bridge between proximal and distal carpal rows — articulates with radius, lunate, capitate, trapezium, trapezoid.

Blood supply: Enters distally via dorsal and superficial palmar branches of radial artery. Proximal pole has poor/retrograde blood supply. Waist fracture disrupts blood supply to proximal fragment → avascular necrosis of proximal pole.

Clinical features of scaphoid fracture:

  • Tender in anatomical snuffbox (floor = scaphoid + trapezium, roof = APL + EPB laterally, EPL medially)
  • Tender at scaphoid tubercle on palmar aspect
  • Often missed on initial X-ray — treat as fracture if snuffbox tenderness present
  • MRI or bone scan for definitive diagnosis
  • Complications: non-union, avascular necrosis, secondary osteoarthritis

Anatomical snuffbox also contains: Radial artery, cephalic vein origin, superficial branch of radial nerve.

Brachial Plexus & Nerves
Describe the formation and branches of the brachial plexus.PYQ Guaranteed

Formation: Ventral rami of C5, C6, C7, C8, T1. Lies in posterior triangle of neck between scalenus anterior and scalenus medius.

Five levels — RTTCB:

  • Roots (C5–T1): Long thoracic (C5,6,7) + Dorsal scapular (C5) + Nerve to subclavius (C5,6)
  • Trunks: Superior (C5+C6) + Middle (C7) + Inferior (C8+T1). Superior gives suprascapular nerve
  • Divisions (6): No branches
  • Cords (named for relation to axillary artery 2nd part): Lateral, Medial, Posterior
  • Branches: 5 terminal (musculocutaneous, median, ulnar, radial, axillary) + collateral branches

Posterior cord is largest — supplies entire posterior compartment of arm + forearm + deltoid + teres minor. All posterior cord branches = C5–T1.

A patient presents with wrist drop after using crutches. Which nerve is injured and at what level?PYQ Clinical

Nerve: Radial nerve, high injury in axilla (crutch palsy from pressure on axillary pad).

Clinical features:

  • Wrist drop (extensor carpi radialis lost) + finger drop
  • Elbow extension weakness (triceps branch given in axilla — also involved in crutch palsy)
  • Sensory loss: posterior arm, posterior forearm, dorsal lateral hand (first web space most reliable)

Differentiation:

  • Axilla (crutch): triceps also involved (elbow ext. weak)
  • Spiral groove (mid-shaft fracture): triceps spared, wrist + finger drop only
  • PIN at elbow: finger drop only, wrist partially extended (ECRL spared), no sensory loss

Saturday night palsy = same mechanism, falls asleep with arm over chair; spiral groove level → triceps spared.

Explain the "ulnar paradox" with reference to nerve injury levels.PYQ Favourite

Ulnar paradox: A lower (distal) ulnar nerve lesion at the wrist produces more prominent claw hand deformity than a high lesion at the elbow.

Mechanism:

  • Low lesion (at wrist): FCU and FDP (ring + little) are spared (they receive branches above the wrist). Interossei + medial 2 lumbricals are paralysed. MCPJs hyperextend (no lumbrical/interosseous to flex them), IPJs stay flexed (FDP is intact). Result: pronounced claw of ring + little.
  • High lesion (at elbow): FCU + FDP (ring + little) are also paralysed. Since FDP cannot flex the IPJs, the claw deformity is less pronounced — fingers cannot complete the claw. Result: milder claw despite more extensive injury.

Paradox: worse injury → less deformity. This is because deformity requires intact antagonist muscles.

What is carpal tunnel syndrome? Give its anatomy, causes, and clinical features.PYQ Multiple

Carpal tunnel: Rigid osseofibrous canal. Roof = flexor retinaculum (transverse carpal ligament). Floor + walls = carpal bones.

Contents (10 structures): FPL tendon (1) + FDS × 4 + FDP × 4 = 9 tendons + Median nerve = 10 total.

Note: FCR is in a separate septum of the flexor retinaculum. FCU + ulnar vessels pass through Guyon's canal (superficial to retinaculum).

Causes of increased pressure: Pregnancy (fluid retention), hypothyroidism (myxoedema), RA (synovial hypertrophy), acromegaly, obesity, diabetes, repetitive wrist use, Colles' fracture malunion.

Clinical features (median nerve compression):

  • Paraesthesia + pain in lateral 3½ fingers (thumb, index, middle, lateral ring)
  • Night-time pain, worse with wrist flexion activities
  • Thenar wasting (LOAF muscles) — late sign
  • Ape hand (thumb flattened in plane of palm)
  • Tinel's sign (percussion at wrist) + Phalen's test (wrist flexion 60 sec)
  • Thenar eminence sensation preserved — palmar cutaneous branch exits median nerve 5 cm proximal to flexor retinaculum

Management: Splinting, steroid injection, surgical decompression (carpal tunnel release).

Axilla, Rotator Cuff & Applied
Name the contents of the axilla.PYQ

Six contents of axilla (VABLAN + Fat):

  • Vessels: Axillary artery (and branches) + Axillary vein
  • Axillary lymph nodes (5 groups: anterior/pectoral, posterior/subscapular, lateral, central, apical)
  • Brachial plexus: cords and their branches
  • Long thoracic nerve (on medial wall, on serratus anterior)
  • Adipose tissue (fat — fills the space)
  • Nerve: Intercostobrachial nerve (T2 — sensory to medial arm)

Lymph nodes are surgically important: cancer of the breast drains to axillary nodes (primarily pectoral group). Apical nodes are the gateway to the thoracic duct/subclavian lymph trunk.

Enumerate the muscles of the rotator cuff and their actions.PYQ Every Year

SITS — 4 muscles:

  • Supraspinatus (C5,6 — suprascapular nerve): Initiates abduction 0–15°; most commonly torn. Superior facet of greater tubercle.
  • Infraspinatus (C5,6 — suprascapular nerve): Lateral rotation. Middle facet of greater tubercle.
  • Teres minor (C5,6 — axillary nerve): Lateral rotation + weak abduction. Inferior facet of greater tubercle.
  • Subscapularis (C5–7 — upper + lower subscapular nerves): Medial rotation (most powerful rotator cuff). Lesser tubercle.

Function as a unit: Stabilise glenohumeral joint by compressing humeral head into glenoid (like a concave socket tightener). The supraspinatus prevents downward subluxation when arm at side; deltoid handles active abduction above 15°.

Painful arc test: pain 60–120° of abduction = supraspinatus impingement under coracoacromial arch. Empty can test specifically tests supraspinatus integrity.

Clinical "Explain Why" Questions
Explain why infection of the pulp space of the little finger spreads to the palm, whereas that of the index finger remains localized.PYQ 2011 supple

This depends on the anatomy of the synovial sheaths of the flexor digitorum tendons:

  • Little finger: The digital synovial sheath surrounding the flexor tendons of the little finger is **continuous with the ulnar bursa** (the common synovial sheath in the palm). Therefore, infection of the little finger pulp space tracks proximally into the palm, threatening the ulnar bursa.
  • Index finger (and middle/ring fingers): The digital synovial sheaths of these fingers are closed, isolated sacs terminating at the level of the metacarpal heads. Hence, infection remains localized in the finger and does not spread directly into the palmar spaces.
Explain why the basilic vein is preferred over the cephalic vein in cardiac catheterization and PICC lines.PYQ 2018, 2020

The **basilic vein** is anatomically superior for cardiac catheterization for two main reasons:

  • Straight Course: The basilic vein ascends medially and runs in a direct, straight line to continue as the axillary vein. In contrast, the cephalic vein ascends laterally and makes a sharp, nearly right-angled bend in the deltopectoral groove to pierce the clavipectoral fascia and enter the axillary vein, making catheter advancement difficult.
  • Caliber & Wasting: The basilic vein is larger, thicker-walled, and less prone to spasm than the cephalic vein.
Explain why enlargement of the central group of axillary lymph nodes causes pain on the medial side of the upper arm.PYQ 2023

The **intercostobrachial nerve** (lateral cutaneous branch of T2) traverses the axillary cavity, passing directly through the **central group of axillary lymph nodes**. Enlargement of these nodes (due to breast cancer metastasis or infection) compresses the nerve, causing referred pain and paresthesia along its cutaneous territory — the medial aspect of the upper arm and axilla floor.

Explain why the first metacarpal is regarded as a modified phalanx.PYQ 2011 supple

The first metacarpal behaves like a phalanx in its **ossification pattern**:

  • Typical metacarpals (2nd to 5th) have their primary center in the shaft and their secondary center at their **distal end (head)**.
  • The first metacarpal (and all phalanges) has its primary center in the shaft and its secondary center at its **proximal end (base)**.
Explain why the upper end of the humerus is considered a compound epiphysis.PYQ 2010

The upper end of the humerus is a **compound epiphysis** because it is formed by the fusion of three separate secondary centers before uniting with the shaft:

  • Center for the **head of humerus** (appears at 1 year).
  • Center for the **greater tubercle** (appears at 3 years).
  • Center for the **lesser tubercle** (appears at 5 years).

These three centers fuse together at 6 years to form a single, composite bony cap (compound epiphysis), which eventually fuses with the humerus shaft at 20 years.

EQ — Brachial Plexus: Formation, Branches & Injuries
"Describe the formation, branches and applied anatomy of the brachial plexus."
  1. Formation: Ventral rami of C5–T1. Located in posterior triangle between scalenus anterior and medius. Passes through the cervicoaxillary canal to reach axilla. Five levels: Roots → Trunks → Divisions → Cords → Branches (RTTDB / "Randy Travis…").
  2. Trunks: Superior (C5+C6), Middle (C7), Inferior (C8+T1). Located at outer border of first rib / posterior triangle. Superior trunk gives: suprascapular nerve + nerve to subclavius.
  3. Cords: Named for relation to 2nd part of axillary artery. Lateral (anterior divisions upper + middle), Medial (anterior division inferior), Posterior (all three posterior divisions). Posterior cord is the largest.
  4. Terminal Branches (5 — from cords): Musculocutaneous (lateral cord), Median (both lateral + medial cords — two roots), Ulnar (medial cord), Radial + Axillary (posterior cord). Collateral branches: long thoracic (roots), suprascapular, subscapular, thoracodorsal, intercostobrachial.
  5. Erb's Palsy (C5,C6 — upper trunk): Excessive neck-shoulder angle separation. "Waiter's tip" — adducted, medially rotated arm, elbow extended, forearm pronated. Deltoid + biceps + brachialis + supraspinatus + infraspinatus paralysed. Treated: physiotherapy, surgical repair in persistent cases.
  6. Klumpke's Palsy (C8,T1 — lower trunk): Excessive abduction. Claw hand — intrinsic muscles paralysed. Horner's syndrome if T1 preganglionic sympathetics involved. Sensory: medial forearm + hand.
EQ — Carpal Tunnel Syndrome: Anatomy, Causes, Features, Management
"Write a short note on carpal tunnel syndrome."
  1. Definition & Anatomy: Compression of median nerve within the carpal tunnel — rigid osseofibrous canal at wrist. Roof = flexor retinaculum (transverse carpal ligament). Contains 9 flexor tendons (FPL, 4×FDS, 4×FDP) + median nerve.
  2. Nerve affected: Median nerve. Important: its palmar cutaneous branch leaves proximal to the tunnel → thenar eminence sensation SPARED. Recurrent motor branch exits at distal edge → supplies LOAF muscles (thenar).
  3. Causes: Anything increasing tunnel pressure — pregnancy (oedema), hypothyroidism, rheumatoid arthritis (synovial proliferation), acromegaly, diabetes, Colles' fracture malunion, obesity, repetitive flexion-extension.
  4. Clinical Features: Nocturnal paraesthesia + pain (lateral 3½ fingers), worsened by wrist flexion/typing. Later: thenar wasting, ape hand (opposition lost). Signs: Tinel's (percussion → tingling), Phalen's (forced wrist flexion 60 sec → symptoms).
  5. Diagnosis: Clinical + NCS/EMG (nerve conduction studies — gold standard; shows slowed conduction across wrist). Ultrasound shows median nerve enlargement proximal to tunnel.
  6. Management: Conservative: splinting at night (neutral position), corticosteroid injection. Surgical: carpal tunnel release (division of flexor retinaculum) — definitive for severe/refractory cases.
EQ — Nerve Injuries of Upper Limb: Radial, Median, Ulnar Comparison
"Compare the injuries of radial, median and ulnar nerves in the upper limb."
  1. Radial Nerve (C5–T1, posterior cord): High (axilla) = wrist drop + finger drop + elbow extension weakness + sensory loss posterior arm/forearm/hand. Mid-shaft humerus (spiral groove) = wrist drop + finger drop, triceps SPARED. PIN at elbow = finger drop only, no sensory loss. Treatment: splinting, nerve exploration if no recovery at 3 months.
  2. Median Nerve (C5–T1, lateral + medial cord): High (elbow) = papal blessing on grip, pronation weak, thenar wasting. Low (carpal tunnel) = ape hand (opposition lost), thenar wasting, sensory lateral 3½ fingers (thenar sensation spared). Froment's sign not applicable (this is ulnar). Key test: Tinel, Phalen, OK sign.
  3. Ulnar Nerve (C7–T1, medial cord): High (medial epicondyle) = mild claw hand ring+little, FCU + FDP medial lost. Low (wrist/Guyon's) = paradoxically more pronounced claw (FCU + FDP spared). Tests: Froment's (adductor pollicis lost), Card test, Egawa test. Guttering between metacarpals.
  4. Comparison Summary: Radial = extensor loss; Median = thenar/flexor loss; Ulnar = intrinsic hand muscle loss. Mixed hand functions require all three nerves working together. The "intrinsic plus" position (MCPJs flexed, IPJs extended) requires interossei (ulnar) + lumbricals (median + ulnar).
  5. Nerve Injury Classification (Seddon): Neurapraxia (demyelination, complete recovery) → Axonotmesis (axon disruption, guided recovery by Schwann tubes) → Neurotmesis (complete division, needs surgical repair).
